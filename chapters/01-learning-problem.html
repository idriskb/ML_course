Introduction & the learning problem

What is machine learning?

Finding patterns in data has been central to science from the start. In the late 16th century Tycho Brahe spent two decades recording very precise planetary positions. From these observations Johannes Kepler inferred his empirical laws of planetary motion, which later became the foundation of Newton's theory of gravitation. Collecting data, finding regularities and turning them into predictive rules is the same process that underlies modern machine learning. The difference is that the rules are now found by algorithms, and the data can be far too large and complex for a human to inspect.

Machine learning is the study of algorithms and models that discover and exploit patterns in data, in order to make predictions or decisions under uncertainty.

A second, more operational definition makes the ingredients of a learning problem explicit.

A computer program learns from experience $E$ with respect to a class of tasks $T$ and a performance measure $P$ if its performance at tasks in $T$, as measured by $P$, improves with experience $E$.

The task $T$ is to classify each incoming e-mail as spam or not spam. The experience $E$ is a collection of past e-mails that users have already labelled. The performance $P$ is, for instance, the proportion of new e-mails that are classified correctly. Nobody writes the rules by hand. The program infers which words, phrases or senders separate spam from legitimate mail.

may read like a tongue twister, but its value is that it names the four components found in every machine learning problem:

  1. Model (the program): the family of mathematical functions that can produce predictions.
  2. Data (the experience): the examples the model learns from.
  3. Objective (the task): what exactly we want to achieve, written as a quantity to optimise.
  4. Evaluation (the performance): a quantitative measure of success on new data.

Together, these give a working definition: machine learning develops mathematical models and algorithms that improve at a task, as measured by a well-defined criterion, by finding and exploiting patterns in data.

Artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning

These three terms are often used as synonyms, but they have different scopes ().

  • Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broadest term: systems that show behaviour we associate with intelligence, such as reasoning, planning, perception or language. Classical AI includes rule-based expert systems and search algorithms, many of which do not learn from data at all.
  • Machine learning (ML) is the part of AI in which behaviour is learned from data instead of being programmed as explicit rules. Linear and logistic regression, decision trees and kernel methods are all machine learning. Today it is the dominant way of building AI systems.
  • Deep learning (DL) is the part of ML that uses neural networks with many layers. These networks learn useful representations directly from raw inputs (pixels, audio, text) instead of relying on hand-crafted features.

Why machine learning matters

Early scientists worked with small, carefully collected datasets. Today data are produced at a scale where hand-made rules no longer work.

  • The internet. The web holds billions of pages, and search engines answer billions of queries every day. Social networks connect hundreds of millions of users through hundreds of billions of links.
  • Economics and finance. Markets execute millions of trades per second. Payment systems process billions of transactions per day, and online shops record billions of user interactions.

Such data are large, high-dimensional, heterogeneous and noisy. To use them we need models that can capture non-linear structure, cope with many interacting variables, and run at scale. Machine learning provides those models. Its reach shows in both research and the labour market: publications that mention machine learning have grown roughly exponentially over recent decades, and so has demand for people with these skills.

The main paradigms of machine learning

Learning problems are usually grouped by what information is available during training. sketches the four settings we study in this course.

Supervised learning

In supervised learning we observe input–output pairs $(\bx_i, y_i)$ and want a rule that predicts $y$ from $\bx$ and works well on new examples. The central questions are how to represent the rule, how to fit it to data, and how to check that it generalises.

  • Regression: the target $y$ is a real number. Our running example is predicting the sale price of a house from features such as its living area, its overall quality or the year it was built (the house prices dataset).
  • Classification: the target $y$ takes a finite number of values. Our running example is deciding whether a breast tumour is malignant or benign from measurements made on a biopsy image (the breast cancer dataset).

Unsupervised learning

In unsupervised learning there is no target, only inputs $\bx_i$. The goal is to find structure in the data.

  • Dimensionality reduction: high-dimensional data often lie close to a lower-dimensional subspace. Finding it helps with visualisation, compression and as a preprocessing step for other methods.
  • Clustering: find groups of similar observations, for example customer segments.

Other paradigms

We will not study these in detail, but the tools of this course are their foundations.

  • Semi-supervised learning uses many unlabelled examples to improve a model when labels are scarce.
  • Self-supervised learning creates its own prediction tasks from the data, for example predicting a hidden word from its context, in order to learn useful representations.
  • Generative AI learns to produce new, realistic samples (text, images) that resemble the training data.
  • Reinforcement learning studies agents that learn which actions to take by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards.

The learning problem, formally

We now make the four components precise. The same set-up will be reused in every chapter.

Data

Let $\mathcal X$ be the input space (usually $\mathcal X = \R^p$, with $p$ features) and $\mathcal Y$ the output space ($\R$ for regression, a finite set such as $\{0, 1\}$ for classification). We assume there is an unknown probability distribution $P$ on $\mathcal X \times \mathcal Y$ that generates the data, and that we observe $n$ independent draws from it:

$$\mathcal D = \{(\bx_1, y_1), \dots, (\bx_n, y_n)\}, \qquad (\bx_i, y_i) \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} P.$$

We want a predictor $f : \mathcal X \to \mathcal Y$ that performs well on new pairs $(\bx, y)$ drawn from the same distribution $P$, not only on the pairs in $\mathcal D$.

Hypothesis space

A hypothesis space (or model class) is a set of candidate predictors $\mathcal H \subseteq \{f : \mathcal X \to \mathcal Y\}$. It is usually parametrised by a vector $\theta \in \Theta \subseteq \R^k$: $$\mathcal H = \{ f_\theta : \theta \in \Theta \}.$$

The choice of $\mathcal H$ decides which patterns the model can represent. Straight lines can only capture simple trends. High-degree polynomials, trees or neural networks can express much more complicated relationships.

Loss and risk

A loss function $\ell : \mathcal Y \times \mathcal Y \to [0, \infty)$ measures the cost $\ell(\hat y, y)$ of predicting $\hat y$ when the true value is $y$. Standard examples are

  • the squared loss $\ell(\hat y, y) = (\hat y - y)^2$ and the absolute loss $\ell(\hat y, y) = |\hat y - y|$, for regression;
  • the 0–1 loss $\ell(\hat y, y) = \mathbf 1\{\hat y \neq y\}$, which is $1$ for a mistake and $0$ for a correct prediction, for classification.

The risk (or expected loss, or generalisation error) of a predictor $f$ is its average loss on a new pair drawn from $P$: $$R(f) = \E_{(\bx, y) \sim P}\big[\ell(f(\bx), y)\big] = \int \ell(f(\bx), y)\, dP(\bx, y).$$ The empirical risk (or training error) is its average loss on the data: $$\hat R_n(f) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(\bx_i), y_i).$$

With the 0–1 loss, $R(f)$ is the probability that $f$ makes a mistake and $\hat R_n(f)$ is the fraction of training points it misclassifies. With the squared loss, $\hat R_n(f)$ is the mean squared error (MSE) on the training set.

We would like to minimise $R(f)$, but we cannot even compute it because $P$ is unknown. What we can compute is $\hat R_n(f)$. The next result says that it is a sensible stand-in, at least for a predictor fixed in advance.

Let $f$ be a predictor chosen independently of the data $\mathcal D$. Then $$\E\big[\hat R_n(f)\big] = R(f), \qquad \Var\big(\hat R_n(f)\big) = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{n},$$ where $\sigma_f^2 = \Var\big(\ell(f(\bx), y)\big)$ for $(\bx, y) \sim P$. In particular, if $\sigma_f^2$ is finite, $\hat R_n(f) \to R(f)$ in probability as $n \to \infty$.

Proof

Write $L_i = \ell(f(\bx_i), y_i)$. Because $f$ does not depend on the data and the pairs are i.i.d., the $L_i$ are i.i.d. with mean $\E[L_i] = R(f)$ and variance $\sigma_f^2$. By linearity of expectation, $\E[\hat R_n(f)] = \frac1n \sum_i \E[L_i] = R(f)$. By independence, $\Var(\hat R_n(f)) = \frac1{n^2} \sum_i \Var(L_i) = \sigma_f^2 / n$. Chebyshev's inequality gives, for every $\varepsilon > 0$, $\P\big(|\hat R_n(f) - R(f)| \geq \varepsilon\big) \leq \sigma_f^2 / (n \varepsilon^2) \to 0.$

Empirical risk minimisation

Once a hypothesis space and a loss are fixed, learning becomes an optimisation problem.

The empirical risk minimiser over $\mathcal H = \{f_\theta\}$ is $$\hat\theta \in \argmin_{\theta \in \Theta} \hat R_n(f_\theta) = \argmin_{\theta \in \Theta}\ \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \ell\big(f_\theta(\bx_i), y_i\big), \qquad \hat f = f_{\hat\theta}.$$

This problem is solved in closed form when possible (linear regression, Chapter 2) and numerically otherwise, typically by gradient descent.

The training error is optimistic

needs $f$ to be chosen before seeing the data. The fitted predictor $\hat f$ is chosen precisely because it makes $\hat R_n$ small on these particular points, so in general $\E[\hat R_n(\hat f)] \leq \E[R(\hat f)]$: the training error underestimates the true error. To estimate $R(\hat f)$ honestly we need data that were not used for fitting.

The learning problem in action: polynomial regression

We now go through the four components on a small regression problem where we know the truth, so that we can see exactly what goes wrong. The inputs are $x \in [0, 1]$ and the outputs are generated as $$y = \sin(2\pi x) + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.25^2),$$ so the data follow a smooth curve plus random noise. We observe only $n = 15$ such points.

Model: polynomials of degree $d$

For each degree $d$ consider the hypothesis space $$\mathcal H_d = \big\{ f_\theta(x) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \dots + \theta_d x^d \big\} = \big\{ f_\theta(x) = \theta\T \phi_d(x) \big\}, \qquad \phi_d(x) = (1, x, x^2, \dots, x^d)\T.$$ The model is non-linear in $x$ but linear in the parameters $\theta \in \R^{d+1}$. Once we replace $x$ by the feature vector $\phi_d(x)$, polynomial regression is just linear regression, and the minimiser of the squared loss has a closed form (Chapter 2).

Objective: mean squared error

We fit $\theta$ by minimising the training MSE, $$\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{train}}(\theta; d) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - \theta\T \phi_d(x_i)\big)^2.$$

Evaluation: training and test error

To measure generalisation we keep a test set: data drawn from the same distribution but never used for fitting. For each degree $d$ we

  1. fit $\hat\theta_d$ by minimising the training MSE;
  2. report the training error $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{train}}(\hat\theta_d; d)$ and the test error $\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{test}}(\hat\theta_d; d)$.

By the same argument as in , applied to the test points, the test error is an unbiased estimate of the risk $R(\hat f)$, because $\hat f$ was chosen without looking at them. In practice we only have one dataset, so we split it at random, typically into 80% for training and 20% for testing.

Two facts that you can see in are in fact theorems.

If $\mathcal H \subseteq \mathcal H'$, then $\min_{f \in \mathcal H'} \hat R_n(f) \leq \min_{f \in \mathcal H} \hat R_n(f)$ (assuming both minima exist). In particular, since $\mathcal H_0 \subseteq \mathcal H_1 \subseteq \mathcal H_2 \subseteq \cdots$, the training error of the fitted polynomial is non-increasing in the degree $d$.

Proof

Let $\hat f$ minimise $\hat R_n$ over $\mathcal H$. Since $\hat f \in \mathcal H'$ too, $\min_{f \in \mathcal H'} \hat R_n(f) \leq \hat R_n(\hat f) = \min_{f \in \mathcal H} \hat R_n(f)$. A polynomial of degree at most $d$ is also one of degree at most $d+1$ (take $\theta_{d+1} = 0$), so $\mathcal H_d \subseteq \mathcal H_{d+1}$.

Let $x_1, \dots, x_n$ be distinct. For any targets $y_1, \dots, y_n$ there is a polynomial of degree $d = n - 1$ that passes through all the points, so its training MSE is exactly $0$.

Proof

The conditions $f_\theta(x_i) = y_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$ form the linear system $V\theta = y$ with the square Vandermonde matrix $V_{ij} = x_i^{\,j-1}$, $1 \leq i, j \leq n$. Its determinant is $\det V = \prod_{1 \leq i \lt j \leq n} (x_j - x_i)$, which is non-zero because the $x_i$ are distinct. Hence $V$ is invertible and $\theta = V^{-1} y$ interpolates the data.

So with $n = 15$ points, the degree-14 polynomial fits the training data perfectly, noise included. That is clearly not what we want: the noise is, by definition, the part we cannot predict.

Capacity, overfitting and generalisation

The example illustrates three ideas that run through the whole course.

The capacity of a hypothesis space is, informally, how complex the functions it contains can be. A model underfits when its capacity is too low to capture the signal, so both training and test errors are high. It overfits when it fits the training data, noise included, much better than new data: the training error is low but the test error is high. A model generalises well when its test error is close to its training error and both are low.

  • Capacity. As $d$ grows, $\mathcal H_d$ becomes richer. Degree 1 only gives straight lines; higher degrees can bend and oscillate.
  • Overfitting. By the training error always decreases with $d$, and by it reaches $0$. The wiggles of a high-degree fit are not real structure, only the noise of this particular sample. Draw a new sample in and they change completely.
  • Generalisation. The test error is U-shaped. It decreases while extra capacity helps to capture the signal, reaches a minimum at a moderate degree, then increases again as the model starts fitting noise.

Intuition

A simple model makes the same systematic mistake on every sample (it is biased). A very flexible model has no systematic mistake, but its fit changes a lot from one sample to the next (it has high variance). The best model balances the two. Chapter 3 turns this into the bias–variance decomposition.

Do not choose the model with the test set

If you try many degrees and keep the one with the smallest test error, the test set has been used to make a choice, and its error is no longer an honest estimate of the risk. Model choices should be made on a separate validation set or by cross-validation, and the test set used once at the end. Both are introduced in Chapter 3.

Every machine learning problem in this course has the same structure: choose a hypothesis space (capacity), fit it to training data (optimisation), and evaluate it on unseen data (generalisation). The following chapters add the tools to do this well: regularisation to control capacity without changing the model class, cross-validation to choose hyperparameters, and richer model classes for non-linear problems.

Key takeaways

  • A learning problem has four parts: a model (hypothesis space), data, an objective (loss), and an evaluation on unseen data.
  • Supervised learning predicts a label (regression: real number; classification: category). Unsupervised learning finds structure without labels (clustering, dimensionality reduction).
  • We want a small risk $R(f)$, but we can only compute the empirical risk $\hat R_n(f)$. Empirical risk minimisation fits the model by minimising $\hat R_n$.
  • The training error always decreases as capacity grows and can reach zero. The test error is U-shaped. Overfitting means a low training error but a high test error.
  • The test set must never be used to fit or to choose the model.

Probabilistic view: the best possible predictor

This section looks at the learning problem through the data-generating distribution $P$. It explains what the ideal predictor is and why even it makes errors.

Suppose for a moment that we knew $P$. Which predictor would minimise the risk over all functions? The answer depends on the loss.

For the squared loss, the risk is minimised by the conditional mean $$f^*(\bx) = \E[\,y \mid \bx\,].$$ Moreover, for every predictor $f$, $$R(f) = \underbrace{\E\big[(y - f^*(\bx))^2\big]}_{R(f^*)\ \text{(irreducible error)}} + \E\big[(f^*(\bx) - f(\bx))^2\big].$$

Proof

Add and subtract $f^*(\bx)$: $$(y - f(\bx))^2 = (y - f^*(\bx))^2 + 2\,(y - f^*(\bx))(f^*(\bx) - f(\bx)) + (f^*(\bx) - f(\bx))^2.$$ Take expectations. For the middle term, condition on $\bx$: the factor $f^*(\bx) - f(\bx)$ is fixed given $\bx$, and $\E[y - f^*(\bx) \mid \bx] = \E[y \mid \bx] - f^*(\bx) = 0$. By the tower property the middle term has expectation $0$, which gives the decomposition. The second term is non-negative and is $0$ for $f = f^*$, so $f^*$ minimises $R$.

If the data are generated as $y = g(\bx) + \varepsilon$ with $\E[\varepsilon \mid \bx] = 0$ and $\Var(\varepsilon \mid \bx) = \sigma^2$, then $f^* = g$ and $R(f^*) = \sigma^2$. In , $\sigma^2 = 0.0625$: no predictor can have a test MSE much below this value, however clever it is. A test error far below $\sigma^2$ would be a sign of a bug.

For the 0–1 loss with $\mathcal Y = \{1, \dots, K\}$, the risk is minimised by $$f^*(\bx) = \argmax_{k} \ \P(y = k \mid \bx),$$ and its risk is $R(f^*) = 1 - \E\big[\max_k \P(y = k \mid \bx)\big]$.

Proof

Conditioning on $\bx$, the risk of any classifier $f$ is $R(f) = \E\big[\P(y \neq f(\bx) \mid \bx)\big] = \E\big[1 - \P(y = f(\bx) \mid \bx)\big]$. For each $\bx$, the quantity inside is smallest when $f(\bx)$ is the class with the largest conditional probability. Choosing it for every $\bx$ minimises the integrand pointwise, hence the expectation.

Both results say the same thing: learning is estimating the conditional distribution of $y$ given $\bx$, or at least its mean or its mode. Later chapters develop this view. Least squares turns out to be maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise (Chapter 2, extra material), and logistic regression is a direct model of $\P(y = 1 \mid \bx)$ (Chapter 4).

Show that for the absolute loss $\ell(\hat y, y) = |\hat y - y|$ the risk is minimised by any conditional median of $y$ given $\bx$.
