Model performance & regularization

How do we know a model will work on new data, and how do we stop it from fitting noise? This chapter splits the data honestly, decomposes the test error into bias and variance, controls capacity with ridge and lasso penalties, and chooses the amount of regularisation by cross-validation.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the bias–variance decomposition of the expected test error and use it to diagnose underfitting and overfitting.

Training, validation and test performance

In Chapter 1 we saw that the training error is an optimistic estimate of the true error: the model was chosen to make it small. Measuring performance honestly requires data the model has not seen. Most models also have settings that are not learned by minimising the training loss: the degree of a polynomial, the strength $\lambda$ of a penalty, the number of neighbours $k$. These are hyperparameters, and choosing them is a second, separate use of data.

The available data are split at random into three disjoint parts:

  • the training set, used to fit the parameters $\theta$ for a given value of the hyperparameters;
  • the validation set, used to compare hyperparameter values and choose one;
  • the test set, used once, at the very end, to estimate the error of the final model.

The errors measured on these sets are the training, validation and test errors. The difference between test and training error is the generalisation gap.

Typical proportions are 60/20/20 or 70/15/15. Once the hyperparameters are chosen, the model is usually refitted on training plus validation data before the final test evaluation.

Data leakage

Any information from the validation or test data that reaches the fitting step makes the evaluation optimistic. A classic mistake is to standardise features with the mean and standard deviation of the whole dataset before splitting. Compute these statistics on the training set only and apply them unchanged to the other sets. In scikit-learn, a Pipeline does this automatically.

The bias–variance trade-off

Why is the test error U-shaped in model complexity? To answer, think of the training set $\mathcal D$ as random. Each draw of $\mathcal D$ gives a different fitted model $\hat f = \hat f_{\mathcal D}$, so $\hat f(\bx)$ is a random variable. We study its average error.

Set-up. The data follow $y = f^*(\bx) + \varepsilon$, with $\E[\varepsilon \mid \bx] = 0$ and $\Var(\varepsilon \mid \bx) = \sigma^2$. Fix a test input $\bx$ and let $y = f^*(\bx) + \varepsilon$ be its (random) label, independent of the training set. Write $$\bar f(\bx) = \E_{\mathcal D}\big[\hat f(\bx)\big]$$ for the prediction at $\bx$ averaged over all possible training sets.

The expected squared error at $\bx$, averaged over training sets and over the noise, is $$\E_{\mathcal D, \varepsilon}\big[(y - \hat f(\bx))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\bar f(\bx) - f^*(\bx)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f(\bx) - \bar f(\bx))^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.$$

Proof

The input $\bx$ is fixed throughout, so we drop it from the notation and write $\hat f = \hat f(\bx)$, $\bar f = \bar f(\bx)$ and $f^* = f^*(\bx)$. Only two things are random: the training set $\mathcal D$, which determines $\hat f$, and the test noise $\varepsilon$. They are independent.

Step 1: separate the noise. Since $y = f^* + \varepsilon$, $$y - \hat f = \varepsilon + (f^* - \hat f).$$ Square and take expectations: $$\E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] = \underbrace{\E[\varepsilon^2]}_{=\ \sigma^2} + 2\,\underbrace{\E\big[\varepsilon\,(f^* - \hat f)\big]}_{=\ 0} + \E\big[(\hat f - f^*)^2\big].$$ The middle term vanishes because $\varepsilon$ is independent of $\hat f$ and has mean zero: $\E[\varepsilon\,(f^* - \hat f)] = \E[\varepsilon]\cdot\E[f^* - \hat f] = 0$. Hence $$\E\big[(y - \hat f)^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f - f^*)^2\big]. \tag{1}$$

Step 2: split the estimation error. Insert the average prediction $\bar f$: $$\hat f - f^* = (\hat f - \bar f) + (\bar f - f^*).$$ The second bracket is a fixed number: it does not depend on $\mathcal D$. Square and take $\E_{\mathcal D}$: $$\E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f - f^*)^2\big] = \E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f - \bar f)^2\big] + 2\,(\bar f - f^*)\,\underbrace{\E_{\mathcal D}\big[\hat f - \bar f\big]}_{=\ 0} + (\bar f - f^*)^2.$$ The middle term vanishes because $\E_{\mathcal D}[\hat f] = \bar f$ by definition. Hence $$\E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f - f^*)^2\big] = \underbrace{(\bar f - f^*)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f - \bar f)^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}. \tag{2}$$

Step 3: combine. Substituting (2) into (1) gives the decomposition.

Averaging over a random test input $\bx$ gives the same decomposition for the expected test MSE: $\text{test MSE} = \E_{\bx}[\text{bias}^2] + \E_{\bx}[\text{variance}] + \sigma^2$.

  • Bias is the systematic error: how far the average model is from the truth. High bias means the model class is too simple (underfitting).
  • Variance measures how much the fitted model changes from one training set to another. High variance means the model is too sensitive to the particular sample (overfitting).
  • Noise $\sigma^2$ is the irreducible error. No model can remove it.

Increasing capacity typically lowers the bias and raises the variance, so their sum is minimised at an intermediate complexity. The same pattern holds for every family in this course: the number of neighbours in k-NN (Chapter 7), the depth of a tree (Chapter 8), the width of a kernel (Chapter 9). The rest of this chapter shows how to control capacity continuously with a penalty, and how to choose its strength.

Regularisation

Choosing the polynomial degree controls capacity in discrete jumps. Regularisation gives a continuous dial: keep a flexible model, but add to the training loss a penalty that discourages complex parameter values, $$\min_\theta\ \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \bx_i\T\theta\big)^2}_{\text{fit}} \ +\ \lambda\, \underbrace{\Omega(\theta)}_{\text{complexity}}, \qquad \lambda \geq 0.$$ The hyperparameter $\lambda$ sets the exchange rate between fitting the data and keeping $\theta$ simple. At $\lambda = 0$ we recover least squares; as $\lambda \to \infty$ the penalty wins and $\theta \to 0$.

Regularisation is especially useful when least squares is unstable: many features, features that are highly correlated, or more features than observations ($p > n$), where $\bX\T\bX$ is singular (Chapter 2).

Two conventions to respect

Do not penalise the intercept. Shifting all prices by a constant should shift the intercept, not be fought by the penalty. Standardise the features first. The penalty treats all coefficients alike, but a coefficient's size depends on the units of its feature (area in m² or in cm² changes $\theta_j$ by a factor $10^4$). In what follows we assume the features are standardised and $\by$ is centred, so the intercept is $\bar y$ and we can drop it from $\theta$.

Ridge regression (L2)

Ridge regression penalises the squared Euclidean norm of the coefficients: $$\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}} = \argmin_{\theta \in \R^p}\ \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \lambda\norm{\theta}_2^2, \qquad \norm{\theta}_2^2 = \sum_{j=1}^p \theta_j^2.$$

For every $\lambda > 0$ the ridge problem has a unique solution, $$\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}} = \big(\bX\T\bX + n\lambda I\big)^{-1}\bX\T\by,$$ even when $\bX$ does not have full column rank (for example when $p > n$).

Proof

Let $J(\theta) = \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \lambda\norm{\theta}^2$ and write $A = \bX\T\bX + n\lambda I$.

Step 1: gradient and Hessian. The first term was differentiated in Chapter 2, and $\nabla\big(\lambda\norm{\theta}^2\big) = 2\lambda\theta$. So $$\nabla J(\theta) = -\frac2n\bX\T(\by - \bX\theta) + 2\lambda\theta = \frac2n\big(A\theta - \bX\T\by\big), \qquad \nabla^2 J(\theta) = \frac2n A.$$

Step 2: $A$ is positive definite. For every $\mathbf v \neq 0$, $$\mathbf v\T A\,\mathbf v = \underbrace{\norm{\bX\mathbf v}^2}_{\geq\ 0} + \underbrace{n\lambda\norm{\mathbf v}^2}_{>\ 0} > 0.$$ This holds whatever the rank of $\bX$, because $\lambda > 0$. Consequently $A$ is invertible, and $J$ is strictly convex (its Hessian is positive definite).

Step 3: conclude. A strictly convex differentiable function has at most one stationary point, and that point is its global minimiser. Setting the gradient to zero, $$\nabla J(\theta) = 0 \iff A\theta = \bX\T\by \iff \theta = A^{-1}\bX\T\by = \big(\bX\T\bX + n\lambda I\big)^{-1}\bX\T\by.$$

Adding $n\lambda$ to the diagonal is what gives ridge its name: it adds a "ridge" to $\bX\T\bX$ that lifts its small eigenvalues away from zero. The directions in which the data carry little information, which are the ones that make least squares unstable, are exactly the ones that ridge shrinks most.

Lasso regression (L1)

The lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) penalises the L1 norm: $$\hat\theta_{\text{lasso}} = \argmin_{\theta \in \R^p}\ \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \lambda\norm{\theta}_1, \qquad \norm{\theta}_1 = \sum_{j=1}^p |\theta_j|.$$

The objective is convex but not differentiable where some $\theta_j = 0$, and there is no closed-form solution in general. It is solved numerically, typically by coordinate descent, which updates one coefficient at a time (this is what scikit-learn does). The decisive property of the lasso is that it sets some coefficients exactly to zero, so it performs feature selection. The cleanest way to see the difference from ridge is a special case in which both can be solved by hand.

Suppose the features are orthogonal with $\bX\T\bX = nI$, and let $\hat\theta = \frac1n\bX\T\by$ be the least-squares solution. Then, coordinate by coordinate, $$\hat\theta_{\text{ridge}, j} = \frac{\hat\theta_j}{1 + \lambda}, \qquad \hat\theta_{\text{lasso}, j} = \operatorname{sign}(\hat\theta_j)\,\Big(|\hat\theta_j| - \frac\lambda2\Big)_+,$$ where $(u)_+ = \max(u, 0)$. Ridge shrinks every coefficient by the same factor and never reaches zero. The lasso subtracts a constant and sets every coefficient with $|\hat\theta_j| \leq \lambda/2$ exactly to zero (soft thresholding).

Proof

Step 1: the problem separates. Expand the loss using $\bX\T\bX = nI$ and $\bX\T\by = n\hat\theta$: $$\begin{aligned} \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 &= \frac1n\norm{\by}^2 - \frac2n\,\theta\T\bX\T\by + \frac1n\,\theta\T\bX\T\bX\theta \\ &= \frac1n\norm{\by}^2 - 2\,\theta\T\hat\theta + \norm{\theta}^2 \\ &= \norm{\theta - \hat\theta}^2 + \text{const}. \end{aligned}$$ Both $\norm{\theta - \hat\theta}^2$ and the penalties are sums over coordinates, so each coordinate can be minimised on its own. Fix $j$ and write $a = \hat\theta_j$.

Step 2: ridge. Minimise $r(t) = (t - a)^2 + \lambda t^2$: $$r'(t) = 2(t - a) + 2\lambda t = 0 \iff t = \frac{a}{1 + \lambda}.$$

Step 3: lasso. Minimise $g(t) = (t - a)^2 + \lambda|t|$. Assume $a \geq 0$ (the case $a \lt 0$ is symmetric). A negative $t$ is never optimal: $-t$ has the same penalty and is closer to $a$. On $t > 0$, $$g'(t) = 2(t - a) + \lambda = 0 \iff t = a - \frac\lambda2 .$$

  • If $a > \lambda/2$, this point is positive and it is the minimiser.
  • If $a \leq \lambda/2$, then $g'(t) > 0$ for all $t > 0$, so $g$ is increasing and the minimum is at $t = 0$.

Both cases are summarised by $t = (a - \lambda/2)_+$, which with the sign restored is the stated formula.

Elastic net

Ridge handles groups of correlated features gracefully by spreading the weight among them. The lasso selects features, but among highly correlated ones it tends to pick one arbitrarily. The elastic net combines the two penalties: $$\hat\theta_{\text{EN}} = \argmin_\theta\ \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \lambda\Big(\alpha\norm{\theta}_1 + (1 - \alpha)\norm{\theta}_2^2\Big), \qquad \alpha \in [0, 1].$$ $\alpha = 0$ gives ridge, $\alpha = 1$ the lasso, and intermediate values give sparse solutions that keep correlated features together.

The geometry of L1 and L2

The penalised problem has an equivalent constrained form: for every $\lambda$ there is a budget $t$ such that the same $\hat\theta$ solves $$\min_\theta\ \frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Omega(\theta) \leq t,$$ with a smaller $t$ corresponding to a larger $\lambda$. (This follows from Lagrange duality for convex problems.) In two dimensions we can draw it. The loss has elliptical contours centred at the least-squares solution. The constraint region is a disc for ridge and a diamond for the lasso. The solution is the point where the smallest ellipse touches the region.

The diamond has corners on the axes, and a corner sticks out towards the ellipses. For a wide range of positions of the least-squares solution, the first contact happens at a corner, where one coordinate is exactly zero. The disc has no corners. Its boundary is smooth, and the contact point generically has both coordinates non-zero. In high dimensions the L1 ball has many corners and edges, which is why the lasso returns sparse solutions so often.

Regularisation and the bias–variance trade-off

Regularisation deliberately introduces bias: coefficients are pulled towards zero. In exchange it reduces variance. The surprising fact is that the exchange is always favourable at first.

Let $\hat\theta$ be an unbiased estimator of a number $\theta \neq 0$ with variance $v > 0$, and consider the shrunk estimator $\hat\theta_\lambda = \hat\theta/(1 + \lambda)$, as in ridge (). Its mean squared error is $$\E\big[(\hat\theta_\lambda - \theta)^2\big] = \underbrace{\Big(\frac{\lambda}{1 + \lambda}\Big)^2\theta^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\frac{v}{(1 + \lambda)^2}}_{\text{variance}},$$ which is strictly smaller than the error $v$ of $\hat\theta$ for every $0 \lt \lambda \lt 2v/(\theta^2 - v)$ (for every $\lambda > 0$ if $\theta^2 \leq v$). It is minimised at $\lambda^* = v/\theta^2$.

Proof

Step 1: bias and variance. By linearity, $$\E[\hat\theta_\lambda] = \frac{\theta}{1 + \lambda}, \qquad \text{bias} = \frac{\theta}{1 + \lambda} - \theta = -\frac{\lambda\,\theta}{1 + \lambda}, \qquad \Var(\hat\theta_\lambda) = \frac{v}{(1 + \lambda)^2}.$$ The MSE is bias² plus variance (step 2 of the proof of ), which gives the formula.

Step 2: change variable. Let $s = \frac{1}{1 + \lambda} \in (0, 1]$, so that $\frac{\lambda}{1 + \lambda} = 1 - s$. The MSE becomes the parabola $$m(s) = (1 - s)^2\,\theta^2 + s^2\,v, \qquad m(1) = v \ \ (\text{no shrinkage}).$$

Step 3: the optimum. $$m'(s) = -2(1 - s)\,\theta^2 + 2s\,v = 0 \iff s^* = \frac{\theta^2}{\theta^2 + v} \lt 1 \iff \lambda^* = \frac{v}{\theta^2}.$$

Step 4: when shrinkage helps. Let $s \lt 1$. Using $1 - s^2 = (1 - s)(1 + s)$ and dividing by $1 - s > 0$, $$\begin{aligned} m(s) \lt v &\iff (1 - s)^2\,\theta^2 \lt (1 - s)(1 + s)\,v \\ &\iff (1 - s)\,\theta^2 \lt (1 + s)\,v \\ &\iff s > \frac{\theta^2 - v}{\theta^2 + v}. \end{aligned}$$ If $\theta^2 \leq v$ the right-hand side is $\leq 0$, so every $\lambda > 0$ helps. Otherwise, substituting $s = 1/(1 + \lambda)$ gives $\lambda \lt 2v/(\theta^2 - v)$.

Small $\lambda$ gives low bias and high variance, and large $\lambda$ the reverse. The best $\lambda$ depends on unknown quantities (here $\theta$ and $v$), so in practice we estimate it from data, by cross-validation.

Cross-validation and model selection

A single validation set wastes data and gives a noisy estimate, especially when $n$ is small. Cross-validation reuses the data: every point is used for validation exactly once.

Split the training data at random into $K$ folds $F_1, \dots, F_K$ of (nearly) equal size. For each $k$, fit the model on all folds except $F_k$, giving $\hat f^{(-k)}$, and compute its error on $F_k$. The cross-validation error is the average $$\mathrm{CV}_K = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{1}{|F_k|}\sum_{i \in F_k}\big(y_i - \hat f^{(-k)}(\bx_i)\big)^2.$$ With $K = n$ (one point per fold) this is leave-one-out cross-validation.

To choose a hyperparameter such as $\lambda$:

  1. set aside a test set and do not touch it;
  2. for each candidate $\lambda$ on a grid (usually logarithmic, e.g. $10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 10^{2}$), compute $\mathrm{CV}_K(\lambda)$ on the remaining data;
  3. choose $\hat\lambda = \argmin_\lambda \mathrm{CV}_K(\lambda)$;
  4. refit with $\hat\lambda$ on all the non-test data, and report its error on the test set.

Some practical points:

  • Choice of $K$. $K = 5$ or $10$ is standard. It costs $K$ fits per candidate value. Leave-one-out uses the most data per fit but is expensive in general and its estimate can be very variable.
  • One-standard-error rule. The CV curve is itself noisy. A common choice is the simplest model whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.
  • Preprocessing inside the folds. Standardisation, feature selection and any other data-dependent step must be refitted within each training fold, or the CV error leaks information.
  • Structured data. Random folds assume i.i.d. data. For time series, validate on the future. For grouped data, such as several houses from the same street, keep each group in one fold.

Key takeaways

  • Fit on the training set, choose hyperparameters on validation data (or by cross-validation), and use the test set once at the end. Fit all preprocessing on training data only.
  • Expected test error = bias² + variance + noise. Capacity trades bias for variance.
  • Ridge, $\hat\theta = (\bX\T\bX + n\lambda I)^{-1}\bX\T\by$, always exists and shrinks all coefficients. The lasso sets some coefficients exactly to zero (soft thresholding).
  • The corners of the L1 ball explain the sparsity of the lasso. The elastic net mixes both penalties.
  • A little shrinkage always helps an unbiased estimator. How much is chosen by $K$-fold cross-validation.

Probabilistic view: regularisation as a prior

In Chapter 2 (extra) least squares appeared as maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise. Here penalties appear as prior beliefs about the parameters.

In the Bayesian approach the parameters are random too. Before seeing data we express our beliefs in a prior $p(\theta)$. After seeing data, Bayes' rule gives the posterior $$p(\theta \mid \mathcal D) = \frac{p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal D)} \ \propto\ p(\mathcal D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta).$$ The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximises the posterior, or equivalently minimises $-\log p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta)$: a data-fit term plus a penalty.

Assume the Gaussian noise model $y_i \mid \bx_i \sim \mathcal N(\bx_i\T\theta, \sigma^2)$ (independent).

  1. With independent Gaussian priors $\theta_j \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)$, the MAP estimate is the ridge estimate with $\lambda = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\tau^2}$.
  2. With independent Laplace priors $p(\theta_j) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|\theta_j|/b}$, the MAP estimate is the lasso estimate with $\lambda = \dfrac{2\sigma^2}{nb}$.
Proof

Step 1: the likelihood term. Up to constants that do not depend on $\theta$, $$-\log p(\mathcal D \mid \theta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 .$$

Step 2: Gaussian prior (part 1). $-\log p(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\theta}_2^2 + \text{const}$. Multiply the sum by the positive constant $2\sigma^2/n$, which does not change the minimiser: $$\frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{n\tau^2}\norm{\theta}_2^2 .$$

Step 3: Laplace prior (part 2). $-\log p(\theta) = \frac1b\norm{\theta}_1 + \text{const}$. Multiplying by $2\sigma^2/n$ gives $$\frac1n\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 + \frac{2\sigma^2}{nb}\norm{\theta}_1 .$$

The formulas make sense. A narrow prior (small $\tau$ or $b$: strong belief that coefficients are small) means a large $\lambda$. A lot of data (large $n$) means a small $\lambda$: the data overrule the prior. The Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero and heavier tails than the Gaussian. It says "most coefficients are zero, a few may be large", which is what the lasso delivers.

Under the Gaussian prior $\theta \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2 I)$, the posterior is Gaussian, $\theta \mid \mathcal D \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ with $$\Sigma = \Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\bX\T\bX + \frac{1}{\tau^2}I\Big)^{-1}, \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\Sigma\bX\T\by.$$ In particular the posterior mean $\mu$ equals the ridge estimate, and $\Sigma$ quantifies the uncertainty about $\theta$ that remains after seeing the data.

Proof

Step 1: the log-posterior is quadratic. Up to a constant, $$-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\by - \bX\theta}^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\norm{\theta}^2 = -\frac12\theta\T\Sigma^{-1}\theta + \theta\T\frac{\bX\T\by}{\sigma^2} + \text{const}.$$

Step 2: complete the square. This equals $-\frac12(\theta - \mu)\T\Sigma^{-1}(\theta - \mu) + \text{const}$ with $\Sigma^{-1}\mu = \bX\T\by/\sigma^2$, which is the log-density of $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$.

Step 3: identify the mean. $$\mu = \Big(\bX\T\bX + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I\Big)^{-1}\bX\T\by ,$$ the ridge solution with $n\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.
