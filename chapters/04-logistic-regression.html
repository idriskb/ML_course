Logistic regression

Our first classifier. Instead of predicting a label directly, logistic regression predicts the probability of each class with a linear score passed through the sigmoid. We derive its loss from the Bernoulli likelihood, train it by gradient descent, extend it to many classes with the softmax, and learn how to evaluate a classifier properly.

Learning objectives

  • State the logistic regression model, interpret its coefficients as log-odds, and describe its decision boundary.
  • Derive the cross-entropy loss from the Bernoulli likelihood, and compute its gradient and Hessian.
  • Extend the model to $K$ classes with the softmax function.
  • Evaluate a classifier with the confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, the ROC curve and the AUC.

Why linear regression fails for classification

In classification the target takes finitely many values. We start with two classes, coded $y \in \{0, 1\}$. Our running example is the breast cancer dataset of Lab 2: from measurements of cell nuclei in a biopsy image, decide whether a tumour is malignant ($y = 1$) or benign ($y = 0$).

A first idea is to reuse linear regression: fit $\hat y = \bx\T\theta$ to the 0/1 labels by least squares, and predict "malignant" when $\hat y \geq 0.5$. This has three problems.

  1. The outputs are not probabilities. $\bx\T\theta$ ranges over all of $\R$. A prediction of $-0.7$ or $1.8$ has no meaning as a probability.
  2. The fit is pulled by easy points. Least squares penalises a very large tumour predicted at $\hat y = 3$ as heavily as a mistake, although it is classified correctly with great confidence. Such points tilt the line and move the threshold ().
  3. The loss does not match the data. Squared error is natural for real-valued noise around a mean. Labels that are either $0$ or $1$ call for a different model of randomness.

Classification as probabilistic prediction

Instead of predicting the label, we predict the probability that the label is $1$: $$p(\bx) = \P(y = 1 \mid \bx), \qquad \P(y = 0 \mid \bx) = 1 - p(\bx).$$ A probability is more useful than a bare label. It says how confident the model is. It can be turned into a decision with a threshold suited to the application, for example a low threshold for cancer screening where missing a malignant tumour is costly. It can also be compared and ranked across patients. So we need a model whose output always lies in $(0, 1)$.

The logistic or sigmoid function is $$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad z \in \R.$$

The sigmoid has the following properties.

  1. $0 \lt \sigma(z) \lt 1$, $\sigma$ is strictly increasing, $\sigma(z) \to 0$ as $z \to -\infty$, $\sigma(z) \to 1$ as $z \to \infty$, and $\sigma(0) = \frac12$.
  2. Symmetry: $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$.
  3. Derivative: $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)$.
  4. Inverse: if $p = \sigma(z)$ then $z = \log\dfrac{p}{1 - p}$, the log-odds or logit of $p$.
Proof

(1) $e^{-z} > 0$, so $1 + e^{-z} > 1$ and $0 \lt \sigma(z) \lt 1$. As $z$ increases, $e^{-z}$ decreases, so $\sigma$ increases. The limits follow from $e^{-z} \to \infty$ and $e^{-z} \to 0$, and $\sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac12$.

(2) Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^{z}$: $$1 - \sigma(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{1}{e^{z} + 1} = \sigma(-z).$$

(3) By the chain rule, $$\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}}_{\sigma(z)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}}_{1 - \sigma(z)}.$$

(4) From $p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ we get $e^{-z} = \frac{1}{p} - 1 = \frac{1 - p}{p}$, so $z = \log\frac{p}{1 - p}$.

The logistic regression model

With $\bx = (1, x_1, \dots, x_p)\T$ including the constant feature, logistic regression models $$\P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \sigma(\bx\T\theta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\bx\T\theta}}, \qquad \theta \in \R^{p+1}.$$ The linear score $z = \bx\T\theta = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \dots + \theta_p x_p$ is the evidence in favour of class $1$, and $\sigma$ turns it into a probability.

By (4) the model can be written in a form that looks like linear regression, but on the log-odds scale: $$\log\frac{\P(y = 1 \mid \bx)}{\P(y = 0 \mid \bx)} = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \dots + \theta_p x_p.$$ This gives the coefficients a precise meaning.

If feature $x_j$ increases by one unit while the other features stay fixed, the odds $\P(y = 1 \mid \bx)/\P(y = 0 \mid \bx)$ are multiplied by $e^{\theta_j}$.

Proof

The odds equal $e^{\bx\T\theta}$. Replacing $x_j$ by $x_j + 1$ adds $\theta_j$ to the score, so the odds become $e^{\bx\T\theta + \theta_j} = e^{\theta_j}\, e^{\bx\T\theta}$.

For example, $\theta_j = 0.7$ means each extra unit of $x_j$ roughly doubles the odds of malignancy ($e^{0.7} \approx 2.01$). A negative $\theta_j$ decreases them, and $\theta_j = 0$ means $x_j$ has no effect.

Decision boundaries

To make a decision we choose a threshold $\tau \in (0, 1)$ and predict $\hat y = 1$ if $\sigma(\bx\T\theta) \geq \tau$. The default is $\tau = \frac12$.

Write $\theta = (\theta_0, \mathbf w)$ with $\mathbf w = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_p)$. The region where logistic regression predicts class $1$ is the half-space $$\big\{\bx : \theta_0 + \mathbf w\T\bx \geq \operatorname{logit}(\tau)\big\}, \qquad \operatorname{logit}(\tau) = \log\frac{\tau}{1 - \tau}.$$ In particular, for $\tau = \frac12$ the decision boundary is the hyperplane $\theta_0 + \mathbf w\T\bx = 0$, and the score is proportional to the signed distance of $\bx$ to it: $$\operatorname{dist}(\bx) = \frac{\theta_0 + \mathbf w\T\bx}{\norm{\mathbf w}}.$$

Proof

Step 1. Since $\sigma$ is strictly increasing with inverse $\operatorname{logit}$, $$\sigma(z) \geq \tau \iff z \geq \operatorname{logit}(\tau).$$ For $\tau = \frac12$, $\operatorname{logit}(\frac12) = \log 1 = 0$.

Step 2. The vector $\mathbf w$ is normal to the hyperplane $H = \{\theta_0 + \mathbf w\T\bx = 0\}$. Moving from a point of $H$ by $t$ along the unit normal $\mathbf w/\norm{\mathbf w}$ changes the score by $t\norm{\mathbf w}$. Hence the score divided by $\norm{\mathbf w}$ is the signed distance to $H$.

So logistic regression is a linear classifier: with one feature the boundary is a threshold, with two a straight line, with more a hyperplane. Points far from the boundary get probabilities close to $0$ or $1$. Points near it get probabilities near $\frac12$. A larger $\norm{\mathbf w}$ makes the transition sharper. With two features, the boundary $\theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2 = 0$ is the line $$x_2 = -\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_2} - \frac{\theta_1}{\theta_2}\, x_1 .$$

A dataset is linearly separable if some hyperplane has all points of class $1$ strictly on one side and all points of class $0$ strictly on the other: there is $\bar\theta$ with $\bx_i\T\bar\theta > 0$ when $y_i = 1$ and $\bx_i\T\bar\theta \lt 0$ when $y_i = 0$.

Curved boundaries with the same model

The boundary is linear in the features. Adding features such as $x_1^2$, $x_2^2$ and $x_1 x_2$ gives a boundary that is a conic section in the original variables: an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola. The loss and the algorithm stay exactly the same. For data such as the two moons of the later labs we will need more flexible feature maps: kernels (Chapter 9) and neural networks (Chapter 10).

The loss: Bernoulli likelihood and cross-entropy

To fit $\theta$ we need a loss. Since the model outputs a probability, the natural criterion is maximum likelihood: choose the $\theta$ under which the observed labels are most probable. A binary label with $\P(y = 1) = p$ has a Bernoulli distribution, whose probability mass function can be written in one line: $$\P(y) = p^{\,y}(1 - p)^{1 - y}, \qquad y \in \{0, 1\}$$ (for $y = 1$ this gives $p$, for $y = 0$ it gives $1 - p$). Write $p_i = \sigma(\bx_i\T\theta)$ for the predicted probability of example $i$.

  1. Assume the labels are independent given the features. The probability of all observed labels, as a function of $\theta$, is the likelihood.

    $$\mathcal L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p_i^{\,y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}.$$

  2. Take logarithms. The log is increasing, so it has the same maximiser, and it turns the product into a sum.

    $$\log\mathcal L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big[y_i\log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\Big].$$

  3. Maximising the log-likelihood is minimising its negative. Divide by $n$ to get an average, as for the MSE.

    $$L(\theta) = -\frac1n\log\mathcal L(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \Big[-y_i\log p_i - (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\Big].$$

The binary cross-entropy (or log-loss) of a predicted probability $p$ for a label $y \in \{0, 1\}$ is $$\ell(p, y) = -y\log p - (1 - y)\log(1 - p) = \begin{cases} -\log p & \text{if } y = 1, \\ -\log(1 - p) & \text{if } y = 0. \end{cases}$$ Logistic regression minimises the average cross-entropy $L(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_i \ell(p_i, y_i)$.

The loss is $0$ only for a perfectly confident correct prediction, and it grows without bound as a wrong prediction becomes confident. Predicting $p = 0.01$ for a malignant tumour costs $-\log 0.01 \approx 4.6$, while $p = 0.4$ costs only $0.92$. In terms of the score $z = \bx\T\theta$ and $s = 2y - 1 \in \{-1, +1\}$, symmetry of $\sigma$ gives the compact form $$\ell = \log\big(1 + e^{-s z}\big),$$ which is small when $s z \gg 0$ (correct side of the boundary, far from it) and roughly $-sz$, i.e. linear, when $s z \ll 0$.

Why not the squared error $(y_i - p_i)^2$?

It can be used, but it is a poor choice. As a function of $\theta$ it is not convex, so gradient descent can get stuck. Its penalty for a confident mistake is at most $1$, and its gradient almost vanishes there (). The cross-entropy penalises confident mistakes heavily and, as we show next, is convex.

Optimisation by gradient descent

Unlike least squares, setting the gradient of $L$ to zero gives non-linear equations in $\theta$ with no closed-form solution. We minimise $L$ numerically. The gradient turns out to be remarkably simple.

The gradient and Hessian of the average cross-entropy are $$\nabla L(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (p_i - y_i)\,\bx_i = \frac1n\bX\T(\mathbf p - \by), \qquad \nabla^2 L(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n p_i(1 - p_i)\,\bx_i\bx_i\T = \frac1n\bX\T S\bX,$$ where $\mathbf p = (p_1, \dots, p_n)\T$ and $S = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big)$.

Proof

Fix one example and drop the index $i$: $\ell = -y\log p - (1 - y)\log(1 - p)$ with $p = \sigma(z)$ and $z = \bx\T\theta$. Apply the chain rule $\nabla_\theta\ell = \frac{d\ell}{dp}\cdot\frac{dp}{dz}\cdot\nabla_\theta z$.

Step 1: derivative in $p$. $$\frac{d\ell}{dp} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)}.$$

Step 2: derivative of the sigmoid. By (3), $\dfrac{dp}{dz} = p(1 - p)$. Also $\nabla_\theta z = \bx$.

Step 3: multiply. The factors $p(1 - p)$ cancel: $$\nabla_\theta\ell = \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)}\cdot p(1 - p)\cdot\bx = (p - y)\,\bx.$$ Averaging over the examples gives $\nabla L$.

Step 4: Hessian. Differentiate $(p - y)\,\bx$ once more. Only $p$ depends on $\theta$, and $\nabla_\theta p = p(1 - p)\,\bx$, so $$\nabla^2_\theta\ell = \bx\,\big(\nabla_\theta p\big)\T = p(1 - p)\,\bx\bx\T.$$ Averaging gives $\nabla^2 L$.

Compare with least squares, where $\nabla L = \frac2n\bX\T(\bX\theta - \by)$. In both cases the gradient is features times prediction errors. Only the prediction has changed, from $\bX\theta$ to $\sigma(\bX\theta)$.

The average cross-entropy $L(\theta)$ of logistic regression is convex. If $\bX$ has full column rank, it is strictly convex, so it has at most one minimiser.

Proof

For every $\mathbf v$, using , $$\mathbf v\T\nabla^2 L(\theta)\,\mathbf v = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \underbrace{p_i(1 - p_i)}_{>\ 0}\,\big(\bx_i\T\mathbf v\big)^2 \ \geq\ 0 .$$ So the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere and $L$ is convex. The sum is $0$ only if $\bx_i\T\mathbf v = 0$ for all $i$, i.e. $\bX\mathbf v = 0$. With full column rank this forces $\mathbf v = 0$, so the Hessian is positive definite and $L$ is strictly convex.

Convexity means there are no bad local minima: gradient descent with a suitable learning rate approaches the lowest possible loss. The updates are those of Chapter 2 with the new gradient: $$\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(p_i^{(t)} - y_i\big)\,\bx_i \qquad\text{(gradient descent)},$$ $$\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \frac{\eta}{m}\sum_{i \in B_t} \big(p_i^{(t)} - y_i\big)\,\bx_i \qquad\text{(mini-batch SGD, batch } B_t \text{ of size } m).$$

How large can $\eta$ be? Since $p_i(1 - p_i) \leq \frac14$, the Hessian of is never more curved than $$\nabla^2 L(\theta) \preceq \frac{1}{4n}\,\bX\T\bX \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \lambda_{\max}\big(\nabla^2 L\big) \leq \frac14\,\lambda_{\max}\Big(\tfrac1n\bX\T\bX\Big) =: M .$$ Exactly as for least squares in Chapter 2, a step $\eta \lt 2/M$ is guaranteed to decrease the loss, and the starting point plays no role in this condition. In the figure, $\lambda_{\max}(\frac1n\bX\T\bX)$ is about $1.4$ for standardised features (safe up to $\eta \approx 5.6$) but about $614$ in raw units (safe only up to $\eta \approx 0.013$). In raw units the column of sizes ($\approx 14$) and of textures ($\approx 20$) dwarf the intercept column of ones, so the loss is far more curved in some directions than others. Too large a step then overshoots and the loss blows up, while a safe step makes very slow progress. Standardising the features removes this imbalance.

Separable data and regularisation

The second experiment in is not a bug.

If the data are linearly separable, the cross-entropy $L$ has no minimiser: $\inf_\theta L(\theta) = 0$, but $L(\theta) > 0$ for every $\theta$.

Proof

Step 1: $L$ is never zero. Each term $\ell_i = \log(1 + e^{-s_i z_i})$ is strictly positive because $1 + e^{-s_i z_i} > 1$. So $L(\theta) > 0$ for every $\theta$.

Step 2: $L$ gets arbitrarily close to zero. Let $\bar\theta$ separate the data, so that $s_i\,\bx_i\T\bar\theta > 0$ for every $i$ (with $s_i = 2y_i - 1$). Along the ray $\theta = c\,\bar\theta$, $c > 0$, $$L(c\bar\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log\Big(1 + e^{-c\, s_i \bx_i\T\bar\theta}\Big) \ \xrightarrow[c \to \infty]{}\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + 0) = 0,$$ since every exponent tends to $-\infty$.

Step 3: conclude. The infimum is $0$ and it is never attained, so there is no minimiser.

On separable data, scaling up a separating $\theta$ always lowers the loss. Gradient descent therefore sends $\norm{\theta} \to \infty$, and the predicted probabilities become $0$ or $1$, which is overconfident. The remedy is the one from Chapter 3: add a penalty. $$L_\lambda(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(p_i, y_i) + \lambda\norm{\mathbf w}_2^2 \quad\text{(L2)}, \qquad L_\lambda(\theta) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(p_i, y_i) + \lambda\norm{\mathbf w}_1 \quad\text{(L1)}.$$ As before, the intercept is not penalised and the features should be standardised. With the L2 penalty, $L_\lambda$ is strictly convex in $\mathbf w$ and tends to infinity as $\norm{\mathbf w} \to \infty$, so a unique minimiser always exists. The L1 penalty again selects features. In scikit-learn, LogisticRegression is L2-regularised by default, with C playing the role of $1/\lambda$ (up to scaling).

More than two classes: the softmax

Suppose $y \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, for example several tumour types. We give each class its own linear score $z_k = \bx\T\theta_k$ and turn the $K$ scores into probabilities.

The softmax of scores $z = (z_1, \dots, z_K)$ is the probability vector $$\operatorname{softmax}(z)_k = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K.$$ Multinomial logistic regression (or softmax regression) models $\P(y = k \mid \bx) = \operatorname{softmax}(\bx\T\theta_1, \dots, \bx\T\theta_K)_k$ and predicts the class with the largest probability, i.e. the largest score.

  1. The outputs are positive and sum to $1$.
  2. Adding the same constant $c$ to all scores does not change the softmax. Hence one class can be used as a reference with $\theta_K = 0$.
  3. For $K = 2$, $\operatorname{softmax}(z)_1 = \sigma(z_1 - z_2)$: softmax regression reduces to logistic regression with $\theta = \theta_1 - \theta_2$.
Proof

(1) Exponentials are positive, and the $K$ fractions share the same denominator, which is the sum of their numerators.

(2) $\dfrac{e^{z_k + c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j + c}} = \dfrac{e^{c}\,e^{z_k}}{e^{c}\sum_j e^{z_j}} = \operatorname{softmax}(z)_k$. Subtracting $\theta_K$ from every $\theta_k$ subtracts $\bx\T\theta_K$ from every score, which changes nothing and makes the last parameter vector $0$.

(3) Divide numerator and denominator by $e^{z_1}$: $$\frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)}} = \sigma(z_1 - z_2).$$

Training follows the binary case. The labels have a categorical distribution, and the negative log-likelihood is the multiclass cross-entropy $$L(\Theta) = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_{i, y_i} = -\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K \mathbf 1\{y_i = k\}\,\log p_{ik}, \qquad p_{ik} = \P(y_i = k \mid \bx_i).$$

The gradient of the multiclass cross-entropy with respect to $\theta_k$ is $$\nabla_{\theta_k} L = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \big(p_{ik} - \mathbf 1\{y_i = k\}\big)\,\bx_i,$$ "predicted minus observed", exactly as in the binary case.

Proof

Fix one example with label $y$ and scores $z_j = \bx\T\theta_j$. Its loss is $$\ell = -\log p_y = -z_y + \log\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}.$$

Step 1: derivative in the score $z_k$. $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z_k} = -\mathbf 1\{y = k\} + \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = p_k - \mathbf 1\{y = k\}.$$

Step 2: chain rule. Only $z_k$ depends on $\theta_k$, and $\nabla_{\theta_k} z_k = \bx$, so $\nabla_{\theta_k}\ell = (p_k - \mathbf 1\{y = k\})\,\bx$. Average over the examples.

The decision regions $\{\bx : \bx\T\theta_k \geq \bx\T\theta_j \text{ for all } j\}$ are intersections of half-spaces, so a softmax classifier divides the feature space into convex polygonal regions. The softmax with cross-entropy is also the standard final layer of neural network classifiers (Chapter 10).

Evaluating a classifier

A classifier is evaluated on a test set by comparing its predictions with the true labels. We call class $1$ (malignant) positive and class $0$ (benign) negative.

The confusion matrix

Confusion matrix of a binary classifier
Predicted negativePredicted positive
Actually negativeTN true negativesFP false positives (type I error)
Actually positiveFN false negatives (type II error)TP true positives
  • Accuracy $= \dfrac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$, the fraction of correct predictions.
  • Precision $= \dfrac{TP}{TP + FP}$: of the tumours predicted malignant, the fraction that are.
  • Recall (sensitivity, true positive rate) $= \dfrac{TP}{TP + FN}$: of the malignant tumours, the fraction detected.
  • False positive rate $= \dfrac{FP}{FP + TN}$, and specificity $= 1 - \text{FPR} = \dfrac{TN}{TN + FP}$.
  • F1-score $= 2\,\dfrac{\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}$, the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

On a test set of 143 tumours a classifier gives $TN = 85$, $FP = 5$, $FN = 10$, $TP = 43$. Then accuracy $= 128/143 \approx 0.90$, precision $= 43/48 \approx 0.90$, recall $= 43/53 \approx 0.81$, specificity $= 85/90 \approx 0.94$, and $F_1 \approx 0.85$. One malignant tumour in five is missed. In a screening context that is the number to worry about, and accuracy hides it.

Accuracy on imbalanced data

If only 5% of patients have cancer, the useless rule "always predict benign" has 95% accuracy and 0% recall. With imbalanced classes, report precision, recall and F1 (or the ROC curve) for the rare class.

Choosing the threshold

The metrics above depend on the threshold $\tau$. Lowering $\tau$ predicts "malignant" more often: recall goes up, but so do false positives, so precision usually goes down. There is no universally right $\tau$. It depends on the costs of the two kinds of error: screening favours high recall, while a spam filter favours high precision (losing a real e-mail is worse than letting spam through).

The ROC curve and the AUC

The ROC curve (receiver operating characteristic) of a classifier that outputs scores is the curve of points $\big(\text{FPR}(\tau), \text{TPR}(\tau)\big)$ obtained as the threshold $\tau$ goes from $+\infty$ (nothing predicted positive: point $(0, 0)$) to $-\infty$ (everything positive: point $(1, 1)$). The AUC is the area under this curve.

A perfect classifier passes through the top-left corner $(0, 1)$ and has AUC $= 1$. A classifier that guesses at random follows the diagonal, with AUC $= \frac12$. The AUC summarises performance over all thresholds, and it has a direct interpretation.

Let a test set contain $P$ positives with scores $s^+_1, \dots, s^+_P$ and $N$ negatives with scores $s^-_1, \dots, s^-_N$, all distinct. Then $$\text{AUC} = \frac{1}{PN}\,\#\big\{(i, j) : s^+_i > s^-_j\big\},$$ the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs that the classifier ranks correctly. It estimates the probability that a random malignant tumour gets a higher score than a random benign one.

Proof

Step 1: the curve is a staircase. Sort all $P + N$ scores in decreasing order and lower the threshold past them one at a time. Passing a positive increases TP by one, so the curve moves up by $1/P$. Passing a negative increases FP by one, so it moves right by $1/N$.

Step 2: area of one column. The area under a staircase is the sum of the areas of the columns below its horizontal steps. The step created by negative $j$ has width $1/N$, and its height is the TPR at that moment: the fraction of positives already passed, which is exactly the fraction with a higher score, $$\text{height}_j = \frac{1}{P}\,\#\{i : s^+_i > s^-_j\}.$$

Step 3: add the columns. $$\text{AUC} = \sum_{j=1}^N \frac1N\cdot\frac1P\,\#\{i : s^+_i > s^-_j\} = \frac{1}{PN}\,\#\{(i, j) : s^+_i > s^-_j\}.$$

Key takeaways

  • Logistic regression models $\P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \sigma(\bx\T\theta)$, i.e. the log-odds are linear in $\bx$. Coefficients act multiplicatively on the odds.
  • Its decision boundary is a hyperplane. Curved boundaries need non-linear features.
  • The loss is the cross-entropy, the negative log of the Bernoulli likelihood. It is convex, with gradient $\frac1n\bX\T(\mathbf p - \by)$, and is minimised by (stochastic) gradient descent.
  • On separable data the unregularised optimum does not exist. Add an L2 or L1 penalty.
  • The softmax extends the model to $K$ classes with the same "predicted minus observed" gradient.
  • Evaluate with the confusion matrix, precision, recall and F1 at a chosen threshold, and with the ROC curve and AUC across all thresholds.

Probabilistic view: cross-entropy, information, and generative models

Two complementary perspectives. Information theory explains the name "cross-entropy". Generative modelling explains when the logistic form is exactly right.

Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence

For distributions $q$ and $p$ on a finite set, define $$H(q) = -\sum_y q(y)\log q(y), \qquad H(q, p) = -\sum_y q(y)\log p(y), \qquad \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \sum_y q(y)\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)}.$$ $H(q)$ is the entropy: the average number of bits (with $\log_2$) needed to encode samples of $q$ with an optimal code. $H(q, p)$ is the cross-entropy: the average length when samples of $q$ are encoded with a code optimised for $p$. The Kullback–Leibler divergence is the extra cost of using the wrong code.

$H(q, p) = H(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ and $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) \geq 0$, with equality if and only if $p = q$. For a one-hot $q$ (all mass on the observed label $y_i$), $H(q) = 0$ and $H(q, p) = -\log p(y_i)$, the cross-entropy loss of example $i$.

Proof

Step 1: decomposition. Add and subtract $\sum_y q(y)\log q(y)$: $$H(q, p) = -\sum_y q(y)\log q(y) + \sum_y q(y)\log\frac{q(y)}{p(y)} = H(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p).$$

Step 2: positivity. Use $\log u \leq u - 1$, with equality only at $u = 1$, applied to $u = p(y)/q(y)$ (sum over $y$ with $q(y) > 0$): $$-\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \sum_y q(y)\log\frac{p(y)}{q(y)} \leq \sum_y q(y)\Big(\frac{p(y)}{q(y)} - 1\Big) \leq 1 - 1 = 0.$$ Equality requires $p(y) = q(y)$ for all $y$.

Step 3: one-hot labels. If $q(y_i) = 1$ and $q = 0$ elsewhere, then $H(q) = -1\cdot\log 1 = 0$ and $H(q, p) = -\log p(y_i)$.

So minimising the average cross-entropy minimises the average KL divergence between the observed labels and the model's predicted distributions: it pushes the model's probabilities towards the truth.

Discriminative versus generative classifiers

Logistic regression is discriminative: it models $\P(y \mid \bx)$ directly and says nothing about how the features are distributed. A generative classifier instead models the class proportions $\pi_k = \P(y = k)$ and the class-conditional densities $p(\bx \mid y = k)$, then uses Bayes' rule. The two views meet in a classical case.

Suppose $\bx \mid y = k \sim \mathcal N(\mu_k, \Sigma)$ for $k = 0, 1$, with the same covariance $\Sigma$. Then $$\P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \sigma(\mathbf w\T\bx + b), \qquad \mathbf w = \Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_0),\quad b = -\tfrac12\mu_1\T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_1 + \tfrac12\mu_0\T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_0 + \log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}.$$ The posterior has exactly the logistic regression form.

Proof

Step 1: Bayes' rule. With $f_k$ the Gaussian density of class $k$, $$\P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \frac{\pi_1 f_1(\bx)}{\pi_0 f_0(\bx) + \pi_1 f_1(\bx)} = \sigma(a(\bx)), \qquad a(\bx) = \log\frac{\pi_1 f_1(\bx)}{\pi_0 f_0(\bx)}.$$

Step 2: the quadratic terms cancel. The normalising constants are equal (same $\Sigma$), so $$\log\frac{f_1(\bx)}{f_0(\bx)} = -\tfrac12(\bx - \mu_1)\T\Sigma^{-1}(\bx - \mu_1) + \tfrac12(\bx - \mu_0)\T\Sigma^{-1}(\bx - \mu_0).$$ Expanding, the $\bx\T\Sigma^{-1}\bx$ terms cancel and $$\log\frac{f_1(\bx)}{f_0(\bx)} = (\mu_1 - \mu_0)\T\Sigma^{-1}\bx - \tfrac12\mu_1\T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_1 + \tfrac12\mu_0\T\Sigma^{-1}\mu_0 .$$

Step 3: conclude. Adding $\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)$ gives $a(\bx) = \mathbf w\T\bx + b$.

The generative route (here, linear discriminant analysis) estimates $\mu_0, \mu_1, \Sigma, \pi_k$ and plugs them in. It is efficient when its Gaussian assumption holds, and it can generate new samples. Logistic regression estimates $\mathbf w, b$ directly, so it remains valid whenever the log-odds are linear, under weaker assumptions. With different covariances the quadratic terms no longer cancel and the boundary becomes quadratic.
