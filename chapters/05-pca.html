Dimensionality reduction: PCA

Our first unsupervised method. Given data without labels, principal component analysis finds the few directions along which the data vary the most. Projecting onto them compresses the data, makes them easy to visualise, and loses as little information as possible. Everything follows from one object, the covariance matrix, and one tool, its eigen-decomposition.

Learning objectives

  • Explain what makes unsupervised learning different, and why reducing dimension is useful.
  • Use a Lagrange multiplier to show that the direction of maximum variance is the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix, and that the maximum variance is its largest eigenvalue.
  • Explain why PCA is the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix.
  • Express the variance kept and the reconstruction error of $k$ components with eigenvalues.
  • Compute PCA with the eigen-decomposition or the SVD, and explain why centring and scaling matter.
  • Choose the number of components, and recognise the limitations of PCA.

From supervised to unsupervised learning

So far every example came with a target: a price $y_i$ for each house, a label for each tumour. We could measure errors against the truth and minimise them. In unsupervised learning we only have the inputs $$\bx_1, \dots, \bx_n \in \R^p,$$ and the goal is to find structure in them. This is harder, for three reasons.

  • There is no target. We must decide what "structure" means: large variance, small distances, high density, and so on.
  • Several structures can be plausible. The same images could be grouped by shape, by thickness of the stroke, or by brightness.
  • Evaluation is indirect. Without a ground truth we judge a solution by interpretability, stability, or how much it helps a later supervised task.

A useful way to think about structure is through latent variables: hidden quantities that generate the measurements.

  • Continuous latent variables. Images of a person walking have thousands of pixels, but they are essentially described by one number, the phase of the stride. The data lie close to a low-dimensional set inside $\R^p$. PCA, the topic of this chapter, looks for such a set when it is flat (a linear subspace).
  • Discrete latent variables. An image of a handwritten digit is essentially described by which digit it is, one of ten categories. Recovering such groups is clustering, the topic of the next chapter (k-means).

Why reduce dimension?

Replacing $\bx_i \in \R^p$ by a few numbers $\bz_i \in \R^k$, with $k \ll p$, helps in several ways.

  • Visualisation. We cannot plot 64 pixels, but we can plot two well-chosen combinations of them.
  • Compression. Storing $k$ numbers per example instead of $p$.
  • Denoising. Noise spreads over all directions, while the signal often concentrates in a few. Dropping the weak directions removes mostly noise.
  • Better supervised learning. Fewer, uncorrelated features make regression more stable (recall collinearity in Chapter 2) and training faster. In high dimension, data become sparse and distances less informative (the curse of dimensionality, which we meet again with k-nearest neighbours).

We use two running examples: a small set of houses described by two features, area and price (so we can see everything), and 150 handwritten digits from scikit-learn (8×8 grey-level images, so $p = 64$).

Centred data and the covariance matrix

Throughout, the data are centred: we subtract the mean $\bar\bx = \frac1n\sum_i \bx_i$ from every observation, so that $\sum_i \bx_i = \mathbf 0$. We will see in why this matters. Stack the centred observations as the rows of the data matrix $\bX \in \R^{n \times p}$.

The sample covariance matrix of centred data is $$\Sigma = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\bx_i\T = \frac1n\bX\T\bX \in \R^{p \times p}, \qquad \Sigma_{jl} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_{ij}\,x_{il}.$$ The diagonal entry $\Sigma_{jj}$ is the variance of feature $j$. The off-diagonal entry $\Sigma_{jl}$ is the covariance of features $j$ and $l$: positive if they tend to be large together, negative if one tends to be large when the other is small. The total variance is $\tr(\Sigma) = \sum_j \Sigma_{jj}$.

Software often divides by $n - 1$ instead of $n$. This rescales every eigenvalue by the same factor and changes nothing below.

PCA describes the data along directions. For a unit vector $\bu \in \R^p$ ($\norm{\bu} = 1$), the score of $\bx_i$ along $\bu$ is the coordinate $z_i = \bu\T\bx_i$: how far $\bx_i$ reaches in the direction $\bu$.

Let $\bu$ be a unit vector and $z_i = \bu\T\bx_i$. The scores have mean $0$ and variance $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^2 = \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu .$$ In particular $\Sigma$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite: $\bv\T\Sigma\bv \geq 0$ for every $\bv$.

Proof

Step 1: mean. By linearity and centring, $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n z_i = \bu\T\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\Big) = \bu\T\mathbf 0 = 0.$$

Step 2: variance. Since the mean is $0$, the variance is the mean of the squares. Write $z_i^2 = (\bu\T\bx_i)(\bx_i\T\bu)$: $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n z_i^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bu\T\bx_i\bx_i\T\bu = \bu\T\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\bx_i\T\Big)\bu = \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu.$$

Step 3: symmetric and positive semidefinite. $\Sigma\T = \frac1n(\bX\T\bX)\T = \Sigma$. For any $\bv$, Step 2 (without the unit-norm assumption) gives $\bv\T\Sigma\bv = \frac1n\sum_i (\bv\T\bx_i)^2 \geq 0$.

The first principal component

Imagine the house data as an elongated cloud of points around the origin. We want to summarise each point by a single number: its score $z_i = \bu\T\bx_i$ along a direction $\bu$. A good summary should keep the points as spread out as possible, so we look for the direction of maximum variance. By , this variance is $\bu\T\Sigma\bu$, and we solve $$\max_{\bu \in \R^p}\ \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad \bu\T\bu = 1 .$$ The constraint is needed: without it, doubling $\bu$ would multiply the variance by $4$, and the maximum would be infinite. Only the direction matters.

Every solution of the problem above is an eigenvector of $\Sigma$, and the variance along an eigenvector equals its eigenvalue. Hence the maximum variance is the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of $\Sigma$, and the direction of maximum variance is a corresponding unit eigenvector $\bu_1$: $$\Sigma\,\bu_1 = \lambda_1\bu_1, \qquad \max_{\bu\T\bu = 1} \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu = \bu_1\T\Sigma\,\bu_1 = \lambda_1 .$$

Proof

Step 1: the Lagrangian. Introduce a multiplier $\lambda$ for the constraint $\bu\T\bu = 1$: $$J(\bu, \lambda) = \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu - \lambda\big(\bu\T\bu - 1\big).$$

Step 2: set the gradient to zero. Since $\Sigma$ is symmetric, $\nabla_{\bu}(\bu\T\Sigma\bu) = 2\Sigma\bu$, and $\nabla_{\bu}(\bu\T\bu) = 2\bu$. So $$\nabla_{\bu} J = 2\Sigma\,\bu - 2\lambda\,\bu = \mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\Sigma\,\bu = \lambda\,\bu} .$$ The derivative with respect to $\lambda$ gives back the constraint $\bu\T\bu = 1$. So any solution is a unit eigenvector of $\Sigma$, and the multiplier $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue.

Step 3: the variance at an eigenvector. If $\Sigma\bu = \lambda\bu$ and $\bu\T\bu = 1$, $$\bu\T\Sigma\,\bu = \bu\T(\lambda\,\bu) = \lambda\,\underbrace{\bu\T\bu}_{=\,1} = \lambda .$$ The variance along an eigenvector is its eigenvalue.

Step 4: pick the best eigenvector. The variance is a continuous function on the unit sphere, which is closed and bounded, so a maximiser exists. By Step 2 it is an eigenvector, and by Step 3 its variance is its eigenvalue. The largest possible value is therefore the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, reached at its eigenvector $\bu_1$.

The first principal component of $\bx_i$ is its score $z_{i1} = \bu_1\T\bx_i$, and the ratio $\lambda_1/\tr(\Sigma)$ is the fraction of the total variance it keeps.

The same direction is the closest line

There is a second natural criterion: replace each point by its orthogonal projection $(\bu\T\bx_i)\bu$ on the line, and ask for the line that loses the least, i.e. that is closest to the data. By Pythagoras (), it gives the same answer.

For every unit vector $\bu$, the average squared distance between the points and their projections is $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\bx_i - (\bu\T\bx_i)\bu}^2 = \tr(\Sigma) - \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu .$$ The total $\tr(\Sigma)$ does not depend on $\bu$, so the line of minimum reconstruction error is the direction of maximum variance, $\bu_1$, and the smallest error is $\tr(\Sigma) - \lambda_1$.

Proof

Step 1: one point. Expand the square and use $\bu\T\bu = 1$: $$\norm{\bx_i - (\bu\T\bx_i)\bu}^2 = \norm{\bx_i}^2 - 2(\bu\T\bx_i)^2 + (\bu\T\bx_i)^2\underbrace{\bu\T\bu}_{=\,1} = \norm{\bx_i}^2 - (\bu\T\bx_i)^2 .$$

Step 2: average. $\frac1n\sum_i\norm{\bx_i}^2 = \sum_j \Sigma_{jj} = \tr(\Sigma)$, and $\frac1n\sum_i(\bu\T\bx_i)^2 = \bu\T\Sigma\bu$ by .

All the components: the eigen-decomposition

One number per point is rarely enough. For a second number, we look for the direction of maximum variance among the directions orthogonal to $\bu_1$, so that it captures something new. Then a third, orthogonal to the first two, and so on. The same Lagrange argument shows that each of these directions is again an eigenvector of $\Sigma$ (). What makes everything fit is the structure of $\Sigma$.

Let $\Sigma$ be symmetric and positive semidefinite. Then:

  1. all its eigenvalues are non-negative;
  2. eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal;
  3. (spectral theorem) $\Sigma$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $\bu_1, \dots, \bu_p$: $$\Sigma = U\Lambda U\T, \qquad U = [\bu_1, \dots, \bu_p], \quad U\T U = I_p, \quad \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p).$$
Proof

Step 1: non-negative eigenvalues. If $\Sigma\bu = \lambda\bu$ with $\bu\T\bu = 1$, then by positive semidefiniteness $$\lambda = \bu\T\Sigma\,\bu \geq 0 .$$

Step 2: orthogonality. Let $\Sigma\bu = \lambda\bu$ and $\Sigma\bv = \mu\bv$ with $\lambda \neq \mu$. Compute $\bv\T\Sigma\bu$ in two ways, using $\Sigma\T = \Sigma$: $$\lambda\,\bv\T\bu = \bv\T(\Sigma\bu) = (\Sigma\bv)\T\bu = \mu\,\bv\T\bu \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda - \mu)\,\bv\T\bu = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bv\T\bu = 0 .$$

Step 3: orthonormal basis. This is the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices from linear algebra. Within an eigenspace of dimension greater than one, we pick an orthonormal basis (Gram–Schmidt); Step 2 makes eigenvectors from different eigenspaces orthogonal.

So the orthogonal directions we are looking for are already provided by linear algebra: PCA is the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix. Order the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_p \geq 0$. By Step 3 of , the variance along $\bu_j$ is $\lambda_j$, and the total variance is the sum of the eigenvalues, $\tr(\Sigma) = \tr(U\Lambda U\T) = \tr(\Lambda U\T U) = \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_p$.

Let $\Sigma = U\Lambda U\T$ with $\lambda_1 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_p$. The columns $\bu_1, \dots, \bu_p$ of $U$ are the principal directions (or loadings). For $k \leq p$, with $U_k = [\bu_1, \dots, \bu_k]$:

  • the principal component scores of $\bx$ are $\bz = U_k\T\bx$, that is $z_j = \bu_j\T\bx$;
  • the rank-$k$ reconstruction is $\hat\bx = U_k\bz = U_kU_k\T\bx$;
  • the explained variance ratio is $$\mathrm{EVR}(k) = \frac{\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k}{\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_p} .$$

Keeping $k$ components keeps the variance $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k$, and the average squared reconstruction error is what is left: $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\bx_i - U_kU_k\T\bx_i}^2 = \lambda_{k+1} + \dots + \lambda_p .$$

Proof

Step 1: Pythagoras with $k$ directions. Complete $\bu_1, \dots, \bu_k$ to the orthonormal basis $\bu_1, \dots, \bu_p$, so $\bx_i = \sum_{j=1}^p (\bu_j\T\bx_i)\bu_j$. The reconstruction keeps the first $k$ terms, so $$\norm{\bx_i - U_kU_k\T\bx_i}^2 = \Big\lVert\sum_{j=k+1}^p (\bu_j\T\bx_i)\,\bu_j\Big\rVert^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^p (\bu_j\T\bx_i)^2 .$$

Step 2: average. By and Step 3 of , $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\bx_i - U_kU_k\T\bx_i}^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^p \bu_j\T\Sigma\,\bu_j = \lambda_{k+1} + \dots + \lambda_p .$$

So the eigenvalues tell the whole story: $\lambda_j$ is the variance along the $j$-th principal direction, the first $k$ directions keep $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_k$, and the error is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues. One can show that no other $k$-dimensional subspace does better. The new coordinates have one more pleasant property.

The principal component scores are uncorrelated, with variances $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$: the covariance matrix of $\bz_i = U_k\T\bx_i$ is $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bz_i\bz_i\T = \Lambda_k = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k).$$

Proof

Step 1: the scores are centred. Since the data are centred (as in ), $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bz_i = U_k\T\underbrace{\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\Big)}_{=\ \mathbf 0} = \mathbf 0 ,$$ so the covariance matrix of the scores is $\frac1n\sum_{i} \bz_i\bz_i\T$.

Step 2: write it in terms of $\Sigma$. Substitute $\bz_i = U_k\T\bx_i$ and take $U_k$ out of the sum: $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bz_i\bz_i\T = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n U_k\T\bx_i\bx_i\T U_k = U_k\T\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\bx_i\T\Big)U_k = U_k\T\Sigma\, U_k . \tag{1}$$

Step 3: compute each entry. The $(j, l)$ entry of (1) is $\bu_j\T\Sigma\bu_l$. Use $\Sigma\bu_l = \lambda_l\bu_l$ and the orthonormality of the eigenvectors: $$\bu_j\T\Sigma\bu_l = \lambda_l\,\underbrace{\bu_j\T\bu_l}_{=\ \delta_{jl}} = \lambda_l\,\delta_{jl} .$$

Step 4: conclude. The off-diagonal entries ($j \neq l$) vanish, so the scores are uncorrelated, and the diagonal entries are the eigenvalues: $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bz_i\bz_i\T = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k) = \Lambda_k .$$

Geometrically, PCA rotates the coordinate system so that its axes line up with the natural axes of the cloud, ordered from the most to the least spread. In the new coordinates the features are uncorrelated.

Computing PCA

In practice PCA is computed through the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the centred data matrix, without forming $\Sigma$. Every $\bX \in \R^{n \times p}$ of rank $r$ can be written $$\bX = A\,S\,V\T, \qquad A \in \R^{n \times r},\ A\T A = I_r, \qquad V \in \R^{p \times r},\ V\T V = I_r, \qquad S = \operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_r),$$ with singular values $s_1 \geq \dots \geq s_r > 0$.

The principal directions are the right singular vectors (the columns $\mathbf v_j$ of $V$), the eigenvalues are $\lambda_j = s_j^2/n$, and the score matrix is $\bX V = AS$.

Proof

Step 1. Substitute the SVD into the covariance and use $A\T A = I_r$: $$\Sigma = \frac1n\bX\T\bX = \frac1n V S\underbrace{A\T A}_{=\,I_r} S V\T = V\,\frac{S^2}{n}\,V\T .$$

Step 2. Multiply by $\mathbf v_j$ and use $V\T\mathbf v_j = \mathbf e_j$: $\Sigma\mathbf v_j = V\frac{S^2}{n}\mathbf e_j = \frac{s_j^2}{n}\mathbf v_j$. The remaining eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ (for directions orthogonal to all $\mathbf v_j$) are $0$.

Step 3. The scores are $\bX V = AS\,V\T V = AS$.

Forming $\bX\T\bX$ squares the condition number and can lose accuracy, so libraries (scikit-learn's PCA included) use the SVD. The cost of the eigen-decomposition of the $p \times p$ matrix $\Sigma$ is of order $p^3$. When there are far more features than examples ($n \ll p$, e.g. images with millions of pixels), it is cheaper to work with the $n \times n$ Gram matrix $\bX\bX\T$.

If $\frac1n\bX\bX\T\mathbf a = \lambda\mathbf a$ with $\lambda > 0$ and $\mathbf a \neq \mathbf 0$, then $\bu = \bX\T\mathbf a / \norm{\bX\T\mathbf a}$ is a unit eigenvector of $\Sigma$ with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$. So the nonzero eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ and of $\frac1n\bX\bX\T$ coincide.

Proof

Step 1: eigenvector. Multiply the eigen-equation on the left by $\bX\T$: $$\Sigma\,(\bX\T\mathbf a) = \frac1n\bX\T\bX\bX\T\mathbf a = \bX\T\Big(\frac1n\bX\bX\T\mathbf a\Big) = \lambda\,(\bX\T\mathbf a).$$

Step 2: it is not zero. $\norm{\bX\T\mathbf a}^2 = \mathbf a\T\bX\bX\T\mathbf a = n\lambda\norm{\mathbf a}^2 > 0$, so we may normalise.

PCA in four steps

  1. Centre each feature (and standardise it if the features have different units).
  2. Compute the SVD $\bX = ASV\T$, or the eigen-decomposition of $\Sigma$.
  3. Choose $k$ from the explained variance ratios $\lambda_j/\sum_l\lambda_l$.
  4. Keep the scores $\bz_i = U_k\T\bx_i$, and reconstruct with $\hat\bx_i = \bar\bx + U_k\bz_i$ if needed (adding back the mean).
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

Xs = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)     # centre and scale each feature
pca = PCA(n_components=2).fit(Xs)
Z = pca.transform(Xs)                      # scores, shape (n, 2)
print(pca.components_)                     # rows = principal directions u_j
print(pca.explained_variance_ratio_)       # lambda_j / sum of all lambdas

Two standardised features have covariance $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.

Step 1: eigenvalues. $\det(\Sigma - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$ gives $2 - \lambda = \pm 1$, so $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.

Step 2: eigenvectors. For $\lambda_1 = 3$: $(\Sigma - 3I)\bu = \mathbf 0$ means $-u_1 + u_2 = 0$, so $\bu_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)\T$. For $\lambda_2 = 1$: $u_1 + u_2 = 0$, so $\bu_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)\T$.

Step 3: explained variance. $\mathrm{EVR}(1) = 3/(3 + 1) = 75\%$. The first component is the (scaled) sum of the two features, which move together. The second, their difference, carries the remaining $25\%$.

Centring and scaling

Why centre?

Without centring, the matrix we diagonalise is the matrix of second moments, not the covariance.

For raw (uncentred) data with mean $\bar\bx$ and covariance $\Sigma$, $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\bx_i\T = \Sigma + \bar\bx\,\bar\bx\T .$$

Proof

Step 1: centre the points. Write $\bx_i = \bar\bx + \mathbf c_i$ with $\sum_i\mathbf c_i = \mathbf 0$, and expand each outer product: $$(\bar\bx + \mathbf c_i)(\bar\bx + \mathbf c_i)\T = \bar\bx\bar\bx\T + \bar\bx\,\mathbf c_i\T + \mathbf c_i\,\bar\bx\T + \mathbf c_i\mathbf c_i\T .$$

Step 2: average; the cross terms vanish. $$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \bx_i\bx_i\T = \bar\bx\bar\bx\T + \bar\bx\underbrace{\Big(\frac1n\sum_i \mathbf c_i\Big)\T}_{=\,\mathbf 0} + \underbrace{\Big(\frac1n\sum_i \mathbf c_i\Big)}_{=\,\mathbf 0}\bar\bx\T + \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf c_i\mathbf c_i\T = \bar\bx\bar\bx\T + \Sigma .$$

The extra term $\bar\bx\bar\bx\T$ adds $\norm{\bar\bx}^2$ of "variance" in the direction of the mean. When the data sit far from the origin, it dominates, and the first "component" just points from the origin towards the cloud. It describes where the cloud is, not its shape. Untick Centre the data in to see it.

Why (usually) standardise?

PCA is driven by variances, so it depends on the units. Measuring area in hundreds instead of thousands of square feet multiplies its values by $10$ and its variance by $100$. The first component then points almost exactly along the area axis, whatever the correlation with price. In , compare the best angle in the three unit settings.

When features have different units or very different ranges, standardise them: divide each centred feature by its standard deviation. The covariance of standardised data is the correlation matrix, so PCA then looks at correlations only. When the features are already on a common scale (pixel intensities, for instance), raw units are fine and often preferable.

Signs and ties

If $\bu$ is a principal direction, so is $-\bu$: two programs can return opposite signs, and a plot of scores can come out mirrored. Only the line matters. If two eigenvalues are equal, any orthonormal basis of their eigenspace is valid, so the individual directions are not determined (nearly equal eigenvalues make them unstable).

How many components?

Keeping too few components discards signal, while keeping too many keeps noise and defeats the purpose. There is no universal rule. Common criteria:

  • Explained variance threshold. Choose the smallest $k$ with $\mathrm{EVR}(k) \geq \tau$, typically $\tau = 0.90$ or $0.95$. The threshold is a convention, not a law.
  • Scree plot and elbow. Plot $\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \dots$ (or the individual ratios). Usually a few large values are followed by a long flat tail; keep the components before the bend ("elbow").
  • Downstream performance. If the components feed a supervised model, treat $k$ as a hyperparameter and choose it by cross-validation (Chapter 3).
  • Interpretability and inspection. For visualisation, $k = 2$ or $3$. For compression, look at reconstructions and stop when they are good enough.

Here the first two components explain about $59\%$ of the variance, $13$ components are needed for $90\%$ and $20$ for $95\%$. The elbow suggests $k = 2$ for a picture of the data, while compression needs more. Different goals, different $k$.

Applications

  • Visualisation. A scatter plot of the first two or three scores is often the first look at a new dataset. It reveals groups, outliers and trends, as in .
  • Compression. Store the mean, $k$ directions and $k$ scores per example: $p + kp + kn$ numbers instead of $np$. For $n = 10\,000$ images with $p = 64$ and $k = 13$ this is about $1.4 \times 10^5$ numbers instead of $6.4 \times 10^5$.
  • Denoising. Reconstructing from the top components discards the small-variance directions, where much of the noise lives.
  • Preprocessing. Regressing on the first few scores instead of the raw features (principal component regression) removes collinearity, because the scores are uncorrelated (). It also speeds up training.
  • Exploration. The loadings $\bu_j$ show which features move together. In , the first component weights both features equally.

Limitations

  • Linearity. PCA finds flat subspaces. Data on a curved surface (a circle, a spiral) need more linear components than their true dimension, and the projection can overlap distant parts. Kernel PCA (Chapter 9) and other non-linear methods address this.
  • Variance is not relevance. PCA ranks directions by spread, not by usefulness for a task. A low-variance direction may be exactly the one that separates two classes (). With labels available, supervised methods may be better.
  • Sensitivity to scaling and centring ().
  • Sensitivity to outliers. Variances are averages of squares, so a few extreme points can rotate the components. Inspect the data, or use robust variants.
  • Interpretability. Each component mixes all features, which can be hard to explain.
  • Global criterion. PCA maximises total variance. Small groups that contribute little variance can be invisible in the first components.

Key takeaways

  • Unsupervised learning looks for structure without labels. PCA looks for a low-dimensional linear subspace that summarises the data.
  • For centred data, the variance along a unit vector $\bu$ is $\bu\T\Sigma\bu$. Maximising it with a Lagrange multiplier gives $\Sigma\bu = \lambda\bu$: the best direction is the top eigenvector, and the maximum variance is $\lambda_1$. The same direction minimises the reconstruction error.
  • $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, so its eigenvectors are orthonormal and its eigenvalues non-negative: PCA is the eigen-decomposition $\Sigma = U\Lambda U\T$. Keeping $k$ components loses exactly $\lambda_{k+1} + \dots + \lambda_p$.
  • The scores are uncorrelated. In practice PCA is computed with the SVD of the centred data matrix, $\lambda_j = s_j^2/n$.
  • Always centre; standardise when units differ. Choose $k$ with the explained variance, the scree plot, or downstream validation.
  • PCA is linear, driven by variance rather than relevance, and sensitive to outliers.

Probabilistic view: Gaussians and probabilistic PCA

PCA was defined without any probability model. Two results show what it means when the data are Gaussian, and how it arises as the limit of a latent variable model.

The axes of a Gaussian cloud

Let $\bx \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma = U\Lambda U\T$ positive definite. Then the principal coordinates $\bz = U\T\bx$ are independent, $z_j \sim \mathcal N(0, \lambda_j)$, and the contours of the density are ellipsoids whose axes point along the eigenvectors $\bu_j$, with semi-axis lengths proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$.

Proof

Step 1: distribution of $\bz$. A linear map of a Gaussian vector is Gaussian, with $$\E[\bz] = U\T\E[\bx] = \mathbf 0, \qquad \Cov(\bz) = U\T\Sigma U = U\T U\Lambda U\T U = \Lambda .$$ A Gaussian vector with diagonal covariance has independent coordinates.

Step 2: contours. The density is a decreasing function of $\bx\T\Sigma^{-1}\bx$. With $\Sigma^{-1} = U\Lambda^{-1}U\T$, $$\bx\T\Sigma^{-1}\bx = \bz\T\Lambda^{-1}\bz = \sum_{j=1}^p \frac{z_j^2}{\lambda_j} .$$ The contour $\sum_j z_j^2/\lambda_j = c^2$ is an ellipsoid in the coordinates $z_j$ along $\bu_j$, with semi-axes $c\sqrt{\lambda_j}$.

So for Gaussian data PCA finds the axes of the ellipsoidal cloud, longest first. Moreover, the principal coordinates are not only uncorrelated (true for any data) but independent.

Probabilistic PCA

Suppose each observation is generated from a low-dimensional latent variable, plus isotropic noise: $$\bz \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I_k), \qquad \bx = \boldsymbol\mu + W\bz + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2 I_p),$$ with $\bz$ and $\boldsymbol\varepsilon$ independent and $W \in \R^{p \times k}$.

Under this model, $\bx \sim \mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu,\ WW\T + \sigma^2 I_p\big)$.

Proof

Step 1. $(\bz, \boldsymbol\varepsilon)$ is a Gaussian vector (independent Gaussian blocks), and $\bx$ is an affine function of it, so $\bx$ is Gaussian.

Step 2: mean. $\E[\bx] = \boldsymbol\mu + W\E[\bz] + \E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon] = \boldsymbol\mu$.

Step 3: covariance. The cross terms vanish by independence: $$\Cov(\bx) = W\underbrace{\Cov(\bz)}_{=\,I_k}W\T + \Cov(\boldsymbol\varepsilon) = WW\T + \sigma^2 I_p .$$

Maximising the likelihood of this model over $\boldsymbol\mu, W, \sigma^2$ can be done in closed form (we state the result without proof): $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = \bar\bx$, $$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{p - k}\sum_{j=k+1}^p \lambda_j, \qquad \hat W = U_k\big(\Lambda_k - \hat\sigma^2 I_k\big)^{1/2}R$$ for any orthogonal $R \in \R^{k \times k}$. The columns of $\hat W$ span exactly the top-$k$ principal subspace, and the noise level is the average of the discarded eigenvalues: what PCA throws away is modelled as noise.

Given $\bx$, the latent variable is Gaussian with mean $$\E[\bz \mid \bx] = M^{-1}W\T(\bx - \boldsymbol\mu), \qquad M = W\T W + \sigma^2 I_k .$$ As $\sigma^2 \to 0$, $W\,\E[\bz \mid \bx] \to W(W\T W)^{-1}W\T(\bx - \boldsymbol\mu)$, the orthogonal projection of $\bx - \boldsymbol\mu$ onto the column space of $W$: classical PCA.

Proof

Step 1: Bayes' rule. Write $\mathbf r = \bx - \boldsymbol\mu$. Up to factors not depending on $\bz$, $$p(\bz \mid \bx) \propto p(\bz)\,p(\bx \mid \bz) \propto \exp\Big(-\tfrac12\norm{\bz}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\mathbf r - W\bz}^2\Big).$$

Step 2: collect the terms in $\bz$. Expanding the square, $$-\tfrac12\norm{\bz}^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\norm{\mathbf r - W\bz}^2 = -\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big(\bz\T\underbrace{(W\T W + \sigma^2 I_k)}_{=\,M}\bz - 2\bz\T W\T\mathbf r\Big) + \text{const}.$$

Step 3: complete the square. With $\mathbf m = M^{-1}W\T\mathbf r$, $$\bz\T M\bz - 2\bz\T M\mathbf m = (\bz - \mathbf m)\T M(\bz - \mathbf m) - \mathbf m\T M\mathbf m .$$ So $\bz \mid \bx \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf m, \sigma^2 M^{-1})$, with mean $\mathbf m$.

Step 4: the limit. As $\sigma^2 \to 0$, $M \to W\T W$ (invertible when $W$ has full column rank), so $W\mathbf m \to W(W\T W)^{-1}W\T\mathbf r$, the projector onto the column space of $W$ applied to $\mathbf r$.

Unlike plain PCA, the probabilistic model has a likelihood. It can compare different $k$ on held-out data, handle missing entries, and be combined with other probabilistic models, for instance mixtures, which relate it to the clustering of the next chapter.
