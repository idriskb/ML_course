Clustering: k-means

PCA looked for continuous structure: a few directions that summarise the data. Clustering looks for discrete structure: groups of points that are close to each other and far from the rest. k-means describes each group by a single point, its centroid, and finds the groups by alternating two simple steps. We derive both steps, prove that the algorithm converges, and see where it fails.

Learning objectives

  • State the k-means objective (within-cluster sum of squares).
  • Derive the optimal centroid of a cluster and the optimal assignment of a point.
  • Describe Lloyd's algorithm and prove that it converges in finitely many steps.
  • Explain why initialisation matters, and how k-means++ helps.
  • Choose the number of clusters with the elbow and silhouette criteria, and recognise the limitations of k-means.

Why cluster?

A shop has data on its customers, say their (standardised) yearly income and spending. Nobody has labelled the customers, yet the shop would like to know whether there are a few typical kinds of customer, to design a different offer for each. This is clustering: partition unlabelled data $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_n \in \R^p$ into groups (clusters) such that points in the same group are similar and points in different groups are not.

In the language of the previous chapter, each point carries a hidden discrete latent variable, the group it belongs to, and clustering tries to recover it. Other examples:

  • grouping handwritten digits by shape without knowing which digit each image shows (in Chapter 5, three such groups were already visible in two principal components);
  • reducing the colours of an image to $K$ representative colours;
  • organising documents by topic.

Clustering is not classification: there are no labels to learn from, and no "correct" answer to compare with. We have to define what a good clustering is. k-means makes the simplest choice.

The k-means objective

Fix the number of clusters $K$. Each cluster $k$ is represented by a centroid $\boldsymbol\mu_k \in \R^p$, and each point is assigned to one cluster. Encode the assignment with indicators $$c_{ik} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \bx_i \text{ is assigned to cluster } k, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad \sum_{k=1}^K c_{ik} = 1 \ \text{ for every } i .$$ Write $C_k = \{i : c_{ik} = 1\}$ for the points of cluster $k$ and $n_k = \sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik} = |C_k|$ for its size.

The k-means objective, or within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), is $$J(c, \boldsymbol\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 .$$ k-means looks for the assignments $c$ and centroids $\boldsymbol\mu_1, \dots, \boldsymbol\mu_K$ that minimise $J$.

Replacing every point by the centroid of its cluster costs $J$ in squared distance: k-means looks for the best summary of the data by $K$ representative points. The objective mixes a discrete unknown (the assignments) and a continuous one (the centroids). The trick is to optimise one while the other is fixed. Both sub-problems have simple, explicit solutions.

The optimal centroids

Suppose the assignments are fixed. Where should the centroids go?

For fixed assignments $c_{ik}$, the objective $$J(c, \boldsymbol\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2$$ is minimised over $\boldsymbol\mu_1, \dots, \boldsymbol\mu_K$ by placing each centroid at the mean of the points assigned to it: $$\boldsymbol\mu_k^\star = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}} = \bar\bx_k, \qquad \text{provided } n_k = \sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik} \geq 1 .$$ This minimiser is unique.

Proof

Step 1: collect the terms that contain $\boldsymbol\mu_k$. Exchange the two sums: $$J = \sum_{k=1}^K \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2}_{=:\ f_k(\boldsymbol\mu_k)} .$$ The centroid $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ appears only in $f_k$ (the $c_{ik}$ are fixed numbers, $0$ or $1$). So we minimise each $f_k$ separately.

Step 2: expand the squared norm. For each $i$, $$\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 = (\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)\T(\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k) = \norm{\bx_i}^2 - 2\,\bx_i\T\boldsymbol\mu_k + \boldsymbol\mu_k\T\boldsymbol\mu_k ,$$ so $$f_k(\boldsymbol\mu_k) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\norm{\bx_i}^2 - 2\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i\Big)\T\boldsymbol\mu_k + \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\Big)\boldsymbol\mu_k\T\boldsymbol\mu_k .$$

Step 3: differentiate with respect to $\boldsymbol\mu_k$. The first sum does not depend on $\boldsymbol\mu_k$; $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\mu}(\mathbf a\T\boldsymbol\mu) = \mathbf a$ and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol\mu}(\boldsymbol\mu\T\boldsymbol\mu) = 2\boldsymbol\mu$. Hence $$\nabla f_k(\boldsymbol\mu_k) = -2\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i + 2\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\Big)\boldsymbol\mu_k = -2\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,(\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k) .$$

Step 4: set the gradient to zero. $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\Big)\boldsymbol\mu_k \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}} ,$$ which is allowed because the denominator $n_k = \sum_i c_{ik}$ (the number of points in cluster $k$) is at least $1$. The numerator adds up exactly the points assigned to cluster $k$, so this is their mean $\bar\bx_k$.

Step 5: it is the global minimum. Differentiating once more, $$\nabla^2 f_k(\boldsymbol\mu_k) = 2\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\Big) I_p = 2\,n_k\,I_p ,$$ which is positive definite. So $f_k$ is strictly convex, and its only stationary point $\bar\bx_k$ is its unique global minimiser.

The next identity says exactly how much a wrong centre costs. It is the same "Pythagoras" decomposition as for the bias–variance trade-off in Chapter 3.

For any $\boldsymbol\mu \in \R^p$, $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2}_{\text{does not depend on } \boldsymbol\mu} + \ n_k\,\norm{\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 .$$ In particular the cost of cluster $k$ is smallest at $\boldsymbol\mu = \bar\bx_k$, and grows quadratically with the distance from the mean.

Proof

Step 1: insert the mean. Write $\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu = (\bx_i - \bar\bx_k) + (\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu)$ and expand: $$\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 = \norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2 + 2\,(\bx_i - \bar\bx_k)\T(\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu) + \norm{\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 .$$

Step 2: multiply by $c_{ik}$ and sum over $i$. The vector $\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu$ does not depend on $i$, so $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2 + 2\,\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}(\bx_i - \bar\bx_k)\Big)\T(\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu) + \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\Big)\norm{\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu}^2 .$$

Step 3: the cross term vanishes. By the definition of $\bar\bx_k$, $$\sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}(\bx_i - \bar\bx_k) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_{ik}\,\bx_i - n_k\,\bar\bx_k = \mathbf 0 ,$$ and the last coefficient is $\sum_i c_{ik} = n_k$.

The within-cluster cost also has a description that does not mention the centroid at all. It measures how spread out the cluster is, through the distances between its points.

For every cluster, $$\sum_{i \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2 = \frac{1}{2n_k}\sum_{i \in C_k}\sum_{j \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \bx_j}^2 .$$ So minimising $J$ makes the points of each cluster close to each other.

Proof

Step 1. Fix $j \in C_k$ and apply with $\boldsymbol\mu = \bx_j$: $$\sum_{i \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \bx_j}^2 = \sum_{i \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2 + n_k\,\norm{\bar\bx_k - \bx_j}^2 .$$

Step 2. Sum over $j \in C_k$. Writing $W_k = \sum_{i \in C_k}\norm{\bx_i - \bar\bx_k}^2$, $$\sum_{j \in C_k}\sum_{i \in C_k} \norm{\bx_i - \bx_j}^2 = n_k\,W_k + n_k\,W_k = 2n_k\,W_k .$$ Divide by $2n_k$.

The optimal assignments

Now fix the centroids. Where should each point go?

For fixed centroids, $J$ is minimised over the assignments by sending each point to its nearest centroid: $$c_{ik} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = \argmin_{j} \norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j}^2, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$ (ties broken by any fixed rule).

Proof

Step 1: one term per point. Group the objective by points: $$J = \sum_{i=1}^n \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K c_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2}_{=:\ g_i(c_{i1}, \dots, c_{iK})} .$$ The term $g_i$ involves only the indicators $c_{i1}, \dots, c_{iK}$ of point $i$, so each $g_i$ can be minimised separately.

Step 2: only one indicator is nonzero. Since exactly one $c_{ik}$ equals $1$, say $c_{im} = 1$, $$g_i = \norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_m}^2 \geq \min_{j} \norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j}^2 ,$$ with equality exactly when $m$ is a nearest centroid. So $c_{ik} = 1$ for the nearest centroid and $0$ for the others.

Lloyd's algorithm

Alternating the two optimal steps gives the standard k-means algorithm.

Lloyd's algorithm

Input: data $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_n$, number of clusters $K$. Initialise the centroids $\boldsymbol\mu_1, \dots, \boldsymbol\mu_K$ (for example $K$ random data points, or k-means++ below). Repeat until the assignments stop changing:

  1. Assignment step. Assign every point to its nearest centroid ().
  2. Update step. Move every centroid to the mean of its points (). If a cluster is empty, keep its centroid (or re-initialise it).

Every step of Lloyd's algorithm leaves $J$ unchanged or decreases it. The algorithm stops after finitely many iterations, at a point where each centroid is the mean of its cluster and each point is assigned to its nearest centroid.

Proof

Step 1: each half-step cannot increase $J$. Let $(c^{(t)}, \boldsymbol\mu^{(t)})$ be the current state. The assignment step chooses the best $c$ for the current centroids, and the update step the best $\boldsymbol\mu$ for the current assignments, so $$J\big(c^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol\mu^{(t)}\big) \leq J\big(c^{(t)}, \boldsymbol\mu^{(t)}\big), \qquad J\big(c^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol\mu^{(t+1)}\big) \leq J\big(c^{(t+1)}, \boldsymbol\mu^{(t)}\big).$$

Step 2: $J$ strictly decreases until the stop. Keep a point's current cluster whenever it is tied for nearest. Then the assignment step changes the assignments only if it strictly lowers some point's distance, hence strictly lowers $J$. Otherwise nothing changes and the algorithm stops.

Step 3: finitely many states. After each update step, the centroids are the means of the current partition, so the state is determined by the partition. There are at most $K^n$ partitions of $n$ points into $K$ groups. Since $J$ strictly decreases until the stop, no partition can occur twice, and the algorithm stops after at most $K^n$ iterations.

Step 4: the final state. When the algorithm stops, the assignments are optimal for the centroids and the centroids are the means of their clusters.

The bound $K^n$ is only a guarantee. In practice a few dozen iterations suffice, and each costs $O(nKp)$ operations (distances from every point to every centroid). The final state is a local minimum in the sense that neither step can improve it. It need not be the global minimum.

The global minimum is hard to find

Minimising $J$ exactly is NP-hard: already for $K = 2$ clusters when the dimension $p$ can be large, and already in the plane ($p = 2$) when the number of clusters $K$ can be large. No efficient algorithm is known for these problems, and none is expected to exist. Lloyd's algorithm is fast but only finds a local minimum, which depends on where it starts.

Initialisation and k-means++

Because $J$ is not convex, the result depends on the starting centroids.

  • Random initialisation. Choose $K$ data points at random as centroids. It is simple but fragile: if two starting centroids fall in the same group, the algorithm can end with one group split in two and two other groups merged.
  • Several restarts. Run the algorithm from several starts and keep the result with the smallest $J$. This is what scikit-learn does (parameter n_init).
  • k-means++. Spread the starting centroids out, by favouring points far from the centroids already chosen.

k-means++ initialisation.

  1. Choose the first centroid uniformly at random among the data points.
  2. For each point, let $D(\bx_i)$ be its distance to the nearest centroid chosen so far. Choose the next centroid among the data points, picking $\bx_i$ with probability $$\frac{D(\bx_i)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^n D(\bx_j)^2} .$$
  3. Repeat step 2 until $K$ centroids are chosen, then run Lloyd's algorithm.

Points that are already well represented have a small chance of being picked, and points in an uncovered region a large one. It can be shown that the expected cost of the initial centroids is at most $8(\ln K + 2)$ times the optimal cost, and in practice it gives better and more stable results than random starts. It is the default in scikit-learn.

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, init="k-means++", n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X)
labels = km.labels_                 # cluster of each point
centroids = km.cluster_centers_     # one row per centroid
print(km.inertia_)                  # the objective J (WCSS)

The geometry of the clusters

Once the centroids are fixed, every location $\bx \in \R^p$ is assigned to its nearest centroid. This splits the space into regions $$R_k = \big\{\bx \in \R^p : \norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_k} \leq \norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_j} \text{ for all } j\big\},$$ the Voronoi cells of the centroids (the shaded regions in ).

The boundary between the cells of $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_j$ is the hyperplane $$2\,(\boldsymbol\mu_j - \boldsymbol\mu_k)\T\bx = \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_j}^2 - \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_k}^2,$$ the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them. Each cell is an intersection of half-spaces, hence convex.

Proof

Step 1: expand both distances. $$\norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 \leq \norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_j}^2 \iff \norm{\bx}^2 - 2\boldsymbol\mu_k\T\bx + \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 \leq \norm{\bx}^2 - 2\boldsymbol\mu_j\T\bx + \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_j}^2 .$$

Step 2: the quadratic terms cancel. Removing $\norm{\bx}^2$ from both sides, $$2\,(\boldsymbol\mu_j - \boldsymbol\mu_k)\T\bx \leq \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_j}^2 - \norm{\boldsymbol\mu_k}^2,$$ a half-space, bounded by the stated hyperplane. It is orthogonal to $\boldsymbol\mu_j - \boldsymbol\mu_k$ and contains the midpoint $\frac12(\boldsymbol\mu_j + \boldsymbol\mu_k)$, so it is the perpendicular bisector.

Step 3: convexity. $R_k$ is the intersection of these half-spaces over $j \neq k$, and an intersection of convex sets is convex.

So k-means always separates clusters by straight boundaries, and its clusters are convex. This is exactly right for compact, roughly round groups of similar size, and wrong for curved or elongated ones.

Choosing the number of clusters

The number $K$ is not learned by the algorithm. And $J$ alone cannot choose it: more clusters always fit better.

Let $J^\star(K)$ be the smallest WCSS achievable with $K$ clusters. Then $J^\star(K + 1) \leq J^\star(K)$, and $J^\star(n) = 0$.

Proof

Take an optimal solution with $K$ centroids and add one more centroid anywhere. Keeping the old assignments gives the same $J$, and reassigning points to their nearest centroid can only lower it. So $J^\star(K + 1) \leq J^\star(K)$. With $n$ clusters, put each point in its own cluster with itself as centroid: $J = 0$.

Common criteria:

  • Elbow method. Plot $J^\star(K)$ against $K$. The curve decreases quickly while new clusters split real groups, then flattens. Choose $K$ at the bend ("elbow"). Simple, but the bend is not always clear.
  • Silhouette score. Combines cohesion and separation (definition below). Choose the $K$ with the highest average silhouette.
  • Davies–Bouldin index. With $\sigma_k$ the average distance of the points of cluster $k$ to $\boldsymbol\mu_k$, $$\mathrm{DB} = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K \max_{j \neq k} \frac{\sigma_k + \sigma_j}{\norm{\boldsymbol\mu_k - \boldsymbol\mu_j}} .$$ Small values mean compact, well-separated clusters. Choose the $K$ that minimises it.
  • The application. A business may want exactly four customer segments; an image may be stored with 16 colours. Often the purpose of the clustering decides.

For a point $\bx_i$ in cluster $C$, let $a(i)$ be its average distance to the other points of $C$, and $b(i)$ the smallest average distance from $\bx_i$ to the points of another cluster. The silhouette of $\bx_i$ is $$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max\{a(i), b(i)\}} \in [-1, 1].$$ Values near $1$ mean $\bx_i$ is much closer to its own cluster than to the next one; values near $0$ mean it lies between two clusters; negative values suggest it is in the wrong cluster. The silhouette score is the average of $s(i)$ over all points.

Applications and limitations

Applications

  • Market segmentation. Group customers with similar behaviour, then describe each group by its centroid ("young, high spending").
  • Vector quantisation and image compression. Cluster the colours of all pixels of an image into $K$ centroids and replace each pixel by its centroid: the image is stored with $K$ colours.
  • Document clustering. Represent documents as vectors and group them by topic.
  • Preprocessing. Use the centroids as features or prototypes, or as a starting point for more flexible models (for instance the mixtures of the extra section).

Limitations

  • Round clusters of similar size. The boundaries are perpendicular bisectors between centroids (), so k-means prefers compact, round clusters of similar extent. Elongated groups get cut across, and a small group next to a large one loses points to it (try the corresponding datasets in ).
  • Non-convex shapes. Two moons or two concentric rings cannot be separated by straight boundaries. Methods based on neighbourhoods or kernels are needed.
  • Outliers. A centroid is a mean, so one extreme point pulls it away from its cluster ( shows a robust alternative).
  • Scaling. Like PCA, k-means uses Euclidean distances: a feature measured in large units dominates. Standardise features with different units.
  • $K$ must be chosen, and the result depends on the initialisation.

PCA and k-means

Both methods summarise unlabelled data, in complementary ways.

PCAk-means
Latent structurecontinuous: a few coordinatesdiscrete: a group label
Summary of $\bx_i$its projection $U_kU_k\T\bx_i$ onto a subspacethe centroid of its cluster
Objectivesquared reconstruction errorsquared distance to the centroid
Solutionexact (eigen-decomposition)local minimum (Lloyd)

Both minimise a squared reconstruction error: PCA with points of a $k$-dimensional subspace, k-means with only $K$ points. They are often combined. Running PCA first removes noisy, low-variance directions and makes distances more meaningful and computation cheaper, as in the digits example of .

Key takeaways

  • k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squares $J = \sum_i\sum_k c_{ik}\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2$.
  • For fixed clusters the optimal centroid is the mean: the gradient $-2\sum_{i} c_{ik}(\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$ vanishes there, and a wrong centre costs an extra $n_k\norm{\bar\bx_k - \boldsymbol\mu}^2$.
  • For fixed centroids the optimal assignment is the nearest centroid.
  • Lloyd's algorithm alternates the two; $J$ never increases and the algorithm stops after finitely many steps, at a local minimum. Use k-means++ and several restarts.
  • Clusters are Voronoi cells with straight boundaries: good for round, similar-sized groups; poor for elongated, unequal or non-convex ones. Standardise features first.
  • Choose $K$ with the elbow, the silhouette, or the application, never with $J$ alone.

Probabilistic view: Gaussian mixtures and EM

k-means makes hard decisions: each point belongs to exactly one cluster. A probabilistic model replaces them by probabilities of membership. k-means then appears as a limiting case.

Gaussian mixture model

Suppose each point is generated in two stages: first a hidden cluster label $z \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ with probabilities $\pi_k = \P(z = k)$, then the point itself around the centre of that cluster, $$\bx \mid z = k \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_k, \sigma^2 I_p\big).$$ Given a point, Bayes' rule gives the probability that it came from cluster $k$, its responsibility $$r_{k}(\bx) = \P(z = k \mid \bx) = \frac{\pi_k\exp\big(-\norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2/2\sigma^2\big)} {\sum_{j=1}^K \pi_j\exp\big(-\norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_j}^2/2\sigma^2\big)}$$ (the Gaussian normalising constants are the same for all clusters and cancel).

Suppose $\bx$ has a unique nearest centroid $\boldsymbol\mu_m$ and all $\pi_k > 0$. As $\sigma \to 0$, $r_m(\bx) \to 1$ and $r_k(\bx) \to 0$ for $k \neq m$: the soft responsibilities become the hard k-means assignment.

Proof

Step 1: divide by the nearest term. Write $d_k = \norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $\pi_m e^{-d_m/2\sigma^2}$, $$r_k(\bx) = \frac{(\pi_k/\pi_m)\,e^{-(d_k - d_m)/2\sigma^2}}{1 + \sum_{j \neq m}(\pi_j/\pi_m)\,e^{-(d_j - d_m)/2\sigma^2}} .$$

Step 2: take the limit. For $j \neq m$, $d_j - d_m > 0$, so $e^{-(d_j - d_m)/2\sigma^2} \to 0$ as $\sigma \to 0$. The denominator tends to $1$, the numerator to $0$ for $k \neq m$ and to $1$ for $k = m$.

The EM algorithm

The parameters are fitted by maximum likelihood with the expectation–maximisation (EM) algorithm, which mirrors Lloyd's two steps:

  • E-step (soft assignment): compute the responsibilities $r_{ik} = r_k(\bx_i)$ for the current parameters.
  • M-step (update): maximise the expected log-likelihood $\sum_i\sum_k r_{ik}\log\big(\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\bx_i \mid \boldsymbol\mu_k, \sigma^2 I)\big)$ over the parameters.

For fixed responsibilities, the M-step for the centres gives the weighted means $$\boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}\,\bx_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}} .$$ With hard responsibilities ($r_{ik} = c_{ik}$) this is the k-means update.

Proof

Step 1: keep the terms that depend on $\boldsymbol\mu_k$. Since $\log\mathcal N(\bx \mid \boldsymbol\mu, \sigma^2 I) = -\norm{\bx - \boldsymbol\mu}^2/2\sigma^2 + \text{const}$, maximising over $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ means minimising $$\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}\,\norm{\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k}^2 .$$

Step 2: set the gradient to zero. As in , $$-2\sum_{i=1}^n r_{ik}(\bx_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k) = \mathbf 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{\sum_i r_{ik}\,\bx_i}{\sum_i r_{ik}},$$ and the Hessian $2\sum_i r_{ik}\,I_p$ is positive definite.

So k-means is the "hard", zero-noise version of EM for a Gaussian mixture with equal spherical covariances. Letting each cluster have its own covariance matrix $\Sigma_k$ gives elliptical clusters of different sizes and orientations, which fixes the failures on elongated and unequal groups (though not on the two moons). Each point also gets a probability of membership instead of a hard label, and the likelihood can be used to compare different $K$.
