k-nearest neighbours

This chapter opens Module 2 of the course: non-linear and non-parametric methods. We first ask what linear models cannot do and what it means to learn without a fixed formula. Then we meet the simplest method of this kind: to predict at a new point, look at the training points closest to it and copy what they do. Simple as it is, k-NN shows every theme of the module: flexibility, the bias–variance trade-off controlled by one number, and the price paid in high dimension.

Learning objectives

  • Explain the limits of global parametric models, and what makes a method non-linear and non-parametric.
  • Define distance-based prediction, and explain why features must be scaled.
  • State the k-NN rules for regression and classification and compute them by hand.
  • Explain how $k$ controls model complexity, derive the bias–variance decomposition of k-NN regression, and choose $k$ by cross-validation.
  • Describe the curse of dimensionality and quantify it with simple calculations.

Beyond global parametric models

Every supervised model of Module 1 had the same shape. We chose a family of functions with a fixed number of parameters, $$f_\theta(\bx) = \bx\T\theta \quad\text{(linear regression)}, \qquad \P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \sigma(\bx\T\theta) \quad\text{(logistic regression)},$$ estimated $\theta \in \R^{p+1}$ from the data, and then threw the data away: all predictions are made with the same $\theta$. Such models are parametric and global: one formula, valid everywhere in the input space.

This works well when the relationship is close to linear, and it has real advantages: few parameters, fast predictions, interpretable coefficients. But it has two limits.

  • The shape must be guessed in advance. A straight line cannot follow a curve. We can add features ($x^2$, $x^3$, $x_1x_2$, …) as in Chapter 1 and Chapter 4, but we must know which ones, and with many inputs the number of possible features explodes.
  • One formula for the whole space. Data often behave differently in different regions: house prices follow different rules in different neighbourhoods, the effect of a drug depends on a combination of a patient's characteristics. A single global formula averages these behaviours away.

We have already met data where this fails: on the two moons, logistic regression can only draw a straight boundary, and k-means can only cut the plane into convex pieces.

The local idea

Recall what we are estimating. In regression, the best prediction of $y$ at $\bx$ (in squared error) is the regression function, the average value of $y$ among all cases with features $\bx$: $$f^\ast(\bx) = \E[\,y \mid \bx\,].$$ In classification, we want the class probabilities $\P(y = c \mid \bx)$. A parametric model guesses a formula for these quantities. But we could also estimate them directly, as averages:

  • we rarely have training points with features exactly equal to $\bx$, so we relax "equal to $\bx$" into "close to $\bx$";
  • and replace the average over all cases by the average over the training points close to $\bx$.

The assumption is only that similar inputs have similar outputs: no formula, no list of features. The prediction rule is then determined by the geometry of the training set itself.

Non-linear and non-parametric methods

  • A method is non-linear if its predictions are not a linear function of the inputs $\bx$ with fixed coefficients.
  • A method is parametric if it summarises the training data by a parameter vector of fixed dimension, which does not grow with the number $n$ of examples. It is non-parametric otherwise: its complexity can grow with $n$, and it often keeps (part of) the training data to make predictions.

Linear regression with $p$ features has $p + 1$ parameters whether $n = 100$ or $n = 100\,000$. A non-parametric method can describe finer and finer detail as more data arrive.

Non-parametric does not mean assumption-free

k-NN assumes that nearby points have similar outputs, which depends entirely on how "nearby" is measured: the choice of features, their scales and the distance. Non-parametric methods also still have hyperparameters (here the number of neighbours $k$) that control their complexity.

The three chapters of this module give three ways of building a flexible predictor, and Module 3 adds a fourth:

  • k-nearest neighbours (this chapter): average the outputs of the closest training points;
  • decision trees: learn a partition of the input space into boxes by asking simple questions;
  • kernel methods: weigh training points by a similarity, or equivalently use implicit, possibly infinite, feature maps;
  • neural networks (Module 3): learn the features themselves. They are parametric, but with so many parameters that they behave like flexible models.

Distance-based prediction

Everything in k-NN rests on a notion of distance between feature vectors $\bx, \bx' \in \R^p$.

For $q \geq 1$, the Minkowski distance of order $q$ is $$d_q(\bx, \bx') = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^p |x_j - x'_j|^q\Big)^{1/q} .$$ The Euclidean distance is $q = 2$, $d_2(\bx, \bx') = \norm{\bx - \bx'}$, the ordinary straight-line distance. The Manhattan distance is $q = 1$, $d_1(\bx, \bx') = \sum_j |x_j - x'_j|$, the distance travelled along a grid of streets.

The points at distance at most $r$ from a query $\bx$ form a ball. For $q = 2$ it is round (a disc in the plane); for $q = 1$ it is a diamond (a square rotated by $45°$). The $k$ nearest neighbours of $\bx$ are the training points inside the smallest such ball that contains $k$ of them, so the distance determines which points count as neighbours. You can switch between the two shapes in .

Scaling comes first

Distances add up differences across features, so the units matter. Take two customers described by (age in years, yearly income in euros): $$\bx = (25,\ 30\,000), \qquad \bx' = (65,\ 31\,000), \qquad \bx'' = (26,\ 34\,000).$$ In Euclidean distance, $d(\bx, \bx') = \sqrt{40^2 + 1000^2} \approx 1001$ and $d(\bx, \bx'') = \sqrt{1^2 + 4000^2} \approx 4000$. So the 25-year-old's nearest neighbour is the 65-year-old: the age difference of 40 years is invisible next to income differences in euros. After standardising each feature (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation), both features count on comparable scales. Standardise numerical features before using k-NN, as for PCA and k-means.

k-NN for regression

Let $\mathcal D = \{(\bx_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ be the training set, with $y_i \in \R$. For a query point $\bx$, let $N_k(\bx) \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$ be the indices of the $k$ training points closest to $\bx$ (ties broken by any fixed rule).

The k-NN regression prediction at $\bx$ is the average output of its $k$ nearest neighbours: $$\hat f_k(\bx) = \frac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} y_i .$$

It is a local average: an estimate of $\E[y \mid \bx]$ that replaces "given $\bx$" by "among the $k$ points closest to $\bx$". Two extreme cases:

  • $k = 1$: $\hat f_1(\bx) = y_{i^\ast}$ where $\bx_{i^\ast}$ is the nearest training point. The predictor copies one point and jumps from value to value.
  • $k = n$: $\hat f_n(\bx) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \bar y$ for every $\bx$. The features are ignored.

One feature, four training points $(x_i, y_i)$: $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(4, 7)$, $(5, 8)$. Predict at $x = 3$ with $k = 2$.

Step 1: distances. $|3 - 1| = 2$, $|3 - 2| = 1$, $|3 - 4| = 1$, $|3 - 5| = 2$.

Step 2: neighbours. The two closest are $x = 2$ and $x = 4$, with outputs $3$ and $7$.

Step 3: average. $\hat f_2(3) = \frac{3 + 7}{2} = 5$. (With $k = 1$ the two candidates are tied, and the tie-breaking rule decides between $3$ and $7$.)

Weighted k-NN. Closer neighbours can be given more influence: $$\hat f(\bx) = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} w_i(\bx)\, y_i}{\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} w_i(\bx)}, \qquad \text{e.g. } w_i(\bx) = \frac{1}{d(\bx, \bx_i) + \varepsilon},$$ with a small $\varepsilon > 0$ to avoid dividing by zero. This smooths the predictions a little and reduces the influence of the $k$-th neighbour when it is much farther than the first.

k-NN for classification

Now $y_i \in \{1, \dots, C\}$. The neighbours vote.

The k-NN class probability estimates at $\bx$ are the class proportions among its neighbours, $$\hat p_c(\bx) = \frac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} \mathbf 1\{y_i = c\}, \qquad c = 1, \dots, C,$$ and the k-NN classifier predicts the majority class, $$\hat y(\bx) = \argmax_{c}\ \hat p_c(\bx) = \argmax_{c}\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} \mathbf 1\{y_i = c\} .$$

With two classes coded $0/1$, $\hat p_1(\bx)$ is the k-NN regression estimate applied to the labels, and the classifier predicts $1$ when $\hat p_1(\bx) > \frac12$. Choosing $k$ odd avoids ties. Weighted voting works as in regression. Unlike logistic regression, whose boundary is a straight line unless we engineer features, the k-NN boundary bends to follow the data.

The geometry of 1-NN. With $k = 1$, each training point owns the region of the plane closer to it than to any other training point: its Voronoi cell, exactly as for the centroids of k-means, but now with one cell per training point. The 1-NN classifier is constant on each cell, and its boundary is made of pieces of perpendicular bisectors between points of different classes. For larger $k$, the regions where the set of $k$ neighbours stays the same are smaller and more intricate, and the predictor is still piecewise constant.

Model complexity and the choice of k

k-NN has no parameter vector to fit, but $k$ plays the role of a complexity knob:

  • small $k$: very local predictions that follow every detail of the training data, including the noise (low bias, high variance);
  • large $k$: averages over large neighbourhoods, smooth and stable predictions that may miss real structure (high bias, low variance).

If the training inputs are distinct, the 1-NN predictor has zero training error, in regression and in classification.

Proof

At a training input $\bx_i$, the nearest training point is $\bx_i$ itself, at distance $0$, and every other point is at positive distance. So $\hat f_1(\bx_i) = y_i$.

Zero training error says nothing about generalisation: it is the interpolation of Chapter 1 again. As in and , the training error grows with $k$, while the test error is U-shaped.

How many parameters does k-NN have?

For least squares with $p + 1$ coefficients, the fitted values are $\hat\by = H\by$ with the hat matrix $H$, and $\tr(H) = p + 1$, the number of parameters. k-NN regression is also linear in $\by$, and the same trace measures its complexity.

Assume the training inputs are distinct. The k-NN fitted values on the training set are $\hat\by = S\by$, where $$S_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1/k & \text{if } j \in N_k(\bx_i), \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \tr(S) = \frac{n}{k} .$$ The effective number of parameters $n/k$ grows with $n$: k-NN is non-parametric.

Proof

Step 1: linear in the outputs. By definition, $\hat y_i = \hat f_k(\bx_i) = \sum_{j=1}^n S_{ij}\,y_j$ with $S_{ij}$ as stated. The neighbourhoods depend only on the inputs, not on $\by$.

Step 2: the diagonal. Each $\bx_i$ is its own nearest neighbour (distance $0$), so $i \in N_k(\bx_i)$ and $S_{ii} = 1/k$. Hence $$\tr(S) = \sum_{i=1}^n S_{ii} = n \cdot \frac1k .$$

So $k = n$ uses one effective parameter (the mean) and $k = 1$ uses $n$ (one per point). With $n$ points in roughly $n/k$ neighbourhoods, k-NN behaves like a model that fits one constant per neighbourhood.

The bias–variance trade-off, exactly

For k-NN regression the decomposition of Chapter 3 can be computed explicitly. Assume a fixed design: the training inputs $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_n$ are fixed, and $$y_i = f(\bx_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \E[\varepsilon_i] = 0, \quad \Var(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2,$$ with independent noise. Fix a query point $\bx_0$, and let $\bx_{(1)}, \dots, \bx_{(k)}$ be its $k$ nearest neighbours (fixed, since the inputs are fixed). A new output at $\bx_0$ is $y_0 = f(\bx_0) + \varepsilon_0$, with fresh noise independent of the training noise.

Under these assumptions, $$\E\big[(y_0 - \hat f_k(\bx_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\Big(f(\bx_0) - \frac1k\sum_{l=1}^k f(\bx_{(l)})\Big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{k}}_{\text{variance}} .$$

Proof

Step 1: split the prediction. Substituting the model, $$\hat f_k(\bx_0) = \frac1k\sum_{l=1}^k y_{(l)} = \underbrace{\frac1k\sum_{l=1}^k f(\bx_{(l)})}_{=:\ \bar f\ \text{(not random)}} + \underbrace{\frac1k\sum_{l=1}^k \varepsilon_{(l)}}_{=:\ \bar\varepsilon} .$$

Step 2: mean and variance of the prediction. $\E[\bar\varepsilon] = 0$, and by independence $$\Var(\bar\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{k^2}\sum_{l=1}^k \Var(\varepsilon_{(l)}) = \frac{1}{k^2}\cdot k\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{k} .$$

Step 3: write the error as a sum of three terms. $$y_0 - \hat f_k(\bx_0) = \varepsilon_0 + \big(f(\bx_0) - \bar f\big) - \bar\varepsilon .$$

Step 4: square and take expectations. The middle term is a constant. The cross terms vanish because $\varepsilon_0$ and $\bar\varepsilon$ have mean zero and are independent of each other: $$\E\big[(y_0 - \hat f_k(\bx_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\E[\varepsilon_0^2]}_{=\,\sigma^2} + \big(f(\bx_0) - \bar f\big)^2 + \underbrace{\E[\bar\varepsilon^2]}_{=\,\sigma^2/k} + \underbrace{2\,\E[\varepsilon_0]\big(f(\bx_0) - \bar f\big) - 2\,\E[\varepsilon_0\bar\varepsilon] - 2\big(f(\bx_0) - \bar f\big)\E[\bar\varepsilon]}_{=\,0} .$$

The formula shows the trade-off term by term:

  • the variance $\sigma^2/k$ decreases as $k$ grows: averaging more noisy outputs cancels more noise;
  • the bias typically increases with $k$: the neighbours $\bx_{(l)}$ get farther from $\bx_0$, and their average $f$-value drifts from $f(\bx_0)$. For $k = 1$ it is small (the nearest neighbour is close); for $k = n$ it is the gap between $f(\bx_0)$ and the average of $f$ over all the data;
  • the noise $\sigma^2$ cannot be reduced by any $k$ (the dotted line in ).

Choosing k

The best $k$ depends on the data, so it is chosen like any hyperparameter: by cross-validation (Chapter 3), on standardised features, never on the test set.

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier())
grid = {"kneighborsclassifier__n_neighbors": range(1, 52, 2),
        "kneighborsclassifier__p": [1, 2]}          # Manhattan or Euclidean
search = GridSearchCV(model, grid, cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
print(search.best_params_, search.score(X_test, y_test))

Putting the scaler inside the pipeline means it is re-fitted on the training folds only, so no information leaks from the validation fold.

The curse of dimensionality

k-NN works when a query point has training points that are genuinely close. In low dimension a moderate sample fills the space well. In high dimension, space is so vast that this fails, even with a lot of data. Three calculations make this precise.

Local neighbourhoods are not small

Let the data be uniform in the unit cube $[0, 1]^p$. A sub-cube that contains on average a fraction $\alpha$ of the data has side length $$e_p(\alpha) = \alpha^{1/p} .$$

Proof

Under the uniform distribution, the fraction of data in a region is its volume. A cube of side $e$ has volume $e^p$, and $e^p = \alpha$ gives $e = \alpha^{1/p}$.

In 20 dimensions, a "neighbourhood" holding only 1% of the data spans 79% of the range of every feature. It is not local any more, and averaging over it introduces a large bias.

The nearest neighbour is far away

Let $n$ points be uniform in the unit ball of $\R^p$, and let the query be the centre. The median distance from the centre to the nearest of the $n$ points is $$d(p, n) = \Big(1 - 2^{-1/n}\Big)^{1/p} .$$

Proof

Step 1: one point. The ball of radius $r$ has volume proportional to $r^p$, so for one uniform point $X$, $$\P(\norm{X} \leq r) = r^p, \qquad \P(\norm{X} > r) = 1 - r^p \qquad (0 \leq r \leq 1).$$

Step 2: the nearest of $n$ independent points. The nearest point is farther than $r$ if and only if all $n$ points are: $$\P\Big(\min_i \norm{X_i} > r\Big) = (1 - r^p)^n .$$

Step 3: the median. Set this probability to $\frac12$: $$(1 - r^p)^n = \tfrac12 \iff 1 - r^p = 2^{-1/n} \iff r = \big(1 - 2^{-1/n}\big)^{1/p} .$$

With $n = 500$ points: $d = 0.001$ for $p = 1$, $0.04$ for $p = 2$, but $0.52$ for $p = 10$. In 10 dimensions the nearest of 500 points is typically more than halfway to the boundary of the ball.

All distances look alike

Let $X, X'$ be independent and uniform in $[0, 1]^p$, and $D = \norm{X - X'}^2$. Then $$\E[D] = \frac{p}{6}, \qquad \Var(D) = \frac{7p}{180}, \qquad \frac{\sqrt{\Var(D)}}{\E[D]} = \frac{6}{\sqrt p}\sqrt{\frac{7}{180}} \approx \frac{1.18}{\sqrt p} \;\xrightarrow[p \to \infty]{}\; 0 .$$ Squared distances grow like $p$, but their spread relative to their size shrinks like $1/\sqrt p$.

Proof

Step 1: a sum of independent terms. $D = \sum_{j=1}^p T_j^2$ with $T_j = X_j - X'_j$, and the $T_j$ are independent with the same distribution as $T = U - U'$ for independent uniform $U, U'$ on $[0, 1]$. So $$\E[D] = p\,\E[T^2], \qquad \Var(D) = p\,\Var(T^2) = p\,\big(\E[T^4] - \E[T^2]^2\big).$$

Step 2: second moment. $\E[T] = 0$ and $\Var(U) = \frac1{12}$, so $\E[T^2] = \Var(U) + \Var(U') = \frac16$.

Step 3: fourth moment. $T$ has the triangular density $1 - |t|$ on $[-1, 1]$, so $$\E[T^4] = 2\int_0^1 t^4(1 - t)\,dt = 2\Big(\frac15 - \frac16\Big) = \frac{1}{15} .$$

Step 4: combine. $\Var(T^2) = \frac1{15} - \frac1{36} = \frac{7}{180}$, so $\E[D] = \frac p6$ and $\Var(D) = \frac{7p}{180}$. The ratio follows.

So in high dimension the distances from a query to all training points are nearly equal: the nearest neighbour is barely nearer than the farthest point, and "nearest" carries little information.

Irrelevant features make it worse. A distance adds up differences over all features. If only 2 of 50 features matter, the 48 irrelevant ones add noise to every distance and can bury the meaningful differences. Feature selection or dimensionality reduction (PCA, Chapter 5) before k-NN can help a lot. The silver lining: real data often lie close to a low-dimensional set inside $\R^p$, and then k-NN behaves as in that lower dimension.

k-NN in practice

  • Preprocessing. Standardise numerical features; remove or down-weight irrelevant ones; consider PCA in high dimension.
  • Choose $k$ (and the distance) by cross-validation. Small $k$ overfits, large $k$ underfits.
  • Choose a meaningful distance. Euclidean is the default; the distance should reflect what "similar" means for the problem.
  • Noise and outliers. With small $k$, a single mislabelled or extreme neighbour can decide the prediction; larger $k$ or distance weighting helps.
  • Computational cost. Training only stores the data (k-NN is a lazy learner). Each prediction computes $n$ distances in $p$ dimensions, $O(np)$ operations, plus memory for the whole training set. Tree-based search structures speed this up in low dimension. Parametric models are the opposite: costly training, cheap predictions.

k-NN works well in low or moderate dimension, with a meaningful distance, enough data to populate neighbourhoods, and outputs that vary smoothly. It struggles in high dimension, with many irrelevant features, or with sparse data.

Connections

  • Linear models are global and interpretable but rigid; k-NN is local and flexible but depends on the geometry and is less interpretable.
  • Kernel methods also predict from similarities, but use all points with weights that decay smoothly with distance (a soft neighbourhood), where k-NN uses exactly $k$ points (a hard neighbourhood).
  • Decision trees also split the space into regions with constant predictions, but learn the regions explicitly by recursive splits, and handle features of different types more naturally.
  • PCA can reduce the dimension first, keeping in mind that it preserves variance, not necessarily what matters for the task.

Key takeaways

  • Parametric models fix a formula with a fixed number of parameters. Non-parametric models let the data shape the predictor, and their complexity can grow with $n$.
  • k-NN estimates $\E[y \mid \bx]$ (regression) or $\P(y = c \mid \bx)$ (classification) by averaging over the $k$ nearest training points. Standardise the features first.
  • $k$ controls complexity: the effective number of parameters is $n/k$; the bias–variance decomposition is noise $+$ bias$^2$ $+\ \sigma^2/k$. Choose $k$ by cross-validation.
  • 1-NN has zero training error; the test error is U-shaped in $k$.
  • In high dimension, neighbourhoods stop being local and distances concentrate: the curse of dimensionality. Reduce or select features.

Probabilistic view: k-NN as an estimate of the Bayes classifier

The best possible classifier predicts $\argmax_c \P(y = c \mid \bx)$ (the Bayes classifier, see the extra section of Chapter 1). k-NN estimates these probabilities without any model, and it can be derived from density estimation.

k-NN from density estimation

Fix $\bx$ and grow a ball around it until it contains $k$ training points, of which $k_c$ belong to class $c$. Let $V$ be its volume, $n_c$ the number of training points of class $c$. If the ball is small, the density is roughly constant inside it, which suggests the estimates $$\hat p(\bx) = \frac{k}{nV}, \qquad \hat p(\bx \mid y = c) = \frac{k_c}{n_c V}, \qquad \hat\P(y = c) = \frac{n_c}{n} .$$

Plugging these estimates into Bayes' rule gives exactly the k-NN probabilities: $$\hat\P(y = c \mid \bx) = \frac{k_c}{k} = \hat p_c(\bx) .$$

Proof

Step 1: Bayes' rule. $$\hat\P(y = c \mid \bx) = \frac{\hat p(\bx \mid y = c)\,\hat\P(y = c)}{\hat p(\bx)} .$$

Step 2: substitute. $$\hat\P(y = c \mid \bx) = \frac{\dfrac{k_c}{n_c V}\cdot\dfrac{n_c}{n}}{\dfrac{k}{nV}} = \frac{k_c}{nV}\cdot\frac{nV}{k} = \frac{k_c}{k} .$$

So the majority vote is the plug-in Bayes classifier built on these local density estimates.

How good can k-NN be?

Two classical results, stated without proof, describe the large-sample behaviour. Let $R^\ast$ be the error rate of the Bayes classifier (the smallest possible).

  • 1-NN is at most twice as bad as the best. As $n \to \infty$, the error rate of the 1-NN classifier converges to a value $R_{1\text{NN}}$ with $R^\ast \leq R_{1\text{NN}} \leq 2R^\ast(1 - R^\ast)$ in two-class problems.
  • Consistency. If $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ (more and more neighbours, but a vanishing fraction of the data), the error of k-NN converges to $R^\ast$ for any distribution of the data.

The second result explains the trade-off: $k \to \infty$ makes the vote reliable (small variance), and $k/n \to 0$ keeps the neighbourhood local (small bias). The curse of dimensionality says how slowly this happens when $p$ is large.

In a two-class problem the Bayes error is $R^\ast = 5\%$. What does the first result say about the large-sample error of 1-NN? Why can 1-NN not reach $R^\ast$ even with infinite data? (Hint: the single nearest neighbour's label is itself random.)
