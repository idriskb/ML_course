Tree-based methods

k-NN predicted with regions defined implicitly by distances. Decision trees learn the regions explicitly, by asking a sequence of simple yes/no questions about the features. A single tree is easy to read but unstable. Averaging many trees (bagging, random forests) or adding them one after the other (boosting) turns it into some of the most accurate predictors for tabular data.

Learning objectives

  • Describe a decision tree as a recursive partition of the input space, and derive its leaf predictions.
  • Compute impurity measures (variance, Gini, entropy) and choose a split by hand.
  • Control the complexity of a tree with its depth, leaf size and cost-complexity pruning.
  • Explain why averaging reduces variance, and how bagging and random forests exploit it; use the out-of-bag error and variable importance.
  • Describe gradient boosting as gradient descent in function space, and compare it with random forests.

Why trees?

Much real data is tabular: each row is an individual (a customer, a patient, a house), each column a feature, often of mixed types (age, income, country, education level). For such data we would like a model that

  • captures non-linear effects and interactions ("young and high income") without hand-crafted features;
  • does not depend on the units of the features;
  • can be explained as rules.

Decision trees do all three. Like k-NN, they predict with a constant in each region of the input space. Unlike k-NN, the regions are learned: they are boxes chosen by the data, and they are described by a few questions of the form "is feature $j$ at most $t$?".

Decision trees for regression and classification

Training data are $(\bx_i, y_i)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, with $\bx_i = (x_{i1}, \dots, x_{ip})$. A binary decision tree is built from tests of the form $$x_j \leq t \quad (\text{go left}) \qquad\text{or}\qquad x_j > t \quad (\text{go right}),$$ for a feature index $j$ and a threshold $t$. Starting from the root with all the data, each internal node sends its points to two children. The leaves define disjoint regions $R_1, \dots, R_M$ that together cover the input space. To predict at a new $\bx$, send it down the tree to its leaf.

Write $R_m$ also for the set of training indices $i$ with $\bx_i$ in region $m$, and $n_m = |R_m|$.

  • Regression tree: for $\bx \in R_m$, predict the mean output of the region, $$\hat f(\bx) = \bar y_m = \frac{1}{n_m}\sum_{i \in R_m} y_i .$$
  • Classification tree with classes $c = 1, \dots, K$: estimate the class proportions of the region and predict the most frequent class, $$\hat\pi_{mc} = \frac{1}{n_m}\sum_{i \in R_m} \mathbf 1\{y_i = c\}, \qquad \hat y(\bx) = \argmax_c\ \hat\pi_{mc} .$$

A tree is therefore a piecewise-constant function: $\hat f(\bx) = \sum_{m=1}^M \bar y_m \mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_m\}$. These leaf values are the best possible constants for the training data.

Given the regions, the leaf mean $\bar y_m$ minimises the training squared error $\sum_{i \in R_m}(y_i - c)^2$ over constants $c$, and the majority class minimises the number of misclassified training points in the leaf.

Proof

Step 1: squared error. $g(c) = \sum_{i \in R_m}(y_i - c)^2$ is a convex parabola with $$g'(c) = -2\sum_{i \in R_m}(y_i - c) = -2\Big(\sum_{i \in R_m} y_i - n_m c\Big) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad c = \bar y_m .$$ (This is the one-dimensional case of the optimal centroid of Chapter 6.)

Step 2: misclassification. Predicting class $c$ in the leaf misclassifies $n_m - n_m\hat\pi_{mc} = n_m(1 - \hat\pi_{mc})$ training points, which is smallest when $\hat\pi_{mc}$ is largest.

Features enter only through comparisons $x_j \leq t$. This has a useful consequence.

If every feature is transformed by a strictly increasing function (for example a change of units, or a logarithm), the tree grown by the algorithm below splits the training points in exactly the same way, with transformed thresholds. In particular, trees do not need standardised features.

Proof

Step 1: the order of the values is preserved. If $\phi$ is strictly increasing, then $$x_{ij} \leq x_{kj} \iff \phi(x_{ij}) \leq \phi(x_{kj}) .$$

Step 2: the candidate splits are the same. A split $x_j \leq t$ only separates the points of a node into those below and above a threshold, so the possible splits of the training points are the same before and after transforming: only the ordering of each feature matters.

Step 3: the chosen splits are the same. Identical splits give identical children, hence identical impurities, so the algorithm makes the same choices. The threshold $t$ between two consecutive values becomes a threshold between their images.

Contrast this with k-NN and k-means, where changing units changes the distances and hence the answer.

Choosing the splits

A good split produces children that are as homogeneous (pure) as possible in the target. Homogeneity is measured by an impurity $I(R)$ of a node; the quality of a split of $R$ into $R_L$ and $R_R$ is the impurity decrease $$\Delta I = I(R) - \Big(\frac{n_L}{n_L + n_R}\, I(R_L) + \frac{n_R}{n_L + n_R}\, I(R_R)\Big),$$ the parent impurity minus the size-weighted average impurity of the children ($n_L$, $n_R$ are the numbers of points sent left and right).

Regression: variance reduction

For regression, the impurity of a node is the variance of its outputs, $$Q(R) = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{i \in R}(y_i - \bar y_R)^2 .$$

Let a node $R$ with $|R|$ points be split into $R_L$ and $R_R$. Then $$|R|\cdot\Delta Q = \mathrm{SSE}(R) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_L) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_R) \;\geq\; 0, \qquad \mathrm{SSE}(A) = \sum_{i \in A}(y_i - \bar y_A)^2 .$$ So the best split by variance reduction is the one that most decreases the training squared error, and no split can increase it.

Proof

Step 1: impurity times size is the SSE. By definition $|A|\,Q(A) = \mathrm{SSE}(A)$, so $$|R|\cdot\Delta Q = |R|\,Q(R) - |R_L|\,Q(R_L) - |R_R|\,Q(R_R) = \mathrm{SSE}(R) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_L) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_R) .$$

Step 2: non-negative. Split the parent's sum into the two children. In each child, the parent mean $\bar y_R$ is just some constant, and the child mean is the best constant (): $$\mathrm{SSE}(R) = \sum_{i \in R_L}(y_i - \bar y_R)^2 + \sum_{i \in R_R}(y_i - \bar y_R)^2 \;\geq\; \sum_{i \in R_L}(y_i - \bar y_{R_L})^2 + \sum_{i \in R_R}(y_i - \bar y_{R_R})^2 ,$$ where $\bar y_{R_L}$ and $\bar y_{R_R}$ are the child means. The right-hand side is $\mathrm{SSE}(R_L) + \mathrm{SSE}(R_R)$.

Classification: Gini and entropy

For classification, the impurity is a function of the class proportions $\pi_c = \hat\pi_{mc}$ of the node:

  • Gini impurity: $G = \sum_{c=1}^K \pi_c(1 - \pi_c) = 1 - \sum_{c=1}^K \pi_c^2$.
  • Entropy: $H = -\sum_{c=1}^K \pi_c\log_2\pi_c$ (with $0\log 0 = 0$), the quantity behind the cross-entropy loss of Chapter 4.
  • Misclassification rate: $E = 1 - \max_c \pi_c$.

The decrease of Gini or entropy is called the information gain of the split.

Gini impurity is $0$ if and only if the node is pure (one class only), and it is largest, equal to $1 - \frac1K$, when all classes are equally frequent. The same holds for entropy, with maximum $\log_2 K$.

Proof

Step 1: zero. Each term $\pi_c(1 - \pi_c)$ is non-negative, and it is zero only if $\pi_c \in \{0, 1\}$. All terms vanish exactly when one $\pi_c = 1$.

Step 2: maximum. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $(\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$ and $(1, \dots, 1)$, $$1 = \Big(\sum_{c=1}^K \pi_c\cdot 1\Big)^2 \leq K\sum_{c=1}^K \pi_c^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad G = 1 - \sum_c\pi_c^2 \leq 1 - \frac1K ,$$ with equality when all $\pi_c$ are equal. (For entropy, the bound $\log_2 K$ follows from $\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\|\, \text{uniform}) \geq 0$, as in Chapter 4.)

The Gini gain and the entropy gain of any split are non-negative.

Proof

Step 1: the parent is a mixture of its children. Let $w_L = n_L/n$ and $w_R = n_R/n$ be the fractions of the parent's $n$ points sent left and right, and $\boldsymbol\pi^L, \boldsymbol\pi^R$ the children's class proportions. Counting the points of class $c$, $$\boldsymbol\pi = w_L\,\boldsymbol\pi^L + w_R\,\boldsymbol\pi^R .$$

Step 2: both impurities are concave. $G(\boldsymbol\pi) = 1 - \sum_c \pi_c^2$ is a sum of concave functions $-\pi_c^2$ (plus a constant), and $H$ is a sum of $-\pi_c\log\pi_c$, also concave (second derivative $-1/\pi_c$).

Step 3: Jensen's inequality. For a concave $I$ and weights $w_L + w_R = 1$, $$I(\boldsymbol\pi) = I\big(w_L\boldsymbol\pi^L + w_R\boldsymbol\pi^R\big) \geq w_L\,I(\boldsymbol\pi^L) + w_R\,I(\boldsymbol\pi^R),$$ which says exactly $\Delta I \geq 0$.

Why not use the misclassification rate? A node has 400 points of each class. Compare split A, into $(300, 100)$ and $(100, 300)$, with split B, into $(200, 400)$ and $(200, 0)$.

Step 1: misclassification. Split A misclassifies $100 + 100 = 200$ points; split B misclassifies $200 + 0 = 200$ points. Both have error $25\%$: the criterion cannot tell them apart.

Step 2: Gini. Parent: $G = 1 - 0.5^2 - 0.5^2 = 0.5$. Split A: each child has $G = 1 - 0.75^2 - 0.25^2 = 0.375$, so the weighted impurity is $0.375$. Split B: the left child has $G = 1 - (\frac13)^2 - (\frac23)^2 = \frac49 \approx 0.444$ and weight $\frac{600}{800}$; the right child is pure. Weighted impurity: $0.75 \times 0.444 = 0.333$.

Step 3: conclusion. Gini prefers split B, which creates a pure node that needs no further splitting. Gini and entropy reward purity more than the error rate does (they are strictly concave), which makes the greedy growth work better. They usually give very similar trees; Gini is the default in scikit-learn.

Growing a tree greedily

Finding the tree with the smallest training error among all trees of a given size is computationally intractable (NP-hard). Trees are therefore grown greedily, top-down (recursive binary splitting):

Recursive binary splitting

  1. Start with one node containing all training points.
  2. For the current node, for every feature $j$ and every threshold $t$ (midpoints between consecutive distinct values of $x_j$ in the node), compute the impurity decrease.
  3. Split with the best pair $(j^\ast, t^\ast)$, sending each point left or right.
  4. Repeat on each child until a stopping rule holds: maximum depth reached, too few points to split, node pure, or no split decreases the impurity enough.

Sorting each feature once per node gives a cost of order $p\,n\log n$ per level of the tree, so growing a tree is fast. But the procedure is myopic: a split that looks useless now (for example on the XOR pattern) may be essential later, and the final tree need not be the best possible one.

Overfitting, depth and pruning

Splitting further never increases the training error (, ). If we keep going until every leaf is pure, the training error is zero, as for 1-NN. A tree of depth $d$ has up to $2^d$ leaves, so depth is a direct measure of complexity:

  • shallow trees (few large regions): coarse approximations, high bias, low variance;
  • deep trees (many small regions, few points per leaf): low bias but high variance. Moving a few training points can change an early split, and then the whole tree below it changes.

Pre-pruning: stop early

The simplest regularisation constrains the growth:

  • maximum depth (a depth-1 tree is a stump);
  • minimum number of points per leaf, so that no prediction rests on one or two points;
  • minimum impurity decrease for a split to be made.

Like $\lambda$ in ridge regression or $k$ in k-NN, these hyperparameters are chosen by cross-validation.

Post-pruning: cost-complexity

Stopping early can miss a useful split that comes after a useless one. Instead, grow a large tree $T_0$, then cut it back. For a tree $T$ with $|T|$ leaves and training error $R(T)$ (the total squared error, or number of misclassifications, over the leaves), define the cost-complexity $$C_\alpha(T) = R(T) + \alpha\,|T|, \qquad \alpha \geq 0 .$$ The penalty $\alpha$ is a price per leaf, as $\lambda$ was a price for large coefficients in Chapter 3. For each $\alpha$ we keep the subtree of $T_0$ with the smallest $C_\alpha$. Which branches go first?

Let $t$ be an internal node of $T$, $T_t$ the branch below it (with $|T_t|$ leaves), $R(T_t)$ the training error of its leaves and $R(t)$ the training error if $t$ were a single leaf. Replacing the branch by a leaf does not increase $C_\alpha$ if and only if $$\alpha \;\geq\; g(t) = \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{|T_t| - 1} .$$

Proof

Step 1: what changes. Only the part of the tree below $t$ changes. Keeping the branch contributes $R(T_t) + \alpha|T_t|$ to $C_\alpha$; collapsing it into one leaf contributes $R(t) + \alpha$.

Step 2: compare. Collapsing is at least as good when $$R(t) + \alpha \leq R(T_t) + \alpha\,|T_t| \iff R(t) - R(T_t) \leq \alpha\big(|T_t| - 1\big) \iff \alpha \geq \frac{R(t) - R(T_t)}{|T_t| - 1},$$ using $|T_t| \geq 2$ for an internal node.

$g(t)$ is the decrease of training error per extra leaf that the branch buys. Weakest-link pruning repeatedly collapses the node with the smallest $g(t)$, producing a nested sequence of subtrees $T_0 \supset T_1 \supset \dots \supset \{\text{root}\}$ as $\alpha$ increases. Cross-validation then chooses $\alpha$ (in scikit-learn, the parameter ccp_alpha).

Interpretability and limitations

A small tree is a short list of if–then rules, easy to communicate and to check against domain knowledge (see the rules printed in ). But:

  • instability: trees have high variance, and a different sample can give a very different tree (and different rules);
  • axis-aligned splits: a boundary like $x_1 + x_2 = 0$ is approximated by a staircase;
  • piecewise-constant predictions: rough, and constant outside the range of the training data;
  • a deep tree with hundreds of leaves is no longer interpretable.

The rest of the chapter fixes the first problem, at the cost of the interpretability of a single tree, by combining many trees.

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier, export_text

tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, min_samples_leaf=5, criterion="gini").fit(X_train, y_train)
print(export_text(tree, feature_names=list(feature_names)))   # the tree as rules
print(tree.score(X_test, y_test))

Ensembles I: averaging trees

Deep trees have low bias and high variance. Averaging reduces variance, provided the averaged predictors are not too similar.

Let $\hat f^{(1)}, \dots, \hat f^{(B)}$ be predictions at a point, each with variance $\tau^2$, with pairwise correlation $\rho$. Their average has variance $$\Var\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \hat f^{(b)}\Big) = \rho\,\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\tau^2 .$$

Proof

Step 1: expand the variance of the sum. $$\Var\Big(\sum_{b=1}^B \hat f^{(b)}\Big) = \sum_{b=1}^B \Var\big(\hat f^{(b)}\big) + \sum_{b \neq b'} \Cov\big(\hat f^{(b)}, \hat f^{(b')}\big) = B\tau^2 + B(B - 1)\,\rho\tau^2 .$$

Step 2: divide by $B^2$. $$\frac{B\tau^2 + B(B - 1)\rho\tau^2}{B^2} = \frac{\tau^2}{B} + \rho\tau^2 - \frac{\rho\tau^2}{B} = \rho\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\tau^2 .$$

The second term vanishes as $B$ grows, but the first one does not: averaging can only remove the uncorrelated part of the variance. To gain a lot, the trees must be both individually flexible and weakly correlated.

The bootstrap and bagging

We have only one training set. The bootstrap creates new ones by resampling it: a bootstrap sample $\mathcal D^{(b)}$ consists of $n$ points drawn with replacement from the $n$ training points. Some points appear several times, others not at all.

The probability that a given training point is left out of a bootstrap sample is $$\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big)^n \;\xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{}\; e^{-1} \approx 0.368 .$$ So each bootstrap sample contains about $63\%$ of the distinct training points.

Proof

Step 1. Each of the $n$ independent draws misses the given point with probability $1 - \frac1n$, so all $n$ draws miss it with probability $(1 - \frac1n)^n$.

Step 2. $\log(1 - \frac1n)^n = n\log(1 - \frac1n) = n\big(-\frac1n - \frac{1}{2n^2} - \dots\big) \to -1$, so $(1 - \frac1n)^n \to e^{-1}$. (Already for $n = 100$ the value is $0.366$.)

Bagging (bootstrap aggregating): draw $B$ bootstrap samples, grow a deep tree $\hat f^{(b)}$ on each, and average, $$\hat f_{\text{bag}}(\bx) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \hat f^{(b)}(\bx),$$ averaging the predicted class probabilities (or taking a majority vote) for classification.

Out-of-bag (OOB) error. Each training point is left out of about $37\%$ of the bootstrap samples, so roughly $0.37B$ trees never saw it. Averaging only those trees gives an honest prediction for that point: $$\hat f_{\text{OOB}}(\bx_i) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal B_i|}\sum_{b \in \mathcal B_i}\hat f^{(b)}(\bx_i), \qquad \mathcal B_i = \{b : i \notin \mathcal D^{(b)}\}.$$ The average loss of these predictions, the OOB error, estimates the test error for free, without a validation set.

Random forests: decorrelating the trees

Bagged trees are often strongly correlated: if one feature is very predictive, almost every tree splits on it first, and the trees look alike. By , a large $\rho$ limits what averaging can achieve. Random forests add a second source of randomness.

A random forest is bagging of deep trees in which, at every split, only a random subset of $m$ of the $p$ features is considered (drawn anew at each node). Common defaults are $m = \sqrt p$ for classification and $m = p/3$ for regression; $m = p$ gives plain bagging.

Strong features are now absent from many splits, so different trees use different structures: $\rho$ drops, while each tree stays flexible. Random forests need little tuning (the number of trees, $m$, and the leaf size), handle mixed features, and are robust; they are a strong default for tabular data. Adding trees does not cause overfitting: it only reduces the variance term of and stabilises after a few hundred trees.

Variable importance

A forest of hundreds of trees cannot be read as rules, but it can tell us which features matter.

  • Impurity importance: for each feature $j$, add up the impurity decreases (weighted by the number of points) of all splits on $j$, over all trees, and normalise. It is cheap, but biased towards features with many possible split points.
  • Permutation importance: randomly shuffle the values of feature $j$ (on OOB or validation data), which breaks its link with the target, and measure how much the error increases. It applies to any model. With strongly correlated features both measures can be misleading, since one feature can stand in for another.
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

rf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=500, max_features="sqrt", oob_score=True, random_state=0)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(rf.oob_score_)                       # out-of-bag accuracy
print(rf.feature_importances_)             # impurity importance
print(permutation_importance(rf, X_test, y_test, n_repeats=10).importances_mean)

Ensembles II: boosting

Bagging and random forests grow deep trees independently and average them, which reduces variance. Boosting grows small trees sequentially, each one correcting the errors of the current ensemble, which mainly reduces bias. The model is an additive expansion $$f_M(\bx) = f_0 + \sum_{m=1}^M \nu\,h_m(\bx),$$ where each $h_m$ is a shallow tree (a "weak learner") and $0 \lt \nu \leq 1$ is a learning rate.

Regression: fitting the residuals

With the squared loss $L(y, f) = \frac12(y - f)^2$, the derivative with respect to the prediction is $$\frac{\partial L(y_i, f)}{\partial f}\Big|_{f = f_{m-1}(\bx_i)} = -\big(y_i - f_{m-1}(\bx_i)\big),$$ so the negative gradient at each training point is the current residual $r_i^{(m)} = y_i - f_{m-1}(\bx_i)$. Boosting fits the next tree to these residuals.

Gradient boosting for regression (squared loss)

  1. Start with a constant: $f_0(\bx) = \bar y$.
  2. For $m = 1, \dots, M$:
    1. compute the residuals $r_i^{(m)} = y_i - f_{m-1}(\bx_i)$;
    2. fit a small regression tree $h_m$ to the data $(\bx_i, r_i^{(m)})$;
    3. update $f_m = f_{m-1} + \nu\,h_m$.

Let $\mathbf r = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$ be the current residuals and $\mathbf h = (h_m(\bx_1), \dots, h_m(\bx_n))$ the fitted values of a regression tree fitted to them (leaf means of the residuals). For $0 \lt \nu \leq 1$, the new training error is $$\sum_{i=1}^n \big(r_i - \nu h_i\big)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2 - \nu(2 - \nu)\sum_{i=1}^n h_i^2 \;\leq\; \sum_{i=1}^n r_i^2 .$$ Each stage decreases the training error, by more when the tree captures more of the residuals.

Proof

Step 1: expand. $$\sum_i (r_i - \nu h_i)^2 = \sum_i r_i^2 - 2\nu\sum_i r_i h_i + \nu^2\sum_i h_i^2 .$$

Step 2: the cross term. In leaf $\ell$ of the tree, $h_i = \bar r_\ell$ for every point of the leaf, so $$\sum_{i \in \ell} r_i h_i = \bar r_\ell\sum_{i \in \ell} r_i = n_\ell\,\bar r_\ell^{\,2} = \sum_{i \in \ell} h_i^2 .$$ Summing over the leaves, $\sum_i r_i h_i = \sum_i h_i^2$.

Step 3: combine. $\sum_i (r_i - \nu h_i)^2 = \sum_i r_i^2 - (2\nu - \nu^2)\sum_i h_i^2$, and $2\nu - \nu^2 = \nu(2 - \nu) > 0$ for $0 \lt \nu \leq 1$.

The training error therefore keeps decreasing as trees are added, and eventually the model fits the noise: unlike a random forest, boosting can overfit when $M$ is too large.

Gradient boosting for any loss

The same recipe works for any differentiable loss. At stage $m$, compute the pseudo-residuals $$r_i^{(m)} = -\frac{\partial L(y_i, f)}{\partial f}\Big|_{f = f_{m-1}(\bx_i)} ,$$ fit a regression tree $h_m$ to them, and add $\nu\,h_m$ (optionally choosing the step by a line search, $\gamma_m = \argmin_\gamma \sum_i L(y_i, f_{m-1}(\bx_i) + \gamma h_m(\bx_i))$). This is gradient descent in function space: the vector of predictions $(f(\bx_1), \dots, f(\bx_n))$ moves along the negative gradient of the training loss, and the tree generalises the step to new inputs.

For binary classification with $y \in \{0, 1\}$, let $f$ be a log-odds and $p = \sigma(f)$. For the cross-entropy loss $L(y, f) = -y\log\sigma(f) - (1 - y)\log(1 - \sigma(f))$, the pseudo-residuals are $$r_i = y_i - \sigma\big(f_{m-1}(\bx_i)\big) = y_i - p_i ,$$ the observed label minus the current predicted probability.

Proof

Step 1. Use $\sigma'(f) = \sigma(f)(1 - \sigma(f))$ (Chapter 4), so $\frac{d}{df}\log\sigma(f) = 1 - \sigma(f)$ and $\frac{d}{df}\log(1 - \sigma(f)) = -\sigma(f)$.

Step 2. $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial f} = -y\big(1 - \sigma(f)\big) + (1 - y)\,\sigma(f) = \sigma(f) - y .$$ The negative gradient is $y - \sigma(f)$. This is the same "predicted minus observed" gradient as in logistic regression.

Regularising boosting

  • Learning rate (shrinkage) $\nu$: small values ($0.01$ to $0.1$) make each step cautious; they need more trees but generalise better.
  • Number of trees $M$: too few underfit, too many overfit. Choose it by monitoring a validation set and stopping when the error stops improving (early stopping).
  • Tree depth: shallow trees (depth 1 to 6). Depth $d$ allows interactions between up to $d$ features.
  • Subsampling: fit each tree on a random fraction of the rows (stochastic gradient boosting), which adds randomness, reduces variance and speeds up training.
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier

gb = HistGradientBoostingClassifier(learning_rate=0.05, max_iter=1000, max_depth=3,
                                    early_stopping=True, validation_fraction=0.1, random_state=0)
gb.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(gb.n_iter_, gb.score(X_test, y_test))   # trees actually used, test accuracy

Random forests or boosting? Practical guidance

Random forestGradient boosting
Treesdeep, grown independently (in parallel)shallow, grown sequentially
Main effectreduces variancereduces bias (and variance with shrinkage)
More treesnever hurts, curve flattenscan overfit: use early stopping
Tuninglittle; robust defaultslearning rate, $M$, depth: needs care
Accuracystrong baselineoften the best on tabular data when tuned

A reasonable workflow for a new tabular problem:

  1. fit a regularised linear or logistic regression as an interpretable baseline;
  2. fit a random forest with default settings and check its OOB or validation error;
  3. look at the variable importances;
  4. if more accuracy is needed, tune gradient boosting with a small learning rate and early stopping;
  5. compare all models on the same held-out test set, once, at the end.

Trees are less suited to images, sound or text, where neural networks (Chapter 10) exploit the structure of the data, and to problems where a linear effect must be estimated and explained. Single trees extrapolate as constants outside the training range.

Key takeaways

  • A tree partitions the input space into boxes by tests $x_j \leq t$ and predicts the leaf mean or majority class, the best constants for the training data. Trees are insensitive to monotone feature transformations.
  • Splits are chosen greedily to maximise the impurity decrease: variance (= SSE decrease) for regression, Gini or entropy for classification; both gains are non-negative.
  • Depth controls complexity. Regularise with maximum depth, minimum leaf size, or cost-complexity pruning $R(T) + \alpha|T|$, with $\alpha$ chosen by cross-validation.
  • Averaging $B$ predictors with correlation $\rho$ leaves variance $\rho\tau^2 + (1 - \rho)\tau^2/B$. Bagging averages bootstrap trees; random forests also subsample features to lower $\rho$. The OOB error estimates the test error for free.
  • Gradient boosting adds small trees fitted to the negative gradient (residuals, or $y - p$ for classification). Tune $\nu$, $M$ (early stopping) and depth.

Probabilistic view: likelihood behind the criteria

The splitting criteria and the boosting losses can be read as maximum likelihood for simple probability models.

Entropy splits maximise a likelihood

Model a classification tree probabilistically: inside leaf $m$, labels are independent draws from a categorical distribution with probabilities $q_{m1}, \dots, q_{mK}$. Let $n_{mc}$ be the number of training points of class $c$ in leaf $m$.

The maximum-likelihood estimates are the leaf proportions $\hat q_{mc} = \hat\pi_{mc} = n_{mc}/n_m$, and the maximised log-likelihood is $$\max_q \log\mathcal L = \sum_m\sum_c n_{mc}\log\hat\pi_{mc} = -\sum_m n_m\,H_e(\hat{\boldsymbol\pi}_m),$$ where $H_e$ is the entropy with natural logarithms. So the entropy decrease of a split, weighted by the node size, is exactly the increase of the maximised log-likelihood.

Proof

Step 1: the likelihood separates over leaves. $\log\mathcal L = \sum_m\sum_c n_{mc}\log q_{mc}$, and each leaf can be maximised separately subject to $\sum_c q_{mc} = 1$.

Step 2: one leaf. With $\hat\pi_c = n_{mc}/n_m$, for any $q$, $$\sum_c n_{mc}\log q_c - \sum_c n_{mc}\log\hat\pi_c = -n_m\sum_c\hat\pi_c\log\frac{\hat\pi_c}{q_c} = -n_m\,\mathrm{KL}(\hat{\boldsymbol\pi}\,\|\,\mathbf q) \leq 0,$$ with equality if and only if $\mathbf q = \hat{\boldsymbol\pi}$ (Chapter 4, extra section).

Step 3: the maximum. $\sum_c n_{mc}\log\hat\pi_{mc} = n_m\sum_c\hat\pi_{mc}\log\hat\pi_{mc} = -n_m H_e(\hat{\boldsymbol\pi}_m)$.

Similarly, a regression tree with Gaussian noise of common variance in every leaf has a log-likelihood that is a decreasing function of the total SSE, so variance reduction is maximum likelihood too. Gini has its own interpretation: $\sum_c\pi_c(1 - \pi_c)$ is the probability that two labels drawn at random from the node differ, i.e. the error rate of a rule that guesses a label at random according to the node's proportions.

What boosting estimates

For a fixed $\bx$ with $p = \P(y = 1 \mid \bx)$, the expected cross-entropy $\E\big[L(y, f) \mid \bx\big] = -p\log\sigma(f) - (1 - p)\log(1 - \sigma(f))$ is minimised at $$f^\ast(\bx) = \log\frac{p}{1 - p},$$ the log-odds. So boosted trees with this loss estimate the log-odds, and $\sigma(f_M(\bx))$ estimates the probability $\P(y = 1 \mid \bx)$.

Proof

Step 1: derivative. As in , with $y$ replaced by its expectation $p$: $$\frac{d}{df}\E[L \mid \bx] = \sigma(f) - p .$$

Step 2: stationary point. $\sigma(f) = p \iff f = \log\frac{p}{1 - p}$. The second derivative $\sigma(f)(1 - \sigma(f)) > 0$, so it is the minimum.

Logistic regression assumes the log-odds are linear in $\bx$; gradient boosting builds them as a sum of trees, which can represent non-linear effects and interactions.
