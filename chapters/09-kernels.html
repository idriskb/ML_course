Kernel methods

Linear models become non-linear as soon as we feed them non-linear features. The trouble is that the number of features explodes. Kernel methods avoid building the features at all: many linear algorithms only need inner products between data points, and a kernel computes these inner products directly, even for infinitely many features. We derive this for ridge regression, then apply it to classification and to PCA.

Learning objectives

  • Explain how feature maps make linear models non-linear, and why explicit feature expansions do not scale.
  • Derive the dual form of ridge regression and show that it only uses inner products.
  • State the kernel trick, recognise valid kernels, and interpret the linear, polynomial and Gaussian kernels.
  • Fit kernel ridge regression and kernel logistic regression, and explain the roles of $\lambda$ and the kernel width.
  • Derive kernel PCA as PCA in feature space.

From linear models to feature spaces

A linear model can describe non-linear relationships if we first transform the inputs. With a feature map $\phi : \R^p \to \R^M$, the model $$\hat y(\bx) = \theta\T\phi(\bx), \qquad \theta \in \R^M,$$ is non-linear in $\bx$ but still linear in the parameters, so everything we know about least squares, ridge and logistic regression still applies, with the design matrix $\Phi$ whose rows are $\phi(\bx_i)\T$. Polynomial regression in Chapter 1 is the example $\phi(x) = (1, x, x^2, \dots, x^d)$.

In the feature space a straight line or plane can separate data that no straight line separates in the input space.

The number of features explodes

With $p$ inputs, a polynomial model of total degree $d$ uses every monomial $x_1^{a_1}x_2^{a_2}\cdots x_p^{a_p}$ with $a_1 + \dots + a_p \leq d$.

The number of monomials of total degree at most $d$ in $p$ variables is $$M = \binom{p + d}{d} .$$

Proof

Step 1: add a slack exponent. Let $a_0 = d - (a_1 + \dots + a_p) \geq 0$. Monomials of degree at most $d$ correspond exactly to non-negative integer solutions of $$a_0 + a_1 + \dots + a_p = d .$$

Step 2: stars and bars. Write $d$ stars in a row and insert $p$ bars to cut them into $p + 1$ groups of sizes $a_0, \dots, a_p$ (groups may be empty). Each solution is one arrangement of $d$ stars and $p$ bars, so their number is the number of ways to choose which $d$ of the $p + d$ positions hold stars: $\binom{p + d}{d}$.

For $p = 2$, $d = 2$ there are 6 features ($1, x_1, x_2, x_1^2, x_1x_2, x_2^2$); for $p = 10$, $d = 5$ there are $\binom{15}{5} = 3003$; for $p = 100$, $d = 3$ already $176\,851$. Building and storing these features, and fitting as many parameters, quickly becomes impossible. The way out is to notice that we never need the features themselves.

The dual form of ridge regression

Ridge regression in feature space minimises $\norm{\Phi\theta - \by}^2 + \lambda\norm{\theta}^2$, with solution (Chapter 3) $$\hat\theta = \big(\Phi\T\Phi + \lambda I_M\big)^{-1}\Phi\T\by .$$ This requires solving an $M \times M$ system. An identity lets us move the inverse to the $N \times N$ side ($N$ is the number of training points).

For any $\Phi \in \R^{N \times M}$ and $\lambda > 0$, $$\big(\Phi\T\Phi + \lambda I_M\big)^{-1}\Phi\T = \Phi\T\big(\Phi\Phi\T + \lambda I_N\big)^{-1} .$$

Proof

Step 1: both matrices are invertible. $\Phi\T\Phi$ and $\Phi\Phi\T$ are positive semidefinite, so adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda > 0$ makes all eigenvalues at least $\lambda > 0$.

Step 2: one product, two ways. $$\Phi\T\big(\Phi\Phi\T + \lambda I_N\big) = \Phi\T\Phi\Phi\T + \lambda\Phi\T = \big(\Phi\T\Phi + \lambda I_M\big)\Phi\T .$$

Step 3: invert. Multiply on the left by $(\Phi\T\Phi + \lambda I_M)^{-1}$ and on the right by $(\Phi\Phi\T + \lambda I_N)^{-1}$.

Let $K = \Phi\Phi\T \in \R^{N \times N}$, so $K_{ij} = \phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx_j)$, and for a new input let $\mathbf k(\bx) \in \R^N$ with $\mathbf k(\bx)_i = \phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx)$. Then $$\hat\theta = \Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i), \qquad \boldsymbol\alpha = \big(K + \lambda I_N\big)^{-1}\by,$$ and the prediction is $$\hat y(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx) = \mathbf k(\bx)\T\big(K + \lambda I_N\big)^{-1}\by .$$

Proof

Step 1: apply the lemma. $$\hat\theta = \big(\Phi\T\Phi + \lambda I_M\big)^{-1}\Phi\T\by = \Phi\T\underbrace{\big(\Phi\Phi\T + \lambda I_N\big)^{-1}\by}_{=\ \boldsymbol\alpha} .$$

Step 2: predict. $$\hat y(\bx) = \phi(\bx)\T\hat\theta = \phi(\bx)\T\Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx)\T\phi(\bx_i) = \mathbf k(\bx)\T\boldsymbol\alpha .$$

The training data enter only through the inner products $\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx_j)$ and $\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx)$. The primal form solves an $M \times M$ system; the dual form an $N \times N$ system, which is cheaper whenever there are more features than examples. The coefficients $\alpha_i$ have three readings:

  • coordinates: $\hat\theta$ lies in the span of the training features, and $\boldsymbol\alpha$ gives its coordinates in that spanning set;
  • weights: $\alpha_i$ measures how much training point $i$ influences predictions;
  • amplitudes: the prediction $\sum_i \alpha_i\,k(\bx_i, \bx)$ is a sum of bumps centred at the training points (see ).

That $\hat\theta$ lies in the span of the training features is not a coincidence of ridge regression.

Let $L$ be any loss and $\lambda > 0$. Every minimiser of $$J(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N L\big(y_i,\ \theta\T\phi(\bx_i)\big) + \lambda\norm{\theta}^2$$ has the form $\theta = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i)$ for some $\boldsymbol\alpha \in \R^N$.

Proof

Step 1: decompose. Let $S = \operatorname{span}\{\phi(\bx_1), \dots, \phi(\bx_N)\}$ and write any $\theta = \theta_S + \theta_\perp$ with $\theta_S \in S$ and $\theta_\perp$ orthogonal to $S$.

Step 2: the loss does not see $\theta_\perp$. For every training point, $\theta\T\phi(\bx_i) = \theta_S\T\phi(\bx_i) + \underbrace{\theta_\perp\T\phi(\bx_i)}_{=\,0}$.

Step 3: the penalty does. By Pythagoras, $\norm{\theta}^2 = \norm{\theta_S}^2 + \norm{\theta_\perp}^2$. Hence $$J(\theta) = J(\theta_S) + \lambda\norm{\theta_\perp}^2 ,$$ so any $\theta$ with $\theta_\perp \neq 0$ is beaten by $\theta_S$. A minimiser lies in $S$.

So for logistic regression, or any regularised linear model, we may search only over $\theta = \sum_i \alpha_i\phi(\bx_i)$, and then every prediction $\theta\T\phi(\bx) = \sum_i\alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx)$ again needs only inner products.

The kernel trick

A kernel is a function $k : \R^p \times \R^p \to \R$ that is an inner product of features, $$k(\bx, \bx') = \phi(\bx)\T\phi(\bx')$$ for some feature map $\phi$ into a (possibly infinite-dimensional) space. Its kernel matrix (Gram matrix) on points $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_N$ is $K_{ij} = k(\bx_i, \bx_j)$.

The kernel trick: in any algorithm that uses the data only through inner products, replace $\phi(\bx)\T\phi(\bx')$ by $k(\bx, \bx')$. We then work in the feature space without ever computing $\phi$.

In one dimension, take $\phi(x) = (1, \sqrt2\,x, x^2)$. Then $$\phi(x)\T\phi(x') = 1 + 2xx' + x^2x'^2 = (1 + xx')^2 .$$ Evaluating $(1 + xx')^2$ costs two multiplications and gives the inner product of the three features without forming them. More generally the polynomial kernel $k(\bx, \bx') = (1 + \bx\T\bx')^d$ is the inner product of a feature map containing all monomials of degree at most $d$ (with suitable weights): the $\binom{p + d}{d}$ features of at the cost of one inner product in $\R^p$.

Three kernels cover most uses:

  • Linear: $k(\bx, \bx') = \bx\T\bx'$. No lifting: ordinary linear models.
  • Polynomial: $k(\bx, \bx') = (1 + \bx\T\bx')^d$. Smooth curved boundaries, all interactions up to degree $d$.
  • Gaussian (radial basis function, RBF): $$k(\bx, \bx') = \exp\Big(-\frac{\norm{\bx - \bx'}^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$ Similarity decays with distance at a length scale $\sigma$. Its feature space is infinite-dimensional.

Which functions are kernels?

For any kernel and any points $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_N$, the kernel matrix $K$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

Proof

Symmetry: $k(\bx_i, \bx_j) = \phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx_j) = \phi(\bx_j)\T\phi(\bx_i)$. For any $\mathbf v \in \R^N$, $$\mathbf v\T K\mathbf v = \sum_{i,j} v_i v_j\,\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx_j) = \Big(\sum_i v_i\phi(\bx_i)\Big)\T\Big(\sum_j v_j\phi(\bx_j)\Big) = \Big\lVert\sum_i v_i\phi(\bx_i)\Big\rVert^2 \geq 0 .$$

The converse is Mercer's theorem (stated without proof): a symmetric function $k$ whose kernel matrices are positive semidefinite for every finite set of points is an inner product $\phi(\bx)\T\phi(\bx')$ in some feature space. So we can check validity without finding $\phi$. Valid kernels can also be built from others.

If $k_1, k_2$ are kernels, $c > 0$ and $g : \R^p \to \R$ is any function, then $k_1 + k_2$, $\ c\,k_1$ and $g(\bx)\,k_1(\bx, \bx')\,g(\bx')$ are kernels. (Products $k_1k_2$ and limits of kernels are kernels too; we state this without proof.)

Proof

We check positive semidefiniteness of the kernel matrices and use Mercer's theorem.

Sum and scaling. $\mathbf v\T(K_1 + K_2)\mathbf v = \mathbf v\T K_1\mathbf v + \mathbf v\T K_2\mathbf v \geq 0$, and $\mathbf v\T(cK_1)\mathbf v = c\,\mathbf v\T K_1\mathbf v \geq 0$.

Rescaling by $g$. The new matrix is $DK_1D$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(g(\bx_i))$, and $\mathbf v\T DK_1D\mathbf v = (D\mathbf v)\T K_1(D\mathbf v) \geq 0$.

For example, the Gaussian kernel is $e^{-\norm{\bx}^2/2\sigma^2}\, e^{\bx\T\bx'/\sigma^2}\, e^{-\norm{\bx'}^2/2\sigma^2}$, and $e^{\bx\T\bx'/\sigma^2} = \sum_{n \geq 0}(\bx\T\bx')^n/(\sigma^{2n}n!)$ is a limit of sums of positive multiples of products of linear kernels. By the rules it is a kernel, with infinitely many features (one block per degree $n$).

Kernels define a geometry

A kernel fixes the inner products in the feature space, hence also lengths and distances: $$\norm{\phi(\bx) - \phi(\bx')}^2 = k(\bx, \bx) + k(\bx', \bx') - 2k(\bx, \bx') .$$ (Expand the square.) For the Gaussian kernel $k(\bx, \bx) = 1$: every point is mapped to the unit sphere of the feature space, and $\norm{\phi(\bx) - \phi(\bx')}^2 = 2 - 2k(\bx, \bx')$. Close inputs have nearly equal features; inputs much farther apart than $\sigma$ are mapped to nearly orthogonal directions. The width $\sigma$ sets which points count as similar.

For the Gaussian kernel on distinct training points $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_N$:

  • as $\sigma \to 0$, $K \to I_N$ and kernel ridge predictions at any new $\bx$ not in the training set tend to $0$ (each point only "sees" itself: memorisation);
  • as $\sigma \to \infty$, every entry of $K$ tends to $1$: all points look alike and the fit tends to a constant.
Proof

Small $\sigma$. For $i \neq j$, $\norm{\bx_i - \bx_j} > 0$, so $e^{-\norm{\bx_i - \bx_j}^2/2\sigma^2} \to 0$, while the diagonal is $1$. Likewise $\mathbf k(\bx) \to \mathbf 0$ for $\bx$ different from all $\bx_i$. Then $\boldsymbol\alpha \to \by/(1 + \lambda)$ and $\hat y(\bx) = \mathbf k(\bx)\T\boldsymbol\alpha \to 0$.

Large $\sigma$. For fixed points, $\norm{\bx - \bx'}^2/2\sigma^2 \to 0$, so every kernel value tends to $e^0 = 1$, independently of the inputs.

Small $\sigma$ gives low bias and high variance; large $\sigma$ the opposite. Like $k$ in k-NN, $\sigma$ is a complexity knob, and it interacts with $\lambda$.

Kernel ridge regression

Kernel ridge regression predicts $$\hat y(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,k(\bx_i, \bx), \qquad \boldsymbol\alpha = (K + \lambda I_N)^{-1}\by .$$ In practice $\by$ is centred first (or a constant is added to the kernel) so that the intercept is not penalised.

The model is non-parametric in the sense of Chapter 7: it keeps all training points, one coefficient each. How many parameters does it effectively use? On the training inputs, $\hat\by = K\boldsymbol\alpha = K(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\by = S\by$, and as for least squares and k-NN we measure complexity by $\tr(S)$.

Let $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_N \geq 0$ be the eigenvalues of $K$. The effective number of parameters of kernel ridge regression is $$\mathrm{df}(\lambda) = \tr\big(K(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\big) = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{\mu_i}{\mu_i + \lambda} .$$ It decreases from $\operatorname{rank}(K)$ (as $\lambda \to 0$) to $0$ (as $\lambda \to \infty$).

Proof

Step 1: diagonalise. $K = U\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i)U\T$ with $U$ orthogonal, so $K + \lambda I = U\operatorname{diag}(\mu_i + \lambda)U\T$ and $$K(K + \lambda I)^{-1} = U\operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{\mu_i}{\mu_i + \lambda}\Big)U\T .$$

Step 2: trace. The trace is invariant under $A \mapsto UAU\T$, so it is the sum of the diagonal entries $\mu_i/(\mu_i + \lambda)$. Each term is $1$ (if $\mu_i > 0$) or $0$ (if $\mu_i = 0$) in the limit $\lambda \to 0$, and tends to $0$ as $\lambda \to \infty$.

Each eigen-direction of $K$ is kept in proportion $\mu_i/(\mu_i + \lambda)$: directions with large eigenvalues (smooth patterns shared by many points) survive, those with small eigenvalues (wiggles) are shrunk. This is the same shrinkage as ridge regression in Chapter 3, now in the kernel's geometry.

Choosing the kernel and its hyperparameters

  • The kernel encodes what "similar" means. The Gaussian kernel is a good default for numerical features; the polynomial kernel suits interactions of known order; the linear kernel is the baseline.
  • The hyperparameters ($\lambda$, and $\sigma$ or $d$) are chosen by cross-validation on a logarithmic grid, e.g. $\lambda \in \{10^{-4}, \dots, 10^{1}\}$, $\sigma$ around the typical distance between points.
  • Scaling: the Gaussian kernel uses Euclidean distances, so standardise the features first, as for k-NN.
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.kernel_ridge import KernelRidge
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KernelRidge(kernel="rbf"))
grid = {"kernelridge__alpha": [1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1, 1],      # λ
        "kernelridge__gamma": [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10]}                # γ = 1 / (2σ²)
search = GridSearchCV(model, grid, cv=5).fit(X_train, y_train)

Kernels for classification

By the representer theorem, a regularised linear classifier in feature space has a decision function of the form $$f(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,k(\bx_i, \bx) + b .$$ The boundary $f(\bx) = 0$ is a hyperplane in feature space, and a curve in input space whose shape is set by the kernel: straight for the linear kernel, smooth curves for the polynomial kernel, flexible and local for the Gaussian kernel.

Kernel logistic regression

Logistic regression with $\P(y = 1 \mid \bx) = \sigma(f(\bx))$ and an L2 penalty (Chapter 4) becomes, with $\theta = \sum_i\alpha_i\phi(\bx_i)$ so that $\norm{\theta}^2 = \boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha$ and $(f(\bx_1), \dots, f(\bx_N)) = K\boldsymbol\alpha$: $$J(\boldsymbol\alpha) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[-y_i\log\sigma\big((K\boldsymbol\alpha)_i\big) - (1 - y_i)\log\big(1 - \sigma((K\boldsymbol\alpha)_i)\big)\Big] + \frac\lambda2\,\boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha .$$

With $\mathbf p = \sigma(K\boldsymbol\alpha)$ (applied entrywise), $$\nabla J(\boldsymbol\alpha) = K\Big(\frac1N(\mathbf p - \by) + \lambda\boldsymbol\alpha\Big) .$$ $J$ is convex, so gradient descent or Newton's method finds the global minimum.

Proof

Step 1: the loss term. Write $\mathbf f = K\boldsymbol\alpha$. From Chapter 4, the gradient of the average cross-entropy with respect to $\mathbf f$ is $\frac1N(\mathbf p - \by)$. By the chain rule, with $\partial\mathbf f/\partial\boldsymbol\alpha = K$ (symmetric), the gradient with respect to $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is $K\cdot\frac1N(\mathbf p - \by)$.

Step 2: the penalty. $\nabla\big(\frac\lambda2\boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha\big) = \lambda K\boldsymbol\alpha$.

Step 3: convexity. The cross-entropy is convex in $\mathbf f$ (Chapter 4) and $\mathbf f$ is linear in $\boldsymbol\alpha$; $\boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha$ is convex because $K$ is positive semidefinite.

Support vector machines. Replacing the cross-entropy by the hinge loss $\max(0, 1 - s_i f(\bx_i))$ with $s_i = \pm1$ gives the support vector machine. Its solution has the same form $\sum_i\alpha_i k(\bx_i, \bx) + b$, with a useful extra property: most $\alpha_i$ are exactly zero, and only the training points on or inside the margin (the support vectors) are needed for prediction. In scikit-learn: SVC(kernel="rbf", C=..., gamma=...), where $C$ plays the role of $1/\lambda$.

Kernel PCA

Kernels also apply to unsupervised methods. PCA (Chapter 5) finds directions of maximum variance, but only straight ones. Kernel PCA performs PCA in the feature space, where a straight direction can correspond to a curved one in the input space.

Recall PCA from Chapter 5. For centred data with rows $\bx_i\T$ of $\bX$, PCA is the eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix: $$\Sigma = \frac1N\bX\T\bX, \qquad \Sigma\,\bu = \lambda\,\bu, \qquad \norm{\bu} = 1, \qquad \text{score } z(\bx) = \bu\T\bx .$$ Kernel PCA is this same computation with every $\bx_i$ replaced by its feature vector $\phi(\bx_i)$. Assume for now that the features are centred, $\sum_i\phi(\bx_i) = \mathbf 0$ (we remove this assumption below).

Let $\Phi$ be the matrix with rows $\phi(\bx_i)\T$ and $K = \Phi\Phi\T$. PCA in feature space, i.e. the eigen-decomposition of $C = \frac1N\Phi\T\Phi$, is obtained from the $N \times N$ problem $$K\boldsymbol\alpha = N\lambda\,\boldsymbol\alpha, \qquad \lambda > 0, \qquad \boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha = 1 :$$ each such $\boldsymbol\alpha$ gives the unit principal direction $\mathbf v = \sum_i\alpha_i\phi(\bx_i)$ of $C$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, every principal direction with $\lambda > 0$ arises this way, and the score of any input is $$z(\bx) = \mathbf v\T\phi(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,k(\bx_i, \bx) .$$

Proof

Step 1: PCA with features. Replace $\bx_i$ by $\phi(\bx_i)$ in the PCA recipe above: the data matrix $\bX$ becomes $\Phi$, the covariance $\Sigma$ becomes $C = \frac1N\Phi\T\Phi$, and PCA asks for $$C\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v \iff \frac1N\Phi\T\Phi\,\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v, \qquad \norm{\mathbf v} = 1 . \tag{1}$$ This is an $M \times M$ problem, with $M$ huge or infinite: we cannot solve it directly.

Step 2: the principal directions lie in the span of the data. If $\lambda > 0$, divide (1) by $\lambda$: $$\mathbf v = \frac{1}{N\lambda}\Phi\T(\Phi\mathbf v) = \Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i), \qquad \boldsymbol\alpha = \frac{1}{N\lambda}\Phi\mathbf v \in \R^N .$$ So we only need the $N$ numbers $\boldsymbol\alpha$, as in the representer theorem.

Step 3: substitute into the eigen-equation. Put $\mathbf v = \Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha$ in (1) and use $\Phi\Phi\T = K$: $$\frac1N\Phi\T\underbrace{\Phi\Phi\T}_{=\,K}\boldsymbol\alpha = \lambda\,\Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha .$$

Step 4: only kernels remain. Multiply on the left by $\Phi$, which turns every $\Phi\Phi\T$ into $K$: $$\frac1N K^2\boldsymbol\alpha = \lambda\,K\boldsymbol\alpha \iff K\big(K\boldsymbol\alpha - N\lambda\,\boldsymbol\alpha\big) = \mathbf 0 .$$ Every solution of $K\boldsymbol\alpha = N\lambda\boldsymbol\alpha$ satisfies this. The other solutions differ from these by some $\mathbf w$ with $K\mathbf w = \mathbf 0$, which does not change $\mathbf v$: $\norm{\Phi\T\mathbf w}^2 = \mathbf w\T K\mathbf w = 0$, so $\Phi\T\mathbf w = \mathbf 0$. Hence it suffices to solve the $N \times N$ eigenvalue problem $$K\boldsymbol\alpha = N\lambda\,\boldsymbol\alpha .$$ Conversely, such an $\boldsymbol\alpha$ gives an eigenvector of $C$: $C\,\Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha = \frac1N\Phi\T K\boldsymbol\alpha = \lambda\,\Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha$.

Step 5: the unit-length condition. PCA requires $\norm{\mathbf v} = 1$. With $\mathbf v = \Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha$, $$\norm{\mathbf v}^2 = \boldsymbol\alpha\T\Phi\Phi\T\boldsymbol\alpha = \boldsymbol\alpha\T K\boldsymbol\alpha = N\lambda\,\norm{\boldsymbol\alpha}^2 = 1 .$$ So the eigenvector of $K$ must be rescaled to $\norm{\boldsymbol\alpha}^2 = 1/(N\lambda)$, not to length $1$.

Step 6: the scores. The PCA score $\bu\T\bx$ becomes $$z(\bx) = \mathbf v\T\phi(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,\phi(\bx_i)\T\phi(\bx) = \sum_{i=1}^N \alpha_i\,k(\bx_i, \bx).$$ Every step uses $\phi$ only through inner products, which the kernel provides.

In practice: compute $K$, centre it (below), find its top eigenvectors, rescale them so that $\boldsymbol\alpha_k\T K\boldsymbol\alpha_k = 1$, and project. On the training points the scores are simply $\mathbf z_k = K\boldsymbol\alpha_k = N\lambda_k\boldsymbol\alpha_k$. With the linear kernel, $K = \bX\bX\T$ and Steps 2 to 4 are exactly the Gram-matrix computation of PCA in Chapter 5: kernel PCA gives back ordinary PCA.

The kernel matrix of the centred features $\tilde\phi(\bx_i) = \phi(\bx_i) - \frac1N\sum_j\phi(\bx_j)$ is $$\tilde K = K - \mathbf 1_N K - K\mathbf 1_N + \mathbf 1_N K\mathbf 1_N ,$$ where $\mathbf 1_N$ is the $N \times N$ matrix with all entries $1/N$. So centring needs only $K$.

Proof

Step 1: expand one entry. With $\bar\phi = \frac1N\sum_j\phi(\bx_j)$, $$\tilde K_{ij} = \big(\phi(\bx_i) - \bar\phi\big)\T\big(\phi(\bx_j) - \bar\phi\big) = K_{ij} - \phi(\bx_i)\T\bar\phi - \bar\phi\T\phi(\bx_j) + \bar\phi\T\bar\phi .$$

Step 2: each term in terms of $K$. $$\phi(\bx_i)\T\bar\phi = \frac1N\sum_l K_{il} = (K\mathbf 1_N)_{ij}, \quad \bar\phi\T\phi(\bx_j) = \frac1N\sum_l K_{lj} = (\mathbf 1_N K)_{ij}, \quad \bar\phi\T\bar\phi = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{l,m}K_{lm} = (\mathbf 1_N K\mathbf 1_N)_{ij} .$$

Strengths, limitations and connections

  • Strengths. Non-linear models built on linear algebra: convex problems with a unique solution (ridge, logistic, SVM); flexible through the choice of kernel; the effective complexity adapts to the data.
  • Cost. The kernel matrix has $N^2$ entries and solving with it costs about $N^3$ operations; every prediction uses all $N$ training points (or the support vectors). Beyond $N \approx 10^4$–$10^5$, approximations such as random features or the Nyström method are needed.
  • Choices. There is no universal kernel; the kernel and its parameters must be tuned, and features scaled.
  • Interpretability. The implicit features cannot be inspected; we see the predictions, not a representation.

Connections. The Gaussian kernel makes kernel methods local like k-NN, but with a soft neighbourhood: every point contributes with a weight that decays with distance. (The Nadaraya–Watson estimator $\hat f(\bx) = \sum_i k(\bx_i, \bx)y_i / \sum_i k(\bx_i, \bx)$ is the soft version of the k-NN average.) Kernel ridge regression is ridge regression in feature space, and kernel PCA is PCA in feature space. Neural networks, next, take the opposite route: they learn the feature map instead of fixing it through a kernel.

Key takeaways

  • A feature map makes a linear model non-linear in $\bx$, but explicit features explode in number ($\binom{p+d}{d}$ monomials).
  • Ridge regression has a dual form $\hat y(\bx) = \mathbf k(\bx)\T(K + \lambda I)^{-1}\by$ that uses only inner products; by the representer theorem the same holds for any regularised linear model.
  • A kernel computes feature inner products directly. Valid kernels have positive semidefinite kernel matrices; linear, polynomial and Gaussian kernels are the usual choices.
  • $\lambda$ and the kernel width control complexity (effective parameters $\sum\mu_i/(\mu_i + \lambda)$); tune them by cross-validation on scaled features.
  • Kernel logistic regression and SVMs classify with $\sum_i\alpha_i k(\bx_i, \bx) + b$; kernel PCA solves $K\boldsymbol\alpha = N\lambda\boldsymbol\alpha$ on the centred kernel matrix.
  • Cost grows like $N^2$ in memory and $N^3$ in time.

Probabilistic view: Gaussian processes

Kernel ridge regression has an exact Bayesian twin. Instead of fitting a function, put a probability distribution on functions whose covariance is the kernel.

A Gaussian process with covariance function $k$, written $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k)$, is a random function such that for any inputs $\bx_1, \dots, \bx_m$ the vector $(f(\bx_1), \dots, f(\bx_m))$ is Gaussian with mean $\mathbf 0$ and covariance matrix $[k(\bx_i, \bx_j)]_{ij}$.

A valid kernel is exactly what makes these covariance matrices positive semidefinite. The Gaussian kernel gives smooth random functions that vary on a length scale $\sigma$. For example, Bayesian linear regression in feature space, $f(\bx) = \theta\T\phi(\bx)$ with prior $\theta \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$, is a Gaussian process with $\Cov(f(\bx), f(\bx')) = \phi(\bx)\T\phi(\bx') = k(\bx, \bx')$.

Let $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k)$ and $y_i = f(\bx_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with independent noise $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, s^2)$. Given the data, $f(\bx)$ at a new input is Gaussian with $$\E[f(\bx) \mid \by] = \mathbf k(\bx)\T\big(K + s^2 I\big)^{-1}\by, \qquad \Var(f(\bx) \mid \by) = k(\bx, \bx) - \mathbf k(\bx)\T\big(K + s^2 I\big)^{-1}\mathbf k(\bx).$$ The posterior mean is exactly kernel ridge regression with $\lambda = s^2$.

Proof

Step 1: the joint distribution. $(\by, f(\bx))$ is Gaussian with mean zero and $$\Cov(\by) = K + s^2 I, \qquad \Cov(\by, f(\bx)) = \mathbf k(\bx), \qquad \Var(f(\bx)) = k(\bx, \bx),$$ because the noise is independent of $f$ and adds $s^2$ to the diagonal only.

Step 2: Gaussian conditioning. For a zero-mean Gaussian vector $(\mathbf a, b)$, the conditional distribution of $b$ given $\mathbf a$ is Gaussian with mean $\Cov(b, \mathbf a)\Cov(\mathbf a)^{-1}\mathbf a$ and variance $\Var(b) - \Cov(b, \mathbf a)\Cov(\mathbf a)^{-1}\Cov(\mathbf a, b)$ (a standard fact, which can be proved by completing the square as in the extra section of Chapter 5).

Step 3: substitute $\mathbf a = \by$, $b = f(\bx)$.

The Bayesian view adds two things to kernel ridge regression. The posterior variance gives error bars: it is small near training points and grows back to $k(\bx, \bx)$ far from them. And the regularisation $\lambda$ gets a meaning, the noise variance, while $\sigma$ and $s^2$ can be chosen by maximising the marginal likelihood of the data instead of by cross-validation.

With a single training point $(\bx_1, y_1)$ and the Gaussian kernel, compute the posterior mean and variance of $f(\bx)$. Describe them as functions of $\norm{\bx - \bx_1}$ and interpret the limits $\norm{\bx - \bx_1} \to 0$ and $\to \infty$.
