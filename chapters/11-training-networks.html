Backpropagation & training neural networks

A network is trained like every model in this course: choose a loss and move the parameters against its gradient. What is new is the scale. A network has thousands to billions of parameters arranged in layers, and its loss is not convex. This chapter derives backpropagation, which computes all the partial derivatives in about the cost of two forward passes, and then the practical tools that make training work: careful initialisation, momentum and Adam, and regularisation by weight decay, early stopping and dropout.

Learning objectives

  • Derive the backpropagation equations from the chain rule and run them by hand on a small network.
  • Explain why backpropagation costs about as much as a forward pass, while finite differences cost one forward pass per parameter.
  • Bound how gradients shrink or grow through layers, and choose an initialisation that breaks symmetry and keeps activations at a stable scale.
  • State and interpret the updates of mini-batch SGD, momentum and Adam.
  • Regularise a network with weight decay, early stopping and dropout, and relate them to ridge regression.

The training problem

We use the notation of Chapter 10: layers $\ell = 1, \dots, L$ of widths $n_\ell$, pre-activations $\mathbf z^{(\ell)} = \bW^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)} + \bb^{(\ell)}$, activations $\ba^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)}(\mathbf z^{(\ell)})$, input $\ba^{(0)} = \bx$ and output $\ba^{(L)} = f_\theta(\bx)$. For one training pair $(\bx_i, y_i)$, write $\mathcal L_i(\theta) = \mathcal L\big(f_\theta(\bx_i), y_i\big)$ for its loss (squared error or cross-entropy). Training minimises the average loss $$J(\theta) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \mathcal L_i(\theta)$$ by (stochastic) gradient descent, exactly as in Chapter 2: $$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla J(\theta), \qquad \nabla J(\theta) = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \nabla\mathcal L_i(\theta) .$$ Two things differ from linear models. $J$ is not convex, so gradient descent finds a local solution that depends on where it starts. And $\nabla\mathcal L_i$ is a vector with one entry per weight and bias of the network, which we need an efficient way to compute.

A direct approach approximates each partial derivative by a finite difference, $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L_i}{\partial\theta_k} \approx \frac{\mathcal L_i(\theta + \varepsilon\mathbf e_k) - \mathcal L_i(\theta - \varepsilon\mathbf e_k)}{2\varepsilon} .$$ This needs two forward passes per parameter. For a network with $10^5$ parameters, whose forward pass costs about $10^5$ operations, one gradient would cost about $2 \times 10^{10}$ operations, and the result is only approximate. Backpropagation computes the exact gradient for about three forward passes.

Backpropagation

Fix one training pair and drop the index $i$. The key quantity is the sensitivity of the loss to each pre-activation.

The error signal of layer $\ell$ is the vector $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf z^{(\ell)}} \in \R^{n_\ell}, \qquad \delta^{(\ell)}_j = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j} .$$ It measures how much the loss would change if the pre-activation $z^{(\ell)}_j$ were nudged.

Let the output activation act entrywise. Writing $\odot$ for the entrywise product,

  • (BP1) output layer: $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} = \sigma^{(L)\prime}\big(\mathbf z^{(L)}\big) \odot \dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\ba^{(L)}}$;
  • (BP2) backward recursion: for $\ell = L - 1, \dots, 1$, $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)\prime}\big(\mathbf z^{(\ell)}\big) \odot \Big(\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}\Big);$$
  • (BP3) weights: $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial W^{(\ell)}_{jk}} = \delta^{(\ell)}_j\,a^{(\ell-1)}_k$, that is, $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$;
  • (BP4) biases: $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bb^{(\ell)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$.
Proof

Everything follows from the chain rule, applied to how each variable influences the loss.

Step 1: (BP1). The pre-activation $z^{(L)}_j$ affects $\mathcal L$ only through $a^{(L)}_j = \sigma^{(L)}(z^{(L)}_j)$. Hence $$\delta^{(L)}_j = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial a^{(L)}_j}\cdot\frac{\partial a^{(L)}_j}{\partial z^{(L)}_j} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial a^{(L)}_j}\,\sigma^{(L)\prime}\big(z^{(L)}_j\big) .$$

Step 2: how $z^{(\ell)}_j$ reaches the loss. For $\ell < L$, $z^{(\ell)}_j$ affects $\mathcal L$ only through $a^{(\ell)}_j$, and $a^{(\ell)}_j$ only through the next pre-activations $$z^{(\ell+1)}_i = \sum_{k} W^{(\ell+1)}_{ik}\,a^{(\ell)}_k + b^{(\ell+1)}_i, \qquad \frac{\partial z^{(\ell+1)}_i}{\partial a^{(\ell)}_j} = W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n_{\ell+1} .$$

Step 3: (BP2). The multivariable chain rule sums over all these paths: $$\delta^{(\ell)}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\ell+1}} \underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z^{(\ell+1)}_i}}_{=\ \delta^{(\ell+1)}_i} \cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(\ell+1)}_i}{\partial a^{(\ell)}_j}}_{=\ W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}} \cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial a^{(\ell)}_j}{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j}}_{=\ \sigma^{(\ell)\prime}(z^{(\ell)}_j)} = \sigma^{(\ell)\prime}\big(z^{(\ell)}_j\big)\,\Big(\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}\Big)_j ,$$ since $\sum_i W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}\delta^{(\ell+1)}_i$ is the $j$-th entry of $\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$.

Step 4: (BP3) and (BP4). The weight $W^{(\ell)}_{jk}$ and the bias $b^{(\ell)}_j$ affect $\mathcal L$ only through $z^{(\ell)}_j = \sum_k W^{(\ell)}_{jk}a^{(\ell-1)}_k + b^{(\ell)}_j$, with $$\frac{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j}{\partial W^{(\ell)}_{jk}} = a^{(\ell-1)}_k, \qquad \frac{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j}{\partial b^{(\ell)}_j} = 1 .$$ Multiply by $\delta^{(\ell)}_j = \partial\mathcal L/\partial z^{(\ell)}_j$.

The error signals flow backwards, from the output to the first layer, through the transposed weight matrices: the same weights that carried activations forward carry sensitivities back. The output layer only enters through $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}$, which is especially simple when the output activation and the loss are matched as in Chapter 10.

(a) Identity output and $\mathcal L = \frac12\norm{\ba^{(L)} - \by}^2$: $\ \boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} = \ba^{(L)} - \by$.
(b) Sigmoid output $p = \sigma(z^{(L)})$ and binary cross-entropy: $\ \delta^{(L)} = p - y$.
(c) Softmax output $\mathbf p = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z^{(L)})$ and cross-entropy $\mathcal L = -\log p_y$: $\ \boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} = \mathbf p - \mathbf e_y$, where $\mathbf e_y$ is the one-hot vector of the true class.

Proof

Step 1: (a). $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\ba^{(L)} = \ba^{(L)} - \by$ and the identity has derivative $1$; apply (BP1).

Step 2: (b). With $\mathcal L = -y\log p - (1 - y)\log(1 - p)$ and $\sigma' = p(1 - p)$ (Chapter 10), $$\delta^{(L)} = \Big(-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p}\Big)p(1 - p) = -y(1 - p) + (1 - y)p = p - y .$$

Step 3: (c), write the loss in terms of $\mathbf z$. Softmax does not act entrywise, so we differentiate directly: $$\mathcal L = -\log\frac{e^{z_y}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = -z_y + \log\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j} .$$

Step 4: differentiate. $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z_k} = -\mathbf 1\{k = y\} + \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = p_k - (\mathbf e_y)_k .$$

In all three cases the output error signal is "prediction minus target", as for linear and logistic regression. Mismatched pairs behave worse: with a sigmoid output and squared error, $\delta^{(L)} = (p - y)\,p(1 - p)$ is tiny when the output saturates at the wrong value, so a confidently wrong network learns slowly ().

  1. Forward pass. For $\ell = 1, \dots, L$ compute and store $\mathbf z^{(\ell)}$ and $\ba^{(\ell)}$.
  2. Output error. Compute $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}$ (BP1, or ).
  3. Backward pass. For $\ell = L, \dots, 1$: record the gradients $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$ and $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$ (BP3, BP4); if $\ell > 1$, compute $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell-1)}$ by (BP2).

For a mini-batch, run this for each example and average the gradients.

Take the network of the forward-pass example in Chapter 10: $\bW^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\bb^{(1)} = (0, -1)$, ReLU hidden units, $\bW^{(2)} = (2, -1)$, $b^{(2)} = 0.5$, identity output, input $\bx = (1, 2)$. Add the target $y = 1$ and the loss $\mathcal L = \frac12(\hat y - y)^2$. Compute the gradient and take one step with $\eta = 0.1$.

Step 1: forward pass (from Chapter 10). $$\mathbf z^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \ba^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat y = -1.5, \quad \mathcal L = \tfrac12(-2.5)^2 = 3.125 .$$

Step 2: output error. By (a), $$\delta^{(2)} = \hat y - y = -2.5 .$$

Step 3: hidden error (BP2). $\operatorname{ReLU}'(z) = \mathbf 1\{z > 0\}$ is $0$ for the first unit and $1$ for the second: $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \odot \Big(\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}(-2.5)\Big) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \odot \begin{pmatrix} -5 \\ 2.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2.5 \end{pmatrix} .$$ The inactive unit receives no error signal.

Step 4: gradients (BP3, BP4). $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(2)}} = \delta^{(2)}\ba^{(1)\top} = (0, -5), \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = -2.5,$$ $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}\bx\T = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 2.5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bb^{(1)}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 2.5 \end{pmatrix} .$$

Step 5: one gradient step. With $\eta = 0.1$: $\bW^{(2)} = (2, -0.5)$, $b^{(2)} = 0.75$, $\bW^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0.75 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$, $\bb^{(1)} = (0, -1.25)$. A new forward pass gives $\mathbf z^{(1)} = (-1, 0.5)$, $\ba^{(1)} = (0, 0.5)$ and $$\hat y = -0.5 \cdot 0.5 + 0.75 = 0.5, \qquad \mathcal L = \tfrac12(0.5 - 1)^2 = 0.125 .$$ One step reduced the loss from $3.125$ to $0.125$.

The cost of backpropagation

For one example, the forward pass of a network with widths $n_0, \dots, n_L$ uses about $C = \sum_{\ell=1}^L n_\ell n_{\ell-1}$ multiplications, and the backward pass about $2C$. So the full gradient costs about $3C$, of the order of the number of parameters, while finite differences cost about $2C$ per parameter, of the order of $C^2$ in total.

Proof

Step 1: forward. $\bW^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)}$ is an $n_\ell \times n_{\ell-1}$ matrix–vector product: $n_\ell n_{\ell-1}$ multiplications. The activations add $O(n_\ell)$, which is smaller.

Step 2: backward, error signals. (BP2) computes $\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$, again $n_{\ell+1}n_\ell$ multiplications, plus $O(n_\ell)$ for the entrywise product. Summing over layers gives at most $C$.

Step 3: backward, gradients. (BP3) forms the outer product $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$: $n_\ell n_{\ell-1}$ multiplications, $C$ in total.

Step 4: finite differences. There are about $C$ parameters (weights), and each needs two forward passes of cost $C$.

This is why networks with millions of parameters can be trained at all. Backpropagation is a special case of reverse-mode automatic differentiation: libraries such as PyTorch record the operations of the forward pass and apply the chain rule backwards automatically (loss.backward()), for any architecture. Finite differences remain useful for checking a hand-written gradient on a few coordinates.

import numpy as np

def forward_backward(W1, b1, W2, b2, X, y):
    """One hidden ReLU layer, sigmoid output, mean binary cross-entropy. X: N × p, y: N."""
    Z1 = X @ W1.T + b1                  # N × m   pre-activations
    A1 = np.maximum(Z1, 0)              # N × m   activations
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(A1 @ W2 + b2)))   # N    output probabilities
    N = len(y)
    d2 = (p - y) / N                    # output error signal, averaged over the batch
    gW2, gb2 = A1.T @ d2, d2.sum()      # BP3, BP4
    D1 = np.outer(d2, W2) * (Z1 > 0)    # BP2:  N × m
    gW1, gb1 = D1.T @ X, D1.sum(axis=0) # BP3, BP4
    return gW1, gb1, gW2, gb2

Vanishing and exploding gradients

The recursion (BP2) multiplies the error signal by $\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}$ and by the derivatives $\sigma'$ at every layer. Its size can therefore shrink or grow geometrically with depth. We write $\norm{\bW}$ for the operator norm, the largest factor by which $\bW$ stretches a vector.

If $|\sigma^{(k)\prime}| \leq s$ for all hidden layers, then for $\ell < L$ $$\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}} \leq s^{L-\ell}\Big(\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}\norm{\bW^{(k)}}\Big)\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}} .$$

Proof

Step 1: one layer. For vectors $\mathbf u, \mathbf v$, $\norm{\mathbf u \odot \mathbf v} \leq \max_j|u_j|\,\norm{\mathbf v}$. Applied to (BP2), and using $\norm{\bW\T} = \norm{\bW}$, $$\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}} \leq s\,\norm{\bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}} \leq s\,\norm{\bW^{(\ell+1)}}\,\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}} . \tag{1}$$

Step 2: iterate. Apply (1) for $\ell, \ell + 1, \dots, L - 1$ and multiply the factors.

With sigmoid units $s = \frac14$, so unless the weights are large the early layers receive geometrically small error signals: the vanishing gradient problem of Chapter 10, now for any width. Conversely, large weights can make the error signals, and the updates, explode. Remedies: ReLU-type activations ($s = 1$), an initialisation at the right scale (next section), and, against explosions, gradient clipping, which rescales the gradient whenever its norm exceeds a threshold.

Initialisation

Gradient descent needs a starting point $\theta^{(0)}$. For a convex loss any start works; for a network the start matters, and two natural choices fail.

Suppose two hidden units $j$ and $j'$ of layer $\ell$ start with the same incoming weights and bias (rows $j$ and $j'$ of $\bW^{(\ell)}$, entries of $\bb^{(\ell)}$) and the same outgoing weights (columns $j$ and $j'$ of $\bW^{(\ell+1)}$). Then they remain identical after any number of gradient steps.

Proof

Step 1: same forward values. Equal incoming weights and biases give $z^{(\ell)}_j = z^{(\ell)}_{j'}$ and $a^{(\ell)}_j = a^{(\ell)}_{j'}$ for every input.

Step 2: same error signals. By (BP2), $\delta^{(\ell)}_j = \sigma'(z^{(\ell)}_j)\sum_i W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}\delta^{(\ell+1)}_i$, and the same expression with $j'$; the columns are equal and so are the $z$'s, hence $\delta^{(\ell)}_j = \delta^{(\ell)}_{j'}$.

Step 3: same gradients. By (BP3), the gradients of the incoming weights are $\delta^{(\ell)}_j\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$ and $\delta^{(\ell)}_{j'}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$: equal. The gradients of the outgoing weights $W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij}$ and $W^{(\ell+1)}_{ij'}$ are $\delta^{(\ell+1)}_i a^{(\ell)}_j$ and $\delta^{(\ell+1)}_i a^{(\ell)}_{j'}$: equal. The bias gradients are equal too. Averaging over examples keeps them equal.

Step 4: induction. The update subtracts equal amounts from equal parameters, so the hypothesis holds again after the step, and at every later step.

So initialising all weights to the same value (for instance zero) makes every hidden layer behave like a single unit forever. Weights are therefore initialised at random, which breaks the symmetry. The second question is the scale of the random weights.

Let $z = \sum_{k=1}^{n} w_k a_k$, where the weights $w_k$ are independent with mean $0$ and variance $v$, and are independent of the inputs $a_k$, which all have the same second moment $\E[a_k^2]$. Then $$\E[z] = 0, \qquad \Var(z) = n\,v\,\E[a_k^2] .$$ If moreover $a_k = \operatorname{ReLU}(u_k)$ for a pre-activation $u_k$ symmetric about $0$, then $\E[a_k^2] = \frac12\Var(u_k)$ and $\Var(z) = \frac{n v}{2}\Var(u_k)$.

Proof

Step 1: mean. By independence, $\E[w_ka_k] = \underbrace{\E[w_k]}_{=\ 0}\E[a_k] = 0$.

Step 2: variance. Since $\E[z] = 0$, $$\Var(z) = \E[z^2] = \sum_{k}\E[w_k^2]\,\E[a_k^2] + \sum_{k \neq k'}\underbrace{\E[w_k]\,\E[w_{k'}]}_{=\ 0}\,\E[a_ka_{k'}] = n\,v\,\E[a_k^2] .$$

Step 3: ReLU. $\operatorname{ReLU}(u)^2 = u^2\,\mathbf 1\{u > 0\}$, and by symmetry the events $u > 0$ and $u < 0$ contribute equally to $\E[u^2] = \Var(u)$, so $\E[\operatorname{ReLU}(u)^2] = \frac12\Var(u)$.

Through $L$ ReLU layers of width $n$ the variance of the pre-activations is multiplied by $(nv/2)^L$. It explodes if $v > 2/n$ and vanishes if $v < 2/n$. The usual variance-preserving initialisations are therefore $$\text{ReLU: } \Var(w) = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}, \qquad \text{tanh: } \Var(w) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}},$$ with $n_{\text{in}} = n_{\ell-1}$ the number of inputs of the unit (for tanh, $\tanh(u) \approx u$ near $0$, so $\E[a^2] \approx \Var(u)$). Biases start at $0$. Deep learning libraries use such initialisations by default.

Optimisation algorithms

Training uses the mini-batch SGD of Chapter 2: at each step, compute the gradient by backpropagation on a random mini-batch of $m$ examples (typically 32 to 256) and update. One pass through the whole training set is an epoch. Two refinements are used almost everywhere.

Momentum

Gradient descent with momentum keeps a velocity $\bv$ and updates, with $\mathbf g_t$ the mini-batch gradient at $\theta_t$ and $0 \leq \beta < 1$, $$\bv_{t+1} = \beta\,\bv_t + \mathbf g_t, \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\bv_{t+1}, \qquad \bv_0 = \mathbf 0 .$$

(a) $\bv_{t+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta^{t-k}\mathbf g_k$: the velocity is an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients.
(b) If the gradient is constant, $\mathbf g_k = \mathbf g$, then $\bv_t \to \mathbf g/(1 - \beta)$: the effective learning rate becomes $\eta/(1 - \beta)$, ten times larger for $\beta = 0.9$.
(c) If it alternates, $\mathbf g_k = (-1)^k\mathbf g$, then $\norm{\bv_t} \leq \norm{\mathbf g}/(1 - \beta)$ but $\bv_t$ alternates around $\pm\mathbf g/(1 + \beta)$: oscillating components are damped.

Proof

Step 1: unroll. By induction, $\bv_{t+1} = \beta\bv_t + \mathbf g_t = \beta(\beta\bv_{t-1} + \mathbf g_{t-1}) + \mathbf g_t = \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta^{t-k}\mathbf g_k$.

Step 2: constant gradient. A geometric sum: $$\bv_{t+1} = \mathbf g\sum_{j=0}^{t}\beta^j = \mathbf g\,\frac{1 - \beta^{t+1}}{1 - \beta} \xrightarrow[t \to \infty]{} \frac{\mathbf g}{1 - \beta} .$$

Step 3: alternating gradient. Now $$\bv_{t+1} = \sum_{k=0}^{t}\beta^{t-k}(-1)^k\mathbf g = (-1)^t\,\mathbf g\sum_{j=0}^{t}(-\beta)^j = (-1)^t\,\mathbf g\,\frac{1 - (-\beta)^{t+1}}{1 + \beta},$$ which alternates in sign with size tending to $\norm{\mathbf g}/(1 + \beta)$.

In a long narrow valley the gradient points mostly across the valley and changes sign from step to step, with a small consistent component along the valley floor. Momentum damps the first and accumulates the second, which is exactly what plain gradient descent lacks.

Adam

With $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ (defaults), $\mathbf m_0 = \mathbf s_0 = \mathbf 0$, and all operations on vectors taken entrywise: $$\mathbf m_t = \beta_1\mathbf m_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\,\mathbf g_t, \qquad \mathbf s_t = \beta_2\mathbf s_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\,\mathbf g_t^2,$$ $$\hat{\mathbf m}_t = \frac{\mathbf m_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf s}_t = \frac{\mathbf s_t}{1 - \beta_2^t}, \qquad \theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf s}_t} + \varepsilon} .$$

$\mathbf m_t$ is a momentum (an average of gradients) and $\mathbf s_t$ an average of squared gradients. Each parameter gets its own step size, $\eta$ divided by the typical size of its gradient.

(a) Bias correction. If $\mathbf g_k = \mathbf g$ for all $k$, then $\hat{\mathbf m}_t = \mathbf g$ and $\hat{\mathbf s}_t = \mathbf g^2$ for every $t \geq 1$, and each coordinate moves by $\eta\,|g_j|/(|g_j| + \varepsilon) \approx \eta$.
(b) Scale invariance. Ignoring $\varepsilon$, multiplying the loss by a constant $c > 0$ does not change the Adam iterates.

Proof

Step 1: unroll $\mathbf m_t$. As for momentum, $$\mathbf m_t = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_1^{t-k}\mathbf g = (1 - \beta_1)\,\frac{1 - \beta_1^t}{1 - \beta_1}\,\mathbf g = (1 - \beta_1^t)\,\mathbf g,$$ so $\hat{\mathbf m}_t = \mathbf g$. The same computation with $\beta_2$ and $\mathbf g^2$ gives $\hat{\mathbf s}_t = \mathbf g^2$, and $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf s}_t} = |\mathbf g|$.

Step 2: scale invariance. Replacing $J$ by $cJ$ replaces every $\mathbf g_k$ by $c\,\mathbf g_k$, hence $\hat{\mathbf m}_t$ by $c\,\hat{\mathbf m}_t$ and $\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf s}_t}$ by $c\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf s}_t}$; with $\varepsilon = 0$ the $c$ cancels in the update.

Without the correction, $\mathbf m_1 = 0.1\,\mathbf g_1$ would make the first steps ten times too small. Part (b) explains why Adam needs less tuning than SGD: its step is about $\eta$ per coordinate whatever the scale of the gradients. A learning rate of $10^{-3}$ is the usual starting point.

Learning rate. As in Chapter 2, too large a learning rate makes the iterates diverge and too small a rate makes progress slow. It is common to decrease it during training (a schedule: divide by 10 at fixed epochs, or decay it smoothly), and sometimes to increase it gradually over the first steps (warm-up).

Regularisation

A network with many more parameters than training points can fit the training set perfectly, noise included. As in Chapter 3 we monitor a validation set and use regularisation to control the effective complexity.

Weight decay

Weight decay adds an L2 penalty on the weights (not the biases): $$J_\lambda(\theta) = J(\theta) + \frac\lambda2\sum_{\ell=1}^L \norm{\bW^{(\ell)}}_F^2, \qquad \norm{\bW}_F^2 = \sum_{j,k}W_{jk}^2 .$$

A gradient step on $J_\lambda$ is a shrinkage followed by a gradient step on $J$: $$\bW^{(\ell)} \leftarrow (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\bW^{(\ell)} - \eta\,\frac{\partial J}{\partial\bW^{(\ell)}} .$$

Proof

Step 1: gradient of the penalty. $\partial\big(\frac\lambda2 W_{jk}^2\big)/\partial W_{jk} = \lambda W_{jk}$, so $\partial J_\lambda/\partial\bW^{(\ell)} = \partial J/\partial\bW^{(\ell)} + \lambda\bW^{(\ell)}$.

Step 2: substitute. $$\bW^{(\ell)} - \eta\Big(\frac{\partial J}{\partial\bW^{(\ell)}} + \lambda\bW^{(\ell)}\Big) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\bW^{(\ell)} - \eta\frac{\partial J}{\partial\bW^{(\ell)}} .$$

This is ridge regression's penalty applied to every layer: small weights give smoother functions (a ReLU network with small weights has small slopes). $\lambda$ is chosen on the validation set. In PyTorch it is the weight_decay argument of the optimiser.

Early stopping

Early stopping: evaluate the validation loss regularly during training (e.g. every epoch), keep a copy of the parameters with the lowest validation loss so far, and stop when it has not improved for a fixed number of evaluations (the patience). Return the saved parameters.

Training starts from small random weights, a simple function, and gradually fits more detail, first the main trends and later the noise. Stopping early therefore limits complexity, much like a penalty. For least squares this can be made exact.

Let $\bX\T\bX = Q\operatorname{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_p)Q\T$ with $s_j > 0$, let $\hat\theta$ be the least-squares solution, and run gradient descent on $\frac12\norm{\bX\theta - \by}^2$ from $\theta_0 = \mathbf 0$ with $0 < \eta < 1/s_{\max}$. In the coordinates $\tilde\theta = Q\T\theta$, $$\text{gradient descent after } t \text{ steps: } \tilde\theta_{t,j} = \big(1 - (1 - \eta s_j)^t\big)\,\tilde{\hat\theta}_j, \qquad \text{ridge: } \tilde\theta_{\lambda,j} = \frac{s_j}{s_j + \lambda}\,\tilde{\hat\theta}_j .$$ Both keep the directions with large $s_j$ and shrink those with small $s_j$; for $\eta s_j \ll 1$ both factors are about $t\eta s_j$ and $s_j/\lambda$, so $t$ steps act like $\lambda \approx 1/(\eta t)$.

Proof

Step 1: the error recursion. The gradient is $\bX\T(\bX\theta - \by)$ and $\bX\T\by = \bX\T\bX\hat\theta$ (normal equations), so $$\theta_{t+1} - \hat\theta = \theta_t - \hat\theta - \eta\bX\T\bX(\theta_t - \hat\theta) = (I - \eta\bX\T\bX)(\theta_t - \hat\theta) .$$

Step 2: unroll from $\theta_0 = \mathbf 0$. $$\theta_t = \hat\theta + (I - \eta\bX\T\bX)^t(\mathbf 0 - \hat\theta) = \big(I - (I - \eta\bX\T\bX)^t\big)\hat\theta .$$

Step 3: diagonalise. $(I - \eta\bX\T\bX)^t = Q\operatorname{diag}\big((1 - \eta s_j)^t\big)Q\T$; multiplying by $Q\T$ gives the first formula.

Step 4: ridge. $\theta_\lambda = (\bX\T\bX + \lambda I)^{-1}\bX\T\bX\hat\theta = Q\operatorname{diag}\big(s_j/(s_j + \lambda)\big)Q\T\hat\theta$.

Step 5: small $\eta s_j$. $(1 - \eta s_j)^t \approx 1 - t\eta s_j$ when $t\eta s_j \ll 1$, and $s_j/(s_j + \lambda) \approx s_j/\lambda$ when $s_j \ll \lambda$.

Dropout

Dropout with rate $p$: at each training step and for each example, every hidden unit is kept independently with probability $1 - p$ and set to zero otherwise, and the kept units are scaled up: $$\tilde a_j = \frac{m_j}{1 - p}\,a_j, \qquad m_j = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{with probability } 1 - p, \\ 0 & \text{with probability } p. \end{cases}$$ At test time all units are used, with no scaling. Backpropagation simply multiplies the error signals by the same mask.

For fixed $a_j$, $\E[\tilde a_j] = a_j$: on average, the next layer receives the same input as at test time.

Proof

Step 1. $\E[m_j] = 1 \cdot (1 - p) + 0 \cdot p = 1 - p$.

Step 2. $\E[\tilde a_j] = \frac{a_j}{1 - p}\E[m_j] = a_j$.

Because any unit may be missing, no unit can rely on a few specific others, which discourages complicated co-adaptations that fit noise. Each step trains a different random sub-network, so dropout also behaves like training an ensemble of many thinned networks that share weights, an idea close to bagging in Chapter 8. Typical rates are $p = 0.1$ to $0.5$.

Other tools. Data augmentation enlarges the training set with transformed copies of the inputs (shifted or rotated images, for example). Batch normalisation standardises each unit's pre-activations over the mini-batch and then applies a learned scale and shift, which stabilises and speeds up the training of deep networks.

A practical recipe

  1. Prepare the data. Standardise the inputs (as in Chapter 2); split into training, validation and test sets.
  2. Start simple. One or two hidden layers of moderate width with ReLU, variance-preserving initialisation, the output layer and loss matched to the task.
  3. Optimise. Adam with learning rate about $10^{-3}$ and mini-batches of 32 to 256. First check that the network can overfit a small subset: if not, there is a bug or the learning rate is wrong.
  4. Watch both curves. Training and validation loss per epoch. Training loss not decreasing: change the learning rate or the architecture. Validation loss rising: regularise (weight decay, dropout, more data) and use early stopping.
  5. Tune width, depth, $\lambda$, $p$ and $\eta$ on the validation set; report the test error once at the end.
import copy, torch, torch.nn as nn

model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(p, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Dropout(0.2), nn.Linear(64, 1))
loss_fn = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()                       # sigmoid + binary cross-entropy
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3, weight_decay=1e-4)

best, best_state, patience = float("inf"), None, 0
for epoch in range(200):
    model.train()                                      # dropout on
    for xb, yb in train_loader:                        # mini-batches
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn(model(xb).squeeze(1), yb)
        loss.backward()                                # backpropagation
        opt.step()                                     # Adam update
    model.eval()                                       # dropout off
    with torch.no_grad():
        val = loss_fn(model(X_val).squeeze(1), y_val).item()
    if val < best:
        best, best_state, patience = val, copy.deepcopy(model.state_dict()), 0
    else:
        patience += 1
        if patience == 10:                             # early stopping
            break
model.load_state_dict(best_state)

Strengths, limitations and connections

  • Backpropagation is exact and cheap: about three forward passes for the whole gradient, for any architecture, which is what makes very large networks trainable.
  • Optimisation is not guaranteed. The loss is not convex; results depend on the initialisation, the learning rate and the optimiser. In practice, large networks trained with SGD or Adam usually reach good solutions, but there is no general theorem.
  • Many hyperparameters. Architecture, learning rate, batch size, $\lambda$, dropout rate, number of epochs: all are chosen on validation data.
  • Regularisation is essential once parameters outnumber examples; weight decay is ridge regression for networks, and early stopping behaves like it too.

Connections. Gradient descent and SGD come from Chapter 2, the validation methodology and the L2 penalty from Chapter 3, the matched output losses from Chapter 4. Next, we train networks without labels: an autoencoder learns to reproduce its input through a narrow layer, and in the linear case it recovers PCA.

Key takeaways

  • Backpropagation: $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma'(\mathbf z^{(\ell)}) \odot \bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bb^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$. With matched output and loss, $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}$ is prediction minus target.
  • The whole gradient costs about three forward passes; finite differences cost one per parameter.
  • Error signals shrink or grow geometrically with depth. Random initialisation breaks symmetry; $\Var(w) = 2/n_{\text{in}}$ (ReLU) or $1/n_{\text{in}}$ (tanh) keeps the scale stable.
  • Momentum accumulates consistent gradient directions and damps oscillations; Adam adapts a step size per parameter and is insensitive to the scale of the loss.
  • Weight decay shrinks weights at every step (ridge); early stopping behaves like ridge with $\lambda \approx 1/(\eta t)$; dropout trains random sub-networks and keeps the expected activations unchanged.

Probabilistic view: weight decay as a prior

In Chapter 3, ridge regression was the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate under a Gaussian prior. The same holds for any network trained with a likelihood loss.

Let $N\,J(\theta) = -\sum_i\log p_\theta(y_i \mid \bx_i)$ be the negative log-likelihood (Chapter 10, Extra), and put independent priors $W^{(\ell)}_{jk} \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2)$ on the weights and a flat prior on the biases. Then the MAP estimate minimises $J_\lambda$ with $\lambda = 1/(N\tau^2)$.

Proof

Step 1: the posterior. By Bayes' rule, $p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$, so maximising it is minimising $$-\log p(\text{data} \mid \theta) - \log p(\theta) = N J(\theta) + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_{\ell}\norm{\bW^{(\ell)}}_F^2 + \text{const} .$$

Step 2: divide by $N$. The minimiser does not change: $$J(\theta) + \frac{1}{2N\tau^2}\sum_\ell\norm{\bW^{(\ell)}}_F^2 = J_\lambda(\theta), \qquad \lambda = \frac{1}{N\tau^2} .$$

A tighter prior (small $\tau$) means stronger weight decay, and with more data ($N$ large) the prior matters less. Dropout also has probabilistic readings, for example as an approximation to averaging the predictions of many networks; running dropout at test time and averaging several predictions gives a rough measure of uncertainty.

Replace the Gaussian prior by the Laplace prior $p(w) \propto e^{-|w|/b}$. Which penalty does the MAP estimate use? What does it do to the weights, by analogy with the lasso of Chapter 3?
