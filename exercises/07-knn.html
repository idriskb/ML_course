Exercises: k-nearest neighbours

Practice for Chapter 7. Throughout, $N_k(\bx)$ is the set of the $k$ training points closest to $\bx$ and $N$ is the number of training points. The questions go from conceptual, to hand computations, to harder questions that link several ideas. Every exercise is followed by a collapsed solution: try the question first, then reveal the solution one step at a time.

What you will practise

  • Computing k-NN predictions by hand for regression and classification.
  • Explaining how $k$ controls bias, variance and smoothness.
  • Explaining why features must be scaled and why high dimension hurts k-NN.
  • Choosing $k$ by cross-validation with a suitable metric.

Core conceptual questions

Explain why k-nearest neighbours is considered a non-parametric method. In your answer, discuss:

  1. why the method does not estimate a fixed-dimensional parameter vector in the same way as linear regression;
  2. how prediction is made at a new point;
  3. why the effective complexity of the model depends on the training sample.
Solution

Step 1: (a) no fixed parameter vector. Linear regression summarises the data by $\bw \in \R^{p+1}$. Once $\bw$ is estimated, the training set can be thrown away, and the size of $\bw$ does not change when $N$ grows. k-NN has no training step that produces such a vector: the "model" is the training set itself, so what is stored grows with $N$.

Step 2: (b) prediction at a new point. Given $\bx$, compute its distance to every training point, keep the $k$ closest, and combine their outputs. For regression, the prediction is the average $$\hat f_k(\bx) = \frac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} y_i .$$ For classification, it is the majority class among the $k$ neighbours.

Step 3: (c) complexity grows with the data. The prediction is a different local average in each region of input space where the set of neighbours stays the same. With more training points there are more such regions, so the fitted function can take more distinct shapes. Roughly, there are about $N/k$ separate neighbourhoods (see Section 7.5.1). The complexity is therefore set by $N$ and $k$, not by a fixed number of parameters.

Step 4: conclude. k-NN is non-parametric because it does not assume a fixed functional form with finitely many parameters. Its flexibility grows with the amount of data.

Describe how the prediction rule changes when k-NN is used for (a) regression, (b) binary classification. How would you interpret the output in each case?

Solution

Step 1: (a) regression. Average the neighbours' outputs: $$\hat f_k(\bx) = \frac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} y_i .$$ This is a local estimate of the mean of $y$ for inputs near $\bx$. It is a number on the same scale as $y$ and always lies between the smallest and the largest neighbour value.

Step 2: (b) binary classification. With labels $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, take the proportion of neighbours in class 1, $$\hat p_1(\bx) = \frac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} \mathbf 1\{y_i = 1\},$$ and predict class 1 when $\hat p_1(\bx) > \frac12$ (majority vote).

Step 3: interpretation. $\hat p_1(\bx)$ is the regression rule applied to the 0/1 labels. It is the fraction of nearby training points that belong to class 1, a rough confidence score that only takes the values $0, \frac1k, \frac2k, \dots, 1$. The predicted class is the decision obtained by thresholding this score. The threshold can be moved away from $\frac12$ when one type of error costs more than the other.

Explain qualitatively how increasing $k$ affects (a) bias, (b) variance, (c) sensitivity to noise, (d) smoothness of the prediction function.

Solution

Step 1: (a) bias increases. A larger $k$ averages over points that are farther from $\bx$, where the true function may take different values. Peaks and valleys are flattened. At the extreme $k = N$, the prediction is the constant $\bar y$ everywhere.

Step 2: (b) variance decreases. The prediction averages $k$ outputs, each with its own noise. Averaging $k$ independent noise terms of variance $\sigma^2$ gives $$\Var\Big(\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k \varepsilon_i\Big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{k},$$ so the prediction changes less from one training sample to another.

Step 3: (c) less sensitive to noise. With $k = 1$ a single mislabelled or noisy point decides the prediction in its whole neighbourhood. With large $k$ it is outvoted (classification) or diluted by a factor $1/k$ (regression).

Step 4: (d) smoother predictions. The prediction changes only when the set of neighbours changes, and when one neighbour is swapped the average moves by about $1/k$ of the difference. So larger $k$ gives smaller jumps: smoother regression curves and simpler decision boundaries with fewer small islands.

Step 5: conclude. Small $k$ means low bias and high variance (overfitting); large $k$ means high bias and low variance (underfitting). $k$ is chosen in between by validation.

Intermediate exercises

Consider the one-dimensional dataset $$(x_i, y_i) \in \{(0, 1), (1, 2), (2.5, 2), (4, 5), (7, 4)\}.$$ Use Euclidean distance.

  1. Compute the prediction at $x = 3$ using $k = 1$.
  2. Compute the prediction at $x = 3$ using $k = 3$.
  3. Explain why the two predictions differ.
Solution

Step 1: distances to $x = 3$. In one dimension the distance is $|x_i - 3|$. Sorted from nearest to farthest:

$x_i$$y_i$$|x_i - 3|$
$2.5$$2$$0.5$
$4$$5$$1$
$1$$2$$2$
$0$$1$$3$
$7$$4$$4$

All distances are different, so there are no ties.

Step 2: (a) $k = 1$. The nearest point is $x = 2.5$, so the prediction copies its output: $$\hat f_1(3) = 2 .$$

Step 3: (b) $k = 3$. The three nearest points are $x = 2.5$, $4$ and $1$, with outputs $2$, $5$ and $2$: $$\hat f_3(3) = \frac{2 + 5 + 2}{3} = 3 .$$

Step 4: (c) why they differ. With $k = 1$ the prediction depends on a single point, $x = 2.5$, and ignores the point $x = 4$ just on the other side, whose output $5$ is much higher. With $k = 3$ the prediction averages outputs from both sides of $x = 3$, so it follows the overall trend (the outputs grow with $x$). If the output at $x = 2.5$ were noisy, the 1-NN prediction would carry all of that noise; the 3-NN prediction would carry only a third of it. This is the variance reduction from averaging in .

Suppose the following labelled points are observed in $\R^2$: $$(0, 0) \mapsto 0, \quad (1, 0) \mapsto 0, \quad (0, 1) \mapsto 1, \quad (1, 1) \mapsto 1, \quad (2, 1) \mapsto 1 .$$ For the new point $\bx_* = (1.2, 0.8)$:

  1. determine the predicted class using $k = 1$;
  2. determine the predicted class using $k = 3$;
  3. explain how ties could arise in k-NN classification and give one reasonable tie-breaking strategy.
Solution

Step 1: squared distances. For example, for $(1, 1)$: $$\norm{\bx_* - (1, 1)}^2 = (0.2)^2 + (-0.2)^2 = 0.08 .$$ Squared distances give the same ranking as distances, so we can compare them directly.

$(0, 0)$0$1.44 + 0.64 = 2.08$$1.44$

Step 2: (a) $k = 1$. The nearest point is $(1, 1)$, of class 1, so $$\hat y(\bx_*) = 1 .$$

Step 3: (b) $k = 3$. The three nearest points are $(1, 1)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 1)$. The last two are tied in distance, but both are needed, so the tie does not matter. Their classes are $1, 0, 1$: $$\hat p_1(\bx_*) = \frac23 > \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat y(\bx_*) = 1 .$$

Step 4: (c) how ties arise. There are two kinds. (i) Vote ties: with an even $k$ the votes can split equally. Here $k = 2$ gives the neighbours $(1, 1)$ and one of $(1, 0)$ or $(2, 1)$, so possibly one vote for each class. With more than two classes, ties are possible even for odd $k$. (ii) Distance ties: two candidates for the $k$-th neighbour are at the same distance, as $(1, 0)$ and $(2, 1)$ are here.

Step 5: tie-breaking. Reasonable strategies: use an odd $k$ for two classes (this removes vote ties); break a vote tie with the class of the single nearest neighbour; or use distance-weighted votes (), which make exact ties very unlikely.

A dataset has two features: $x_1$ = annual income in euros, and $x_2$ = customer satisfaction score from 1 to 10. Explain why applying k-NN directly to the raw variables may be problematic. What preprocessing step would you apply, and why?

Solution

Step 1: one feature dominates the distance. Income differences are in thousands of euros, while satisfaction differences are at most 9. Take two customers whose incomes differ by €1 000 and whose scores are 1 and 10: $$d^2 = 1000^2 + 9^2 = 1\,000\,000 + 81 .$$ Satisfaction contributes less than $0.01\%$ of the squared distance.

Step 2: consequence. The neighbours are chosen almost only by income. The satisfaction score is ignored even if it is the most useful feature for prediction. The result also depends on the units: measuring income in thousands of euros would change the neighbours.

Step 3: the fix. Standardise each feature, $x_j \mapsto (x_j - \bar x_j)/s_j$, so that both features have mean 0 and standard deviation 1 and contribute on comparable scales. Compute $\bar x_j$ and $s_j$ on the training data only, and apply them to the validation and test data. A pipeline does this automatically:

from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5))
model.fit(X_train, y_train)

Min–max scaling to $[0, 1]$ is a reasonable alternative. Income is often skewed, so a log transform before standardising can also help.

A shop records the age (years) and annual income (euros) of three customers and whether they bought a product (class 1) or not (class 0):

CustomerAgeIncome (€)Class
A2540 0000
B5041 0001
C4060 0001

A new customer has age 27 and income €41 500.

  1. Compute the Euclidean distance from the new customer to A, B and C using the raw features, and give the 1-NN prediction.
  2. What fraction of the squared distance to B comes from income?
  3. Standardise the features using the training standard deviations, 10 years for age and €10 000 for income (subtracting the means does not change distances, so you only need to divide). Recompute the three distances and the 1-NN prediction.
  4. What does 3-NN predict in each case? Comment.
Solution

Step 1: (a) raw distances. The differences from the new customer $(27, 41\,500)$ are $(-2, -1500)$ for A, $(23, -500)$ for B and $(13, 18\,500)$ for C: $$d_A = \sqrt{2^2 + 1500^2} = \sqrt{2\,250\,004} \approx 1500.0,$$ $$d_B = \sqrt{23^2 + 500^2} = \sqrt{250\,529} \approx 500.5,$$ $$d_C = \sqrt{13^2 + 18\,500^2} \approx 18\,500.0 .$$ The nearest customer is B, so the 1-NN prediction is class 1.

Step 2: (b) income dominates. For B, income contributes $$\frac{250\,000}{250\,529} \approx 99.8\%$$ of the squared distance. Age has almost no say: B is 23 years older than the new customer, but it is chosen because its income is close.

Step 3: (c) standardised distances. Divide age differences by 10 and income differences by 10 000. A: $(-0.2, -0.15)$; B: $(2.3, -0.05)$; C: $(1.3, 1.85)$. Then $$d_A = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.0225} = 0.25, \qquad d_B = \sqrt{5.29 + 0.0025} \approx 2.30, \qquad d_C = \sqrt{1.69 + 3.4225} \approx 2.26 .$$ Now A is by far the nearest, and the 1-NN prediction is class 0.

Step 4: (d) 3-NN. With only three training points, 3-NN uses all of them in both cases: votes $0, 1, 1$, so it predicts class 1 whatever the scaling. Scaling matters through which points are neighbours; when $k$ covers the whole training set the prediction is the majority class and the features are ignored.

Step 5: conclude. The raw 1-NN prediction was decided by the unit of income. After standardising, the new customer's nearest neighbour is A, who is close in both age and income.

Challenging questions

Explain why k-NN often performs poorly in high dimensions, even if the training sample is large. In your answer, discuss distance concentration and the difficulty of defining meaningful neighbourhoods.

Solution

Step 1: neighbourhoods are not local. Take data uniform in $[0, 1]^p$. A sub-cube that contains a fraction $\alpha$ of the data has edge length $$e_p(\alpha) = \alpha^{1/p} .$$ To capture $1\%$ of the data, the edge is $0.1$ for $p = 2$, $0.63$ for $p = 10$ and $0.95$ for $p = 100$. In high dimension, the "neighbourhood" of $k$ points stretches across almost the whole range of every feature (Section 7.6.1).

Step 2: distance concentration. For independent uniform points the squared distance $D$ has mean $p/6$, and its relative spread shrinks: $$\frac{\sqrt{\Var(D)}}{\E[D]} \approx \frac{1.18}{\sqrt p} \xrightarrow[p \to \infty]{} 0 .$$ The nearest and the farthest training points end up at almost the same distance (Section 7.6.3). The ranking of "near" and "far" then carries little information.

Step 3: why more data does not help enough. To keep the neighbourhood edge at a fixed small length $e$, we need a fraction $e^p$ of the data to fall inside it, so the required $N$ grows exponentially with $p$. A sample that is "large" for $p = 2$ is tiny for $p = 50$.

Step 4: irrelevant features. Every feature counts equally in the Euclidean distance. When most features are irrelevant, their noise dominates the distance and the chosen neighbours are close in the useless directions, not in the ones that matter.

Step 5: conclude. In high dimension, neighbours are neither close nor meaningfully different from other points, so local averaging loses its justification. Remedies: feature selection, dimensionality reduction (PCA, Chapter 5), or models that learn which directions matter.

You must choose among $k \in \{1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25\}$ for a classification problem.

  1. Describe a validation strategy for selecting $k$.
  2. Explain which evaluation metric you would use if the dataset were strongly imbalanced.
  3. Why should the test set not be used to choose $k$?
Solution

Step 1: (a) cross-validation on the training set. Split off a test set first and do not touch it. On the remaining data, run $K$-fold cross-validation (for example $K = 5$, stratified so that each fold keeps the class proportions). For each candidate $k$, fit on $K - 1$ folds, score on the held-out fold, and average the $K$ scores. Choose the $k$ with the best average score; if two are close, prefer the larger $k$ (the simpler model). Refit on all training data with that $k$. Scaling belongs inside the pipeline so that it is learnt on the training folds only.

from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, StratifiedKFold

pipe = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("knn", KNeighborsClassifier())])
grid = {"knn__n_neighbors": [1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25]}
search = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=StratifiedKFold(5), scoring="f1")
search.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(search.best_params_)

Step 2: (b) metric for imbalanced data. Accuracy is misleading. With $95\%$ negatives, predicting "negative" always gives $95\%$ accuracy and finds no positives. Use a metric that looks at the minority class: recall (if missing positives is costly), precision, the F1 score, balanced accuracy, or the area under the ROC or precision–recall curve computed from the scores $\hat p_1(\bx)$ (Chapter 4).

Step 3: (c) why not the test set. If $k$ is chosen to maximise the test score, the test set has been used for fitting, since $k$ is a parameter of the model. The test score of the chosen $k$ is then the best of six noisy scores, which is optimistically biased. It no longer estimates performance on new data. The test set must be used once, at the end, for the final model.

Standard k-NN gives equal weight to the $k$ nearest points. A weighted version assigns larger weight to closer observations.

  1. Explain why this may improve predictions.
  2. Propose one reasonable weighting scheme as a function of distance.
  3. Discuss one possible disadvantage of heavily weighting the closest point.
Solution

Step 1: (a) why weight. Among the $k$ neighbours, the closest ones are most similar to $\bx$, so their outputs are the best guide to $f(\bx)$. When the $k$-th neighbour is much farther than the first, equal weights let a distant point pull the prediction away (bias). Weights also make the prediction change continuously as $\bx$ moves, and make exact vote ties unlikely.

Step 2: (b) a scheme. Inverse-distance weights: $$\hat f(\bx) = \frac{\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} w_i\, y_i}{\sum_{i \in N_k(\bx)} w_i}, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{d(\bx, \bx_i) + \varepsilon},$$ with a small $\varepsilon > 0$ to avoid dividing by zero. A smooth alternative is $w_i = \exp\!\big(-d(\bx, \bx_i)^2 / (2h^2)\big)$ with a bandwidth $h$. In scikit-learn, KNeighborsRegressor(weights="distance") uses $1/d$.

Step 3: (c) disadvantage. If the closest point receives almost all the weight, the method behaves like 1-NN whatever $k$ is: the variance returns and one noisy or mislabelled point decides the prediction near it. With $w_i = 1/d$ and $\varepsilon = 0$, a test point that coincides with a training point copies its label exactly. Strong weighting also adds a choice ($\varepsilon$ or $h$) that must be tuned.
