Exercises: tree-based methods

Practice for Chapter 8: single decision trees first, then ensembles of trees. For a node with class proportions $\pi_c$, the Gini impurity is $G = 1 - \sum_c \pi_c^2$ and the entropy is $H = -\sum_c \pi_c\log_2\pi_c$. Every exercise is followed by a collapsed solution: try the question first, then reveal the solution one step at a time.

What you will practise

  • Computing the impurity of a node (variance, Gini, entropy) and the gain of a split.

Decision trees

Background: Chapter 8, Sections 8.1–8.4. For a node $R$ with class proportions $\pi_c$, the Gini impurity is $G = 1 - \sum_c \pi_c^2$ and the entropy is $H = -\sum_c \pi_c\log_2\pi_c$.

Core conceptual questions

Explain what it means to say that a decision tree partitions the input space recursively. What does each internal node represent, and what does each leaf represent?

Solution

Step 1: one split. The root asks one question about one feature, $x_j \le t$. This cuts the input space into two half-spaces, $\{x_j \le t\}$ and $\{x_j > t\}$.

Step 2: recursion. Each half is then split again by its own question, using only the training points that fall in it, and so on. Each new split cuts one existing box into two smaller boxes. The process repeats until a stopping rule is met. The final cells are axis-aligned boxes $R_1, \dots, R_M$ that do not overlap and cover the whole space.

Step 3: internal nodes. An internal node is a test $x_j \le t$ (a feature and a threshold). It corresponds to the box of all inputs that pass the tests on the path from the root to it. It sends each point to one of its two children.

Step 4: leaves. A leaf is one final box $R_m$ together with a constant prediction $c_m$, computed from the training points in $R_m$. The tree's prediction is $$\hat f(\bx) = \sum_{m=1}^M c_m\,\mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_m\} .$$

Describe the prediction rule at a leaf for (a) regression trees, (b) classification trees. How does the notion of impurity differ in the two settings?

Solution

Step 1: (a) regression leaf. Predict the mean of the training outputs in the leaf, $$c_m = \bar y_{R_m} = \frac{1}{|R_m|}\sum_{i \in R_m} y_i ,$$ the constant that minimises the squared error in the leaf.

Step 2: (b) classification leaf. Compute the class proportions $\pi_c$ in the leaf. Predict the majority class $\argmax_c \pi_c$; the proportions themselves serve as class scores (for predict_proba).

Step 3: impurity in regression. Impurity measures the spread of the outputs, through the variance within the node: $$Q(R) = \frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{i \in R}(y_i - \bar y_R)^2 .$$

Step 4: impurity in classification. Impurity measures how mixed the classes are, through Gini $1 - \sum_c \pi_c^2$, entropy $-\sum_c \pi_c\log_2\pi_c$, or the misclassification rate $1 - \max_c \pi_c$. All are 0 for a pure node and largest when the classes are equally frequent.

Step 5: conclude. In both settings a split is chosen to maximise the decrease in impurity, weighted by node sizes. Only the impurity measure changes: spread of numbers for regression, mixing of labels for classification.

A student says: "A decision tree just compares variables to thresholds, so it is basically linear regression in disguise." Explain why this statement is false.

Solution

Step 1: the form of the function. Linear regression predicts $\hat f(\bx) = w_0 + \bw\T\bx$: a plane, where increasing $x_j$ by one unit always changes the prediction by the same amount $w_j$. A tree predicts $$\hat f(\bx) = \sum_m c_m\,\mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_m\},$$ a piecewise constant function. It is flat inside each box and jumps at the thresholds. The indicator functions are not linear in $\bx$.

Step 2: interactions. In a tree, the effect of $x_1$ can depend on $x_2$. In , moving $x_1$ from $4$ to $6$ raises the prediction by $7$ if $x_2 \le 2$ but by $4$ if $x_2 > 2$. A linear model cannot do this without hand-made product features.

Step 3: a counterexample. Let $y = 1$ when $x_1$ and $x_2$ have the same sign and $y = 0$ otherwise (the XOR pattern). A depth-2 tree (split on $x_1 \le 0$, then on $x_2 \le 0$) fits it exactly. Any linear function of $(x_1, x_2)$ thresholded at $\frac12$ gives a straight boundary, which cannot separate the two diagonal quadrants from the other two.

Step 4: conclude. Comparing a variable to a threshold is itself a nonlinear operation (a step function). Combining such tests along paths gives nonlinear, interacting, piecewise constant fits.

Intermediate exercises

A node contains four observations with target values $1, 2, 6, 7$. A candidate split sends $\{1, 2\}$ to the left child and $\{6, 7\}$ to the right child.

  1. Compute the mean target in the parent node.
  2. Compute the sum of squared deviations from the mean in the parent node.
  3. Compute the total within-node sum of squares after the split.
  4. Determine the impurity reduction.
Solution

Step 1: (a) parent mean. $$\bar y = \frac{1 + 2 + 6 + 7}{4} = \frac{16}{4} = 4 .$$

Step 2: (b) parent sum of squares. $$\mathrm{SSE}(R) = (1-4)^2 + (2-4)^2 + (6-4)^2 + (7-4)^2 = 9 + 4 + 4 + 9 = 26 . \tag{1}$$

Step 3: (c) children. The left mean is $1.5$ and the right mean is $6.5$: $$\mathrm{SSE}(R_L) = (0.5)^2 + (0.5)^2 = 0.5, \qquad \mathrm{SSE}(R_R) = (0.5)^2 + (0.5)^2 = 0.5,$$ so the total within-node sum of squares is $$\mathrm{SSE}(R_L) + \mathrm{SSE}(R_R) = 1 . \tag{2}$$

Step 4: (d) reduction. Subtract (2) from (1): $$\mathrm{SSE}(R) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_L) - \mathrm{SSE}(R_R) = 26 - 1 = 25 .$$ In terms of the variance impurity $Q = \mathrm{SSE}/|R|$ of Chapter 8, $Q(R) = 6.5$, the weighted children give $\frac24(0.25) + \frac24(0.25) = 0.25$, and $$\Delta Q = 6.5 - 0.25 = 6.25 = \frac{25}{4} .$$

Step 5: conclude. The split removes $25/26 \approx 96\%$ of the squared error: it separates the low values from the high ones, which is exactly the kind of split a regression tree looks for.

A classification node contains 10 observations: 7 from class 0 and 3 from class 1.

  1. Compute the Gini impurity.
  2. Compute the entropy.
  3. Explain in words what these numbers are measuring.
Solution

Step 1: proportions. $\pi_0 = 0.7$ and $\pi_1 = 0.3$.

Step 2: (a) Gini. $$G = 1 - (0.7^2 + 0.3^2) = 1 - (0.49 + 0.09) = 0.42 .$$ Equivalently, for two classes $G = 2\pi_1(1 - \pi_1) = 2(0.3)(0.7) = 0.42$.

Step 3: (b) entropy. With base-2 logarithms, $\log_2 0.7 \approx -0.515$ and $\log_2 0.3 \approx -1.737$: $$H = -\big(0.7\log_2 0.7 + 0.3\log_2 0.3\big) \approx 0.7(0.515) + 0.3(1.737) \approx 0.881 \text{ bits}.$$

Step 4: (c) meaning. Both measure how mixed the node is. They are 0 for a pure node and largest for a $50/50$ node, where $G = 0.5$ and $H = 1$. So this node is fairly impure. Gini is the probability that two points drawn at random from the node (with replacement) have different labels. Entropy is the average number of bits needed to encode the label of a point from the node. A good split produces children with lower values of these measures.

Suppose a regression tree produces the following rule:

  • if $x_1 \le 5$ and $x_2 \le 2$, predict 3;
  • if $x_1 \le 5$ and $x_2 > 2$, predict 6;
  • if $x_1 > 5$, predict 10.

Draw or describe the regions of the input space in the plane $(x_1, x_2)$ and explain why the fitted function is piecewise constant.

Solution

Step 1: the tree. The root tests $x_1 \le 5$. The right branch ($x_1 > 5$) is a leaf with value 10. The left branch tests $x_2 \le 2$ and ends in two leaves with values 3 and 6.

Step 2: the regions. The three leaves are three boxes that do not overlap and cover the plane: $$R_1 = \{x_1 \le 5,\ x_2 \le 2\}, \quad R_2 = \{x_1 \le 5,\ x_2 > 2\}, \quad R_3 = \{x_1 > 5\} .$$ The vertical line $x_1 = 5$ splits the plane. The horizontal line $x_2 = 2$ splits only the left half.

Step 3: the fitted function. $$\hat f(x_1, x_2) = 3\cdot\mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_1\} + 6\cdot\mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_2\} + 10\cdot\mathbf 1\{\bx \in R_3\} .$$

Step 4: why piecewise constant. Every point is sent to exactly one leaf, and the leaf returns a fixed number (the mean of its training outputs) whatever the exact position of the point in the box. So $\hat f$ is constant on each $R_m$ and changes only at the boundaries. Note also that $x_2$ only matters when $x_1 \le 5$: this is an interaction between the two features.

A node contains 20 points, 10 of class 0 and 10 of class 1. Two candidate splits give the children (counts written as class 0 / class 1):

  • Split A: left $8/2$, right $2/8$;
  • Split B: left $10/4$, right $0/6$.
  1. Compute the weighted Gini impurity of the children and the Gini gain of each split.
  2. Do the same with entropy (base 2).
  3. Compute the weighted misclassification rate of each split. What do you notice?
Solution

Step 1: the parent. With $\pi = 0.5$, $G = 0.5$, $H = 1$ and the misclassification rate is $0.5$.

Step 2: (a) Gini. For two classes $G = 2\pi(1 - \pi)$. Split A: both children have $\pi = 0.2$, so $G = 2(0.2)(0.8) = 0.32$, the weighted impurity is $0.32$ and the gain is $$0.5 - 0.32 = 0.18 .$$ Split B: the left child has $\pi = 4/14$, so $G = 2\cdot\frac{4}{14}\cdot\frac{10}{14} = \frac{80}{196} \approx 0.408$; the right child is pure, $G = 0$. The weighted impurity is $\frac{14}{20}(0.408) + \frac{6}{20}(0) \approx 0.286$ and the gain is $$0.5 - 0.286 = 0.214 .$$

Step 3: (b) entropy. Split A: $H(0.2) = -(0.2\log_2 0.2 + 0.8\log_2 0.8) \approx 0.722$ in both children, so the gain is $1 - 0.722 = 0.278$. Split B: $H(4/14) \approx 0.863$ on the left and $0$ on the right; the weighted entropy is $0.7 \times 0.863 \approx 0.604$ and the gain is $$1 - 0.604 = 0.396 .$$ Both Gini and entropy prefer split B, which creates a pure node.

Step 4: (c) misclassification rate. Split A: each child misclassifies 2 of its 10 points, in total $4/20 = 0.2$. Split B: the left child misclassifies 4 of 14 and the right child none, again $4/20 = 0.2$. The error rate cannot tell the two splits apart, while Gini and entropy reward the pure node in B. This is why trees are grown with Gini or entropy rather than the error rate.

Challenging questions

Why can a fully grown decision tree have very low training error and still generalise poorly? Relate your answer to the bias–variance trade-off.

Solution

Step 1: zero training error. A tree can keep splitting until every leaf is pure. If all inputs are distinct, this can go down to one training point per leaf, and then each leaf predicts its own $y_i$: $$\hat f(\bx_i) = y_i \quad\text{for every } i, \qquad \text{training error} = 0 .$$

Step 2: it has fitted the noise. The outputs are $y_i = f(\bx_i) + \varepsilon_i$. A leaf with one point reproduces $\varepsilon_i$ too, so isolated noisy points create small regions with wrong predictions. New points in those regions get the noise of one training point rather than an average.

Step 3: bias and variance. Such a tree has low bias (it can follow any shape) but very high variance. Each leaf value is based on one or two points, and the splits themselves are unstable: a small change in the data changes the first split, and then the whole tree below it. Two training samples from the same population give very different trees.

Step 4: conclude. The low training error comes from high variance, not from learning the true $f$. Limiting the depth or the leaf size, pruning, or averaging many trees (bagging, random forests) adds a little bias and removes much more variance.

Decision trees are usually built by greedy top-down splitting.

  1. Explain what greedy means in this context.
  2. Why does greedy splitting not guarantee a globally optimal tree?
  3. Why is the greedy strategy still used in practice?
Solution

Step 1: (a) greedy. At each node, the algorithm tries every feature and every threshold and keeps the single split with the largest impurity decrease right now. The split is never revisited, and its effect on later splits is not considered.

Step 2: (b) not globally optimal. The best first split is not necessarily the first split of the best tree. Take the XOR pattern of with equal class counts in the four quadrants. Any single split on $x_1 \le 0$ or $x_2 \le 0$ leaves both children $50/50$, so its gain is zero and a greedy rule may choose an unhelpful split or stop. Yet two splits together classify perfectly. Greedy search judges each split alone, not combinations.

Step 3: (c) why it is used anyway. Finding the best tree of a given size means searching over a number of trees that grows combinatorially with the depth and the number of candidate thresholds; this is computationally infeasible beyond tiny problems. The greedy split only needs sorting each feature, about $O(pN\log N)$ work per level, so it scales to large datasets. It works well in practice, and its weaknesses are reduced by growing deep trees and pruning them back (Section 8.4) or by averaging many trees ().

Tree ensembles

Background: Chapter 8, Sections 8.5–8.7. For $B$ predictions with variance $\tau^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the average has variance $$\rho\,\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\tau^2 . \tag{$\ast$}$$

Random forests and bagging

Explain why bagging is especially useful for decision trees. In your answer, discuss instability and variance reduction.

Solution

Step 1: trees are unstable. A deep tree has low bias but high variance. Its splits depend on small differences in impurity, so changing a few training points can change the root split and then the whole tree below it. Predictions from trees fitted to different samples differ a lot.

Step 2: averaging reduces variance. Bagging fits $B$ trees on $B$ bootstrap samples and averages their predictions (majority vote for classes). By ($\ast$), if the trees were uncorrelated ($\rho = 0$) the variance would fall from $\tau^2$ to $$\frac{\tau^2}{B} .$$ In practice $\rho > 0$, but the variance still falls to $\rho\tau^2 + (1 - \rho)\tau^2/B$, below $\tau^2$.

Step 3: bias is unchanged. Each bagged tree has about the same bias as one deep tree, and averaging does not change the expected prediction. So bagging keeps the low bias of deep trees and removes much of their variance.

Step 4: conclude. Bagging helps most for methods with high variance and low bias, which describes deep trees. For a stable method such as linear regression, the bootstrap fits are nearly identical ($\rho \approx 1$) and averaging gains almost nothing.

What is a bootstrap sample? Why is training many trees on bootstrap samples different from simply duplicating the original dataset many times?

Solution

Step 1: definition. A bootstrap sample is obtained by drawing $N$ points from the $N$ training points uniformly with replacement. Some points appear several times and others not at all.

Step 2: how many points are left out. A given point is missed by one draw with probability $1 - \frac1N$, so it is missed by all $N$ draws with probability $$\Big(1 - \frac1N\Big)^N \xrightarrow[N \to \infty]{} e^{-1} \approx 0.368 .$$ Each bootstrap sample therefore contains about $63.2\%$ of the distinct training points. The other $36.8\%$ are "out of bag" and can be used to estimate the test error.

Step 3: copying the data gives identical trees. The tree algorithm is deterministic (apart from ties). Fitting it to the same dataset $B$ times, or to the dataset repeated $B$ times (which has the same proportions and therefore the same impurities and splits), gives $B$ identical trees. Then $\rho = 1$ and ($\ast$) gives $$\rho\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\tau^2 = \tau^2 ,$$ no variance reduction at all.

Step 4: conclude. Bootstrap samples differ from each other, so the trees differ. They imitate drawing new training sets from the population, and averaging over that variation is what reduces the variance.

A random forest modifies bagging by selecting only a random subset of features at each split.

  1. Why does this reduce correlation between trees?
  2. Why can reducing correlation improve the ensemble?
  3. Why are random forests often grown with deep trees rather than heavily pruned trees?
Solution

Step 1: (a) less correlation. In plain bagging, if one feature is much stronger than the others, almost every bootstrap tree splits on it at the root and on the same few features below. The trees look alike and their predictions are highly correlated. If each split may use only $m \lt p$ randomly chosen features (for example $m = \sqrt p$), the strong feature is not available at many splits, so other features get used and the trees take different shapes.

Step 2: (b) why lower correlation helps. In ($\ast$), the second term vanishes as $B$ grows, but the first does not: $$\lim_{B \to \infty}\Big(\rho\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\tau^2\Big) = \rho\,\tau^2 .$$ With bagging alone, adding trees cannot push the variance below $\rho\tau^2$. Lowering $\rho$ lowers this floor. Each tree may become slightly worse (larger $\tau^2$, a little more bias), but the drop in $\rho$ usually wins.

Step 3: (c) deep trees. Averaging reduces variance but does not reduce bias: the forest has about the bias of its individual trees. So the individual trees should have low bias, which means deep, unpruned trees. Their high variance is exactly what averaging removes. Heavily pruned trees would add bias that the forest cannot remove.

Each tree in an ensemble has prediction variance $\tau^2 = 4$ at some point $\bx$.

  1. Bagged trees have pairwise correlation $\rho = 0.3$. Compute the variance of the average for $B = 1, 10, 100$ trees and its limit as $B \to \infty$.
  2. A random forest lowers the correlation to $\rho = 0.1$. Repeat (a).
  3. For a training set of $N = 10$ points, compute the probability that a given point is left out of a bootstrap sample, and the expected number of distinct points in the sample.
Solution

Step 1: the formula. The variance of the average of $B$ trees is $$\rho\,\tau^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\tau^2 .$$

Step 2: (a) $\rho = 0.3$. Here $\rho\tau^2 = 1.2$ and $(1 - \rho)\tau^2 = 2.8$: $$B = 1:\ 4, \qquad B = 10:\ 1.2 + 0.28 = 1.48, \qquad B = 100:\ 1.2 + 0.028 = 1.228, \qquad B \to \infty:\ 1.2 .$$ Going from 10 to 100 trees gains little: the variance cannot go below $1.2$.

Step 3: (b) $\rho = 0.1$. Now $\rho\tau^2 = 0.4$ and $(1 - \rho)\tau^2 = 3.6$: $$B = 1:\ 4, \qquad B = 10:\ 0.76, \qquad B = 100:\ 0.436, \qquad B \to \infty:\ 0.4 .$$ The floor is three times lower. Even if each forest tree were a little worse (larger $\tau^2$), the lower correlation would usually win.

Step 4: (c) the bootstrap with $N = 10$. A point is missed by one draw with probability $0.9$, so by all ten draws with probability $$0.9^{10} \approx 0.349 .$$ The expected number of distinct points is $10 \times (1 - 0.349) \approx 6.5$. This is already close to the large-$N$ values $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$ and $63.2\%$.

Boosting

Explain the key idea behind boosting. How is it conceptually different from bagging?

Solution

Step 1: the idea of boosting. Build the model as a sum of small trees, added one at a time: $$f_M(\bx) = f_0 + \nu\sum_{m=1}^M h_m(\bx) .$$ Each new tree $h_m$ is fitted to what the current model $f_{m-1}$ still gets wrong (for squared loss, the residuals). Each tree corrects the errors of the trees before it.

Step 2: what it reduces. The individual trees are shallow (high bias, low variance). Adding many of them, each correcting the previous errors, mainly reduces bias.

Step 3: the contrast with bagging.

In gradient boosting for regression with squared loss, each new tree is fitted to the residuals of the current model.

  1. Explain why the residuals are the relevant signal here.
  2. What happens if you add too many trees?
  3. What is the role of the learning rate?
Solution

Step 1: (a) residuals are what is missing. If the current model is $f_{m-1}$, the residual $r_i = y_i - f_{m-1}(\bx_i)$ is exactly the amount to add at $\bx_i$ to reach $y_i$. A tree that predicts the residuals therefore improves the fit where it is worst. Also, for $L(y, f) = \frac12(y - f)^2$, $$-\frac{\partial L(y_i, f)}{\partial f}\Big|_{f = f_{m-1}(\bx_i)} = y_i - f_{m-1}(\bx_i) = r_i ,$$ so fitting the residuals is a gradient descent step on the training loss, with the tree generalising the step to new inputs.

Step 2: (b) too many trees. Each stage decreases the training error (Section 8.6.1), so with enough trees the model starts to fit the noise in the residuals. The test error first falls, reaches a minimum, then rises: the model overfits. The number of trees $M$ is chosen by validation or early stopping (stop when the validation error has not improved for a while).

Step 3: (c) the learning rate. The update is $f_m = f_{m-1} + \nu\,h_m$ with $0 \lt \nu \le 1$. A small $\nu$ (e.g. $0.05$) adds only a fraction of each tree, so the model moves in small, cautious steps and no single tree can fit the noise strongly. It needs more trees, but the minimum of the test error is usually lower and wider, so the exact choice of $M$ matters less. $\nu$ and $M$ are tuned together: halving $\nu$ roughly doubles the number of trees needed.

You are given a medium-sized tabular dataset and need a strong baseline quickly.

  1. Give one reason to start with a random forest.
  2. Give one reason to try gradient boosting afterwards.
  3. Explain one trade-off between the two approaches.
Solution

Step 1: (a) start with a random forest. It works well with default settings: adding trees never hurts, deep trees need no depth tuning, and only $m$ (max_features) and the leaf size matter much. It needs no feature scaling, trains in parallel, and the out-of-bag score gives a free estimate of the test error. It is a strong baseline with little effort.

Step 2: (b) then try boosting. Because boosting reduces bias step by step, a well-tuned gradient boosting model is often more accurate than a random forest on tabular data. It is worth trying once the baseline is in place.

Step 3: (c) a trade-off. Boosting has more hyperparameters that interact (learning rate, number of trees, depth, subsampling) and can overfit if they are chosen badly, so it needs careful validation and early stopping. Its trees are fitted one after another, so training cannot be parallelised across trees. The forest is safer and faster to set up; boosting can be more accurate but costs more tuning time and has a higher risk of overfitting.
