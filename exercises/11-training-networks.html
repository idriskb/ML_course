Exercises: backpropagation and training

Practice for Chapter 11. The error signal of layer $\ell$ is $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\mathbf z^{(\ell)}$; backpropagation computes it from the output backwards with $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)\prime}(\mathbf z^{(\ell)}) \odot \bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$, and the gradients are $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bb^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$. Every exercise is followed by a collapsed solution: try the question first, then reveal the solution one step at a time.

What you will practise

  • Running backpropagation by hand and checking a gradient with a finite difference.
  • Computing output error signals for squared error, sigmoid and softmax outputs.
  • Predicting how the learning rate affects gradient descent, and computing momentum and Adam steps.
  • Computing the effect of weight decay, dropout and early stopping.
  • Diagnosing common training failures from loss curves.

Core conceptual questions

Training a network minimises $J(\theta) = \frac1N\sum_i \mathcal L_i(\theta)$ by gradient descent.

  1. Why is there no closed-form solution, as there is for linear regression?
  2. What does it mean that $J$ is not convex, and what are two practical consequences?
  3. Why is computing each partial derivative by a finite difference not an option for a real network?
Solution

Step 1: (a) no closed form. For linear regression the gradient is linear in $\bw$, so setting it to zero gives a linear system (the normal equations). In a network, the parameters enter through compositions of non-linear functions, $f_\theta(\bx) = \sigma(\bW^{(2)}\sigma(\bW^{(1)}\bx + \bb^{(1)}) + \dots)$, and $\nabla J(\theta) = \mathbf 0$ is a non-linear system with no formula for its solution. We must iterate.

Step 2: (b) non-convexity. A convex function has no bad local minima: every local minimum is global. $J$ is not convex: for instance, swapping two hidden units gives the same function and the same loss, but the midpoint of the two parameter vectors is usually a worse network. Consequences: (i) gradient descent can end in different solutions depending on the initialisation and the random mini-batches, so results vary from run to run; (ii) the initialisation and the optimiser matter, and it is worth trying several seeds.

Step 3: (c) finite differences. One central difference costs two forward passes per parameter. A network with $10^5$ parameters and a forward pass of about $10^5$ multiplications needs about $$2 \times 10^5 \times 10^5 = 2 \times 10^{10}$$ operations for one approximate gradient. Backpropagation gives the exact gradient for about three forward passes, $3 \times 10^5$ operations.

  1. What does the error signal $\delta^{(\ell)}_j$ measure, in words?
  2. Why does the computation go backwards, from the output to the input?
  3. Why must the forward pass store the activations of every layer, and what does this cost in memory?
  4. Why is the gradient of a weight $W^{(\ell)}_{jk}$ the product $\delta^{(\ell)}_j a^{(\ell-1)}_k$?
Solution

Step 1: (a) meaning. $\delta^{(\ell)}_j = \partial\mathcal L/\partial z^{(\ell)}_j$ is the sensitivity of the loss to the pre-activation of unit $j$: if $z^{(\ell)}_j$ increases by a small $\varepsilon$, the loss changes by about $\delta^{(\ell)}_j\varepsilon$. A large $|\delta|$ means the unit matters a lot for the current error; its sign says in which direction to push the unit.

Step 2: (b) backwards. The loss depends on layer $\ell$ only through layer $\ell + 1$. By the chain rule, $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$ is computed from $\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)}$: $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)} = \sigma^{(\ell)\prime}\big(\mathbf z^{(\ell)}\big) \odot \bW^{(\ell+1)\top}\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell+1)} .$$ Starting from the output, where $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}$ is known directly, each layer reuses the work of the layer above. Going forwards would require a separate chain-rule computation for every parameter.

Step 3: (c) memory. The backward formulas use $\mathbf z^{(\ell)}$ (in $\sigma'$) and $\ba^{(\ell-1)}$ (in the weight gradient), which were computed in the forward pass. Storing them costs memory proportional to the total number of units times the batch size. This, not the number of parameters, is often what limits the batch size on a GPU.

Step 4: (d) the weight gradient. $W^{(\ell)}_{jk}$ affects the loss only through $z^{(\ell)}_j = \sum_k W^{(\ell)}_{jk}a^{(\ell-1)}_k + b^{(\ell)}_j$, and $\partial z^{(\ell)}_j/\partial W^{(\ell)}_{jk} = a^{(\ell-1)}_k$. So $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial W^{(\ell)}_{jk}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j}\cdot\frac{\partial z^{(\ell)}_j}{\partial W^{(\ell)}_{jk}} = \delta^{(\ell)}_j\,a^{(\ell-1)}_k .$$ A weight changes a lot when its unit matters (large $\delta$) and its input is active (large $a$).

Explain in words what problem each of the following addresses, compared with plain gradient descent on the full training set: (a) mini-batch stochastic gradient descent; (b) momentum; (c) Adam.

Solution

Step 1: (a) mini-batches. The full gradient averages over all $N$ examples, which is expensive when $N$ is large. A mini-batch of, say, 64 examples gives an unbiased but noisy estimate at a tiny fraction of the cost, so we can take many more steps for the same computation. The noise also helps the optimiser move away from poor flat regions.

Step 2: (b) momentum. In a long narrow valley the gradient points mostly across the valley, so gradient descent zig-zags and advances slowly along it. Momentum keeps a running sum of past gradients, $\bv_{t+1} = \beta\bv_t + \mathbf g_t$: the components that change sign (across the valley) cancel, and the consistent component (along the valley) builds up. It also smooths mini-batch noise.

Step 3: (c) Adam. Different parameters can have gradients of very different sizes, and a single learning rate is too large for some and too small for others. Adam divides each parameter's average gradient by the square root of its average squared gradient, so every parameter moves by roughly $\eta$ per step whatever the scale of its gradient. It also includes momentum. It works well with little tuning, which is why it is the default for networks.

For weight decay, early stopping and dropout, explain how each one reduces overfitting, and what hyperparameter controls it.

Solution

Step 1: weight decay. It adds $\frac\lambda2\sum_\ell\norm{\bW^{(\ell)}}_F^2$ to the loss, as ridge does for linear regression. Each step shrinks the weights by the factor $1 - \eta\lambda$ before the usual gradient step, which keeps the weights small and the network's function smoother. Controlled by $\lambda$.

Step 2: early stopping. Training starts from small weights and the network becomes more complex as training goes on. Monitoring the validation loss and keeping the parameters where it was lowest stops before the network starts fitting noise. Controlled by the patience (and the maximum number of epochs); it costs nothing extra and is almost always used.

Step 3: dropout. During training each hidden unit is set to zero with probability $p$, at random, at every step. The network cannot rely on any single unit or on precise combinations of units, so it learns redundant, more robust features; it behaves like an average of many thinned sub-networks. Controlled by the rate $p$ (typically $0.1$ to $0.5$).

Step 4: conclude. All three trade a little training fit for lower variance. Their strength ($\lambda$, patience, $p$) is chosen on a validation set, never on the test set.

Intermediate exercises

A network has two inputs, two ReLU hidden units and an identity output, with loss $\mathcal L = \frac12(\hat y - y)^2$ and parameters $$\bW^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \bb^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \bW^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b^{(2)} = 0.5 .$$ The training example is $\bx = (1, 2)$, $y = 3$.

  1. Run the forward pass and compute the loss.
  2. Compute $\delta^{(2)}$ and $\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}$.
  3. Compute all the gradients: $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(2)}$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial b^{(2)}$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(1)}$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bb^{(1)}$.
  4. Take one gradient step with $\eta = 0.1$ and compute the new prediction and loss.
  5. Check $\partial\mathcal L/\partial W^{(1)}_{11}$ with a central finite difference.
Solution

Step 1: (a) forward pass. $$\mathbf z^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 2 \\ 1 - 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ba^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat y = 1(3) + 2(0) + 0.5 = 3.5,$$ $$\mathcal L = \tfrac12(3.5 - 3)^2 = 0.125 .$$

Step 2: (b) error signals. For an identity output with squared error, $$\delta^{(2)} = \hat y - y = 0.5 .$$ Then, with $\operatorname{ReLU}'(3) = 1$ and $\operatorname{ReLU}'(-1) = 0$, $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \odot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}(0.5) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .$$

Step 3: (c) gradients. Using $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}\ba^{(\ell-1)\top}$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bb^{(\ell)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}$: $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(2)}} = 0.5\,(3,\ 0) = (1.5,\ 0), \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial b^{(2)}} = 0.5,$$ $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(1)}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}(1,\ 2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bb^{(1)}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .$$ The inactive unit 2 receives no gradient on its incoming weights, and its outgoing weight gets none either (its activation is 0).

Step 4: (d) one step. Subtract $0.1$ times each gradient: $$\bW^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.95 & 0.9 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \bb^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.05 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \bW^{(2)} = (0.85,\ 2), \quad b^{(2)} = 0.45 .$$ Now $z^{(1)}_1 = 0.95 + 1.8 - 0.05 = 2.7$ and unit 2 is still off, so $$\hat y = 0.85(2.7) + 0.45 = 2.745, \qquad \mathcal L = \tfrac12(0.255)^2 \approx 0.033 .$$ The loss fell from $0.125$ to $0.033$. (The prediction overshot slightly below 3: the step was a little too large for this example.)

Step 5: (e) finite-difference check. Changing $W^{(1)}_{11}$ by $\pm\varepsilon$ changes $z^{(1)}_1$ and $\hat y$ by $\pm\varepsilon$ (since $x_1 = 1$ and $W^{(2)}_1 = 1$), so $\hat y = 3.5 \pm \varepsilon$ and $$\frac{\tfrac12(0.5 + \varepsilon)^2 - \tfrac12(0.5 - \varepsilon)^2}{2\varepsilon} = \frac{2 \cdot 0.5\,\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon} = 0.5,$$ in agreement with backpropagation.

The last hidden layer of a three-class network has activations $\ba^{(L-1)} = (1, 2)$, and the output logits are $\mathbf z^{(L)} = (1, 0, -1)$. The true class is $y = 2$. The loss is the cross-entropy $-\log p_y$.

  1. Compute $\mathbf p = \operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf z^{(L)})$ and the loss.
  2. Compute $\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} = \mathbf p - \mathbf e_y$. Why do its entries sum to 0?
  3. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the $3 \times 2$ output weight matrix.
  4. After a small gradient step, which logits go up and which go down?
Solution

Step 1: (a) probabilities. $e^1 + e^0 + e^{-1} \approx 2.718 + 1 + 0.368 = 4.086$, so $$\mathbf p \approx (0.665,\ 0.245,\ 0.090), \qquad \mathcal L = -\log 0.245 \approx 1.41 .$$

Step 2: (b) the error signal. With $\mathbf e_2 = (0, 1, 0)$: $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)} = (0.665,\ 0.245 - 1,\ 0.090) = (0.665,\ -0.755,\ 0.090) .$$ The entries sum to $\sum_k p_k - 1 = 0$, because the probabilities sum to 1. Raising all logits together does not change the softmax, so the gradient has no component along $(1, 1, 1)$.

Step 3: (c) the weight gradient. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(L)} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}\ba^{(L-1)\top}$: $$\begin{pmatrix} 0.665 \\ -0.755 \\ 0.090 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.665 & 1.330 \\ -0.755 & -1.510 \\ 0.090 & 0.180 \end{pmatrix} .$$

Step 4: (d) direction. The step subtracts $\eta$ times the gradient. Row 2 has a negative gradient, so the weights into logit 2 increase and $z_2$ goes up; rows 1 and 3 have positive gradients, so $z_1$ and $z_3$ go down, $z_1$ the most because the network wrongly gave class 1 the highest probability. The step moves probability towards the true class.

Gradient descent minimises $J(\theta) = \frac12\theta_1^2 + 5\theta_2^2$ from $\theta = (1, 1)$.

  1. Show that one step multiplies $\theta_1$ by $1 - \eta$ and $\theta_2$ by $1 - 10\eta$.
  2. Compute three steps for $\eta = 0.15$ and for $\eta = 0.25$.
  3. For which $\eta$ does gradient descent converge? Why is the progress along $\theta_1$ then slow?
  4. How do momentum and Adam help here?
Solution

Step 1: (a) one step. $\nabla J = (\theta_1,\ 10\theta_2)$, so $$\theta_1 \leftarrow \theta_1 - \eta\theta_1 = (1 - \eta)\theta_1, \qquad \theta_2 \leftarrow \theta_2 - 10\eta\theta_2 = (1 - 10\eta)\theta_2 .$$

Step 2: (b) three steps.

Step$\eta = 0.15$: $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$$\eta = 0.25$: $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$
1$(0.85,\ -0.5)$$(0.75,\ -1.5)$
2$(0.723,\ 0.25)$$(0.563,\ 2.25)$
3$(0.614,\ -0.125)$$(0.422,\ -3.375)$

With $\eta = 0.15$, $\theta_2$ flips sign but shrinks: it zig-zags across the valley. With $\eta = 0.25$ the factor is $-1.5$ and $\theta_2$ explodes: gradient descent diverges.

Step 3: (c) convergence. We need $|1 - \eta| \lt 1$ and $|1 - 10\eta| \lt 1$, that is $$0 \lt \eta \lt 0.2 .$$ The steep direction $\theta_2$ caps the learning rate. The flat direction $\theta_1$ would tolerate a much larger step, but with $\eta \lt 0.2$ it only shrinks by a factor above $0.8$ per step: with $\eta = 0.15$, $\theta_1$ still equals $0.85^{20} \approx 0.04$ after 20 steps.

Step 4: (d) momentum and Adam. Momentum averages the gradients: the zig-zagging $\theta_2$ components cancel, while the steady $\theta_1$ components add up and speed up progress along the valley. Adam divides each coordinate's step by its own gradient scale, so both directions move at a similar rate and the steep direction no longer dictates a tiny learning rate for the flat one.

A parameter receives the same gradient $g = 0.5$ at every step. Use the default Adam settings $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, and ignore $\varepsilon$.

  1. Compute $m_1$, $s_1$, the bias-corrected $\hat m_1$, $\hat s_1$, and the first step size in units of $\eta$.
  2. Repeat for step 2.
  3. What would the first step be without the bias correction?
  4. Repeat (a) with $g = 0.005$. What do you notice?
Solution

Step 1: (a) step 1. Starting from $m_0 = s_0 = 0$: $$m_1 = 0.1 \times 0.5 = 0.05, \qquad s_1 = 0.001 \times 0.25 = 0.00025,$$ $$\hat m_1 = \frac{0.05}{1 - 0.9} = 0.5, \qquad \hat s_1 = \frac{0.00025}{1 - 0.999} = 0.25, \qquad \text{step} = \eta\,\frac{\hat m_1}{\sqrt{\hat s_1}} = \eta\,\frac{0.5}{0.5} = \eta .$$

Step 2: (b) step 2. $$m_2 = 0.9(0.05) + 0.05 = 0.095, \qquad s_2 = 0.999(0.00025) + 0.00025 \approx 0.0004998,$$ $$\hat m_2 = \frac{0.095}{1 - 0.9^2} = \frac{0.095}{0.19} = 0.5, \qquad \hat s_2 = \frac{0.0004998}{1 - 0.999^2} = \frac{0.0004998}{0.001999} \approx 0.25,$$ so the step is again $\eta$. With a constant gradient, the corrected averages equal $g$ and $g^2$ exactly.

Step 3: (c) without correction. $$\eta\,\frac{m_1}{\sqrt{s_1}} = \eta\,\frac{0.05}{0.0158} \approx 3.16\,\eta .$$ Both averages start at 0, but $s$ forgets its start much more slowly than $m$, so $\sqrt{s_t}$ is underestimated more and the early steps would be about 3 to 5 times too large. The bias correction removes this.

Step 4: (d) a 100 times smaller gradient. $\hat m_1 = 0.005$ and $\hat s_1 = 0.000025$, so the step is $\eta\cdot 0.005/0.005 = \eta$ again. Adam's step does not depend on the scale of the gradient, only on its consistency: a parameter with tiny gradients moves as fast as one with large gradients.

  1. A weight equals $W = 2$, and $\partial J/\partial W = 0.5$. Compute one gradient step with $\eta = 0.1$, without weight decay and with weight decay $\lambda = 0.1$.
  2. If the data gradient were 0 for 100 steps, what would weight decay alone do to $W = 2$?
  3. A hidden layer has activations $\ba = (2,\ 0.4,\ 1,\ 3)$ and dropout rate $p = 0.25$. The random mask is $(1, 0, 1, 1)$. What does the next layer receive? What does it receive at test time?
  4. Check that the expected input to the next layer is the same in training and at test time, for the sum of the four values.
Solution

Step 1: (a) one step. Without decay, $W \leftarrow 2 - 0.1(0.5) = 1.95$. With decay the weight is first shrunk by $1 - \eta\lambda = 0.99$: $$W \leftarrow 0.99 \times 2 - 0.1 \times 0.5 = 1.98 - 0.05 = 1.93 .$$

Step 2: (b) decay alone. Each step multiplies $W$ by 0.99: $$2 \times 0.99^{100} \approx 0.73 .$$ A weight that the data do not support shrinks steadily towards 0.

Step 3: (c) dropout. Kept units are divided by $1 - p = 0.75$: $$\tilde\ba = \Big(\frac{2}{0.75},\ 0,\ \frac{1}{0.75},\ \frac{3}{0.75}\Big) \approx (2.667,\ 0,\ 1.333,\ 4) .$$ At test time there is no dropout and no scaling: the next layer receives $(2, 0.4, 1, 3)$.

Step 4: (d) expectations. Each $\tilde a_j$ equals $a_j/0.75$ with probability 0.75 and 0 otherwise, so $\E[\tilde a_j] = 0.75 \cdot a_j/0.75 = a_j$. Hence the expected sum is $$2 + 0.4 + 1 + 3 = 6.4,$$ the test-time sum. This particular mask gave $8.0$: individual training steps are noisy, but correct on average.

  1. With a patience of 3 epochs, after which epoch does training stop, and which parameters are returned?
  2. Repeat with a patience of 1 epoch. What is the drawback of a very small patience?
  3. From which epoch is the network clearly overfitting? Why should the final performance be reported on a separate test set rather than as the best validation loss?
Solution

Step 1: (a) patience 3. The best validation loss is updated at epochs 1 to 5 (0.50), not at 6 (0.51), then again at 7 (0.49). Epochs 8, 9 and 10 do not improve on 0.49, so after the third of them, epoch 10, training stops, and the parameters saved at epoch 7 are returned.

Step 2: (b) patience 1. Epoch 6 does not improve on epoch 5, so training stops after epoch 6 and returns the parameters of epoch 5 (validation loss 0.50). The better model at epoch 7 is never reached. The validation loss is noisy, and a tiny patience mistakes a small bump for the start of overfitting.

Step 3: (c) overfitting. From epoch 8 the training loss keeps falling (0.24, 0.21, 0.18) while the validation loss rises steadily (0.53, 0.55, 0.58): the network is fitting noise in the training set. The best validation loss was chosen as the minimum over many epochs, so it is optimistically biased, as when a hyperparameter is chosen on a validation set. An untouched test set gives an honest estimate.

Challenging questions

Let $f(\bx) = \bW^{(2)}\operatorname{ReLU}(\bW^{(1)}\bx)$ with $\bx \in \R^p$, $\bW^{(1)} \in \R^{m \times p}$, $\bW^{(2)} \in \R^{1 \times m}$ (no biases), and $\mathcal L = \frac12(f(\bx) - y)^2$.

  1. Derive $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(2)}$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(1)}$ using the chain rule, and check that the shapes match the parameters.
  2. Show that if a hidden unit is inactive for every training example, neither its incoming weights nor its outgoing weight ever change.
  3. Show that if all entries of $\bW^{(2)}$ start at 0, then $\bW^{(1)}$ does not change at the first step. Does training get stuck?
Solution

Step 1: (a) forward quantities. Write $\mathbf z = \bW^{(1)}\bx \in \R^m$, $\mathbf h = \operatorname{ReLU}(\mathbf z)$, $f = \bW^{(2)}\mathbf h$, and $r = f - y$. Then $\partial\mathcal L/\partial f = r$.

Step 2: (a) output weights. $f = \sum_j W^{(2)}_j h_j$, so $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(2)}} = r\,\mathbf h\T \in \R^{1 \times m} .$$

Step 3: (a) hidden weights. The error signal of the hidden layer is $$\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\mathbf z} = \mathbf 1\{\mathbf z > 0\} \odot \bW^{(2)\top} r \in \R^m,$$ and $z_j = \sum_k W^{(1)}_{jk}x_k$ gives $\partial z_j/\partial W^{(1)}_{jk} = x_k$, so $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\bW^{(1)}} = \boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}\bx\T \in \R^{m \times p} . \tag{1}$$ Both shapes match $\bW^{(2)}$ and $\bW^{(1)}$.

Step 4: (b) a dead unit. If $z_j \le 0$ for every example, then $h_j = 0$, so the $j$-th entry of $r\mathbf h\T$ is 0, and $\delta^{(1)}_j = 0$, so row $j$ of (1) is 0. Averaging over examples keeps them 0. The unit's weights never change and it stays dead: this is the "dying ReLU" problem.

Step 5: (c) zero output weights. With $\bW^{(2)} = \mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)} = \mathbf 0$, so by (1) $\bW^{(1)}$ does not move at step 1. But $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\bW^{(2)} = r\mathbf h\T$ is not zero (as long as $\mathbf h \ne \mathbf 0$ and $r \ne 0$), so $\bW^{(2)}$ becomes non-zero and from step 2 on $\bW^{(1)}$ moves too. Training is not stuck, unlike when all weights start at 0, where every hidden activation is 0 and nothing but the biases can move.

A network has $L = 10$ layers, and every weight matrix has norm $\norm{\bW^{(k)}} = 2$. Use the bound $\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(\ell)}} \le s^{L-\ell}\big(\prod_{k=\ell+1}^L\norm{\bW^{(k)}}\big)\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}}$, where $s$ bounds $|\sigma'|$.

  1. For sigmoid hidden units ($s = \frac14$), bound $\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}}/\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}}$.
  2. For ReLU hidden units ($s = 1$), compute the same bound. What can go wrong now?
  3. What choice of weight scale makes the ReLU bound equal to 1, and how is this related to the initialisation $\Var(w) = 2/n$?
Solution

Step 1: (a) sigmoid. From $\ell = 1$ to $L = 10$ there are 9 factors, each at most $s\norm{\bW} = \frac14 \cdot 2 = \frac12$: $$\frac{\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(1)}}}{\norm{\boldsymbol\delta^{(L)}}} \le \Big(\frac12\Big)^9 \approx 0.002 .$$ The first layer's gradients are at least 500 times smaller than the last layer's: it learns very slowly (vanishing gradients).

Step 2: (b) ReLU. Each factor is at most $1 \cdot 2 = 2$, so the bound is $$2^9 = 512 .$$ The bound is only an upper bound, but it shows the risk: when the weights are too large, error signals can grow layer after layer (exploding gradients), causing huge steps and a loss that jumps or becomes nan.

Step 3: (c) the right scale. The product is $1$ when $s\norm{\bW^{(k)}} = 1$ for every layer; signals then neither shrink nor grow systematically. The initialisation $\Var(w) = 2/n$ for ReLU layers has the same aim in the forward direction: it keeps the variance of the pre-activations constant from layer to layer (the factor $\frac12$ lost by ReLU is compensated by the 2). Starting at this scale keeps both activations and error signals in a healthy range.

A binary classifier is trained with binary cross-entropy. For each symptom, give a likely cause and a fix.

  1. After a few steps the loss becomes nan.
  2. The training loss stays at about $0.693$ for many epochs.
  3. The training loss falls to almost 0 while the validation loss rises after epoch 15.
  4. Training and validation losses are both high and close to each other, and stop improving.
Solution

Step 1: (a) nan. The steps are too large: the parameters diverge as in with $\eta = 0.25$, or gradients explode (). Another cause is taking $\log$ of a probability rounded to 0. Fixes: lower the learning rate, standardise the inputs, use BCEWithLogitsLoss on logits rather than log of a sigmoid output.

Step 2: (b) stuck at $0.693$. $0.693 = \log 2$ is the loss of always predicting $p = 0.5$: the network has learnt nothing. Possible causes: learning rate far too small, all weights initialised to 0 (symmetry: nothing can move), dead ReLU units, or labels not matched to the inputs. Fixes: random initialisation at the right scale, a larger learning rate (or Adam), and a check that a tiny model can overfit a handful of examples.

Step 3: (c) overfitting. The network has enough capacity to memorise the training set. Fixes: early stopping at the minimum of the validation loss, weight decay, dropout, a smaller network, or more data.

Step 4: (d) underfitting. The model is too simple or too constrained for the task, or training is too slow to reach a good solution. Fixes: a wider or deeper network, less regularisation (smaller $\lambda$ or dropout rate), more epochs, or a better-tuned learning rate.
